Principles of Antihypertensive Therapy

  • Goal: reduce BP to decrease CV morbidity and mortality (effective control ↓ incidence of MI, stroke, heart failure and CKD progression).
  • Target: <130/80 mmHg. Less aggressive targets in frail or symptomatic older adults; stricter control for diabetes, CKD or ASCVD.
  • Non-pharmacologic therapy: sodium restriction <1.5–2.3 g/day · aerobic exercise ~30 min most days (↓ SBP 5–8 mmHg) · weight loss ~1 mmHg per kg · DASH diet (fruits, vegetables, whole grains, low-fat dairy) · limit alcohol; avoid smoking.

Initial therapy & key rules

Initial therapy: thiazide/thiazide-like diuretic, ACE inhibitor/ARB, or CCB for most patients; select therapy according to comorbidities and patient-specific factors.

Combination therapy: commonly required; combine agents with complementary mechanisms rather than simply maximizing one drug.

Avoid harmful combinations: do not combine ACE inhibitor + ARB/direct renin inhibitor; use caution with β-blocker + non-DHP CCB because of bradycardia/AV block.

CKD with albuminuria: ACE inhibitor or ARB provides renal protection; monitor serum creatinine and K⁺.

Resistant hypertension: confirm adherence, interfering drugs, and secondary causes; spironolactone is the preferred fourth-line agent.

Pregnancy: labetalol or nifedipine ER are preferred; methyldopa is an alternative. Avoid ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and direct renin inhibitors.

Hypertensive emergency: defined by acute target-organ injury, not BP alone → use a titratable IV agent with controlled BP reduction; select the agent according to the clinical condition.

Key points

Loop diuretics: preferred for rapid removal of excess volume; inhibit NKCC2 → marked natriuresis with K⁺, Ca²⁺, and Mg²⁺ loss → monitor for volume depletion and electrolyte abnormalities.

Thiazides: useful for hypertension and calcium nephrolithiasis; inhibit NCC → moderate natriuresis but increase Ca²⁺ reabsorption.

Loop + thiazide: sequential nephron blockade can improve diuresis when loop response is inadequate, but increases the risk of volume and electrolyte depletion.

Potassium-sparing diuretics: reduce K⁺ loss; ENaC inhibitors are often used as adjuncts, while MR antagonists also have important disease-specific roles → monitor for hyperkalemia.

Acetazolamide and mannitol have specialized roles: acetazolamide promotes HCO₃⁻ loss → metabolic acidosis; mannitol shifts water out of cells and can reduce intracranial/intraocular pressure.

ADH drugs primarily alter water balance: desmopressin increases water reabsorption and can cause hyponatremia; vaptans increase free-water excretion and may be used for dilutional hyponatremia.

1. Choosing the agent

  • ACEIs/ARBs: important RAAS blockers for HTN, CKD with albuminuria, HFrEF, and selected post-MI patients with LV dysfunction
  • ACEI-induced cough: switch to an ARB
  • Sacubitril/valsartan: ARB + neprilysin inhibition; key therapy for HFrEF
  • MRAs: block aldosterone; important in HFrEF, resistant HTN, and primary aldosteronism

2. Safety & monitoring

  • ACEI: ↑ bradykinin → cough + angioedema
  • ACEI/ARB/ARNI/MRA: monitor K⁺ + renal function
  • ACEI/ARB: efferent arteriolar dilation → ↓ intraglomerular pressure → ↓ proteinuria + possible initial ↑ creatinine
  • Monitor K⁺ + creatinine after initiation or dose increase
  • Avoid dual RAAS blockade with ACEI + ARB or inappropriate addition of aliskiren
  • ACEI/ARB/ARNI/aliskiren: contraindicated in pregnancy
  • Sacubitril/valsartan: wait 36 hours after stopping an ACEI
  • Bilateral renal artery stenosis or significant volume depletion: ↑ risk of AKI
