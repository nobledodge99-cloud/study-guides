A1. Part A: Slowing Progression: Glomerular Hemodynamics and Hyperfiltration

Know the physiology and the drug mechanisms become intuitive. Glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure is the major force for filtration and is set by the afferent (inflow) and efferent (outflow) arterioles.

Arteriole changeRenal blood flowIntraglomerular pressureGFR
Afferent dilation↑↑↑
Afferent constriction↓↓↓
Efferent constriction (moderate)↓↑Maintained/↑ (marked constriction eventually ↓ GFR)
Efferent dilation—↓Initial ↓

Afferent tone controls blood entering; efferent tone controls pressure held within the glomerulus.

RAAS in the glomerulus

  • Low perfusion or low NaCl at the macula densa → renin → angiotensin II, which preferentially constricts the efferent arteriole to preserve intraglomerular pressure and GFR.
  • Angiotensin II also stimulates aldosterone; aldosterone binds the mineralocorticoid receptor (MR) and increases ENaC and Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase → ↑ Na⁺ reabsorption, ↑ K⁺ secretion.
  • Short term this preserves GFR; chronic overactivity causes persistent intraglomerular hypertension, and excess MR signaling promotes inflammation and fibrosis.

Hyperfiltration cycle

With fewer nephrons, survivors raise single-nephron GFR, flow and pressure. Persistent pressure stresses the capillary wall and podocytes → podocyte injury, ↑ permeability, albuminuria, glomerulosclerosis, further nephron loss → more hyperfiltration (self-perpetuating).

Albuminuria categoryUrine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR)
A1<30 mg/g (normal to mildly increased)
A230–300 mg/g (moderately increased)
A3>300 mg/g (severely increased)

Albuminuria marks glomerular damage and predicts CKD progression, kidney failure and cardiovascular events, even with preserved eGFR. Therapies that lower intraglomerular pressure usually reduce albuminuria, and lower albuminuria means lower progression risk.

Tubuloglomerular feedback (TGF)

The macula densa senses NaCl delivery: high NaCl → afferent constriction (↓ GFR); low NaCl → afferent dilation, ↑ renin, efferent constriction. In diabetes, more filtered glucose → more proximal Na⁺/glucose reabsorption via SGLT2 → less NaCl at the macula densa → relative afferent dilation + RAAS → ↑ intraglomerular pressure and hyperfiltration.

Physiology→pharmacology linkDrugs that lower intraglomerular pressure can cause an early dip in GFR / rise in creatinine. A modest early change is the intended hemodynamic effect; the question is whether it is expected and stable, or large/progressive (volume depletion, hypotension, AKI).

NSAIDs constrict the afferent arteriole, lowering glomerular pressure further, so adding them to an ACEi/ARB (especially with volume depletion or diuretics) raises AKI risk.

A3. Choosing Kidney-Protective Therapy

Choice rests on albuminuria, diabetes status, eGFR, potassium and comorbidities (heart failure, hypertension). The drugs are complementary.

TherapyRecommended / suggested when
ACE inhibitor or ARB CORERecommended: A3 without diabetes; A2 or A3 with diabetes. Suggested: A2 without diabetes. A1: still used for another indication (hypertension, heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, HFrEF). Titrate to the highest tolerated dose (trial benefit used maximal dosing). Never combine an ACEi with an ARB or direct renin inhibitor (more harm, no kidney benefit)
SGLT2 inhibitor CORERecommended: type 2 diabetes (T2D) + CKD + eGFR ≥20; or CKD with eGFR ≥20 and UACR ≥200 mg/g regardless of diabetes; or heart failure regardless of albuminuria. Suggested: eGFR 20–45 with UACR <200. May continue after eGFR falls <20 if tolerated; usually stopped when dialysis starts
Finerenone CORET2D, eGFR >25, K⁺ in range, persistent UACR >30 mg/g despite maximum tolerated ACEi/ARB. Can be added to ACEi/ARB and an SGLT2 inhibitor. Guidelines apply only to T2D CKD; role in non-diabetic CKD not established

B1. Part B: CKD Complications: Hyperkalemia Physiology

K⁺ is mostly intracellular; kidneys control long-term balance. K⁺ is reabsorbed in the proximal tubule and thick ascending limb and secreted in principal cells of the late distal tubule and cortical collecting duct via basolateral Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase then apical ROMK and BK channels; Na⁺ entry through ENaC makes the lumen negative, favoring K⁺ secretion. Secretion rises with ↑ plasma K⁺, ↑ aldosterone, ↑ distal Na⁺ delivery and flow. High K⁺ directly stimulates aldosterone (negative feedback).

Why CKD predisposes: fewer nephrons; surviving nephrons compensate, so hyperkalemia often needs extra contributors: RAAS blockade, MR antagonists, low distal Na⁺ delivery/flow, AKI, metabolic acidosis, increased K⁺ load/release.

Membrane effect: higher extracellular K⁺ depolarizes cells; severe or sustained depolarization inactivates Na⁺ channels → weakness, conduction abnormalities, ventricular arrhythmias, cardiac arrest.

Monitoring and chronic management

  • Recheck K⁺ (rebound), glucose after insulin, continue cardiac monitoring.
  • Acute causes: AKI, transcellular shift (acidosis, insulin deficiency), large K⁺ load, tissue destruction. Chronic: CKD plus RAAS inhibitors, low aldosterone activity, acidosis, low distal Na⁺ delivery. The rate of rise and context matter, not just the number.
  • Chronic plan: fix contributors (RAAS inhibitors/MRAs, K⁺ supplements, NSAIDs, trimethoprim [ENaC-blocking], acidosis, uncontrolled diabetes, volume depletion, constipation, diet); add a loop diuretic if residual function and volume overload; consider patiromer or SZC when hyperkalemia limits protective therapy.
  • Hyperkalemia should not automatically stop ACEi/ARBs (KDIGO): lower K⁺ first; reduce or stop only if uncontrolled despite treatment.

B3. Anemia of CKD: Physiology

  • Iron path: enterocyte absorption → ferroportin export → plasma transferrin → marrow; stored as ferritin (liver, macrophages).
  • EPO: made by renal interstitial cells in response to hypoxia (HIF stabilization → EPO gene transcription); binds EPO receptors on erythroid progenitors (survival, proliferation, differentiation). In CKD, damaged kidney makes too little → hypoproliferative anemia.
  • Absolute iron deficiency: low stores (blood loss, phlebotomy, hemodialysis losses, poor intake or absorption). Functional iron restriction: stores adequate but unavailable because inflammation and reduced renal clearance raise hepcidin, which inhibits ferroportin (↓ absorption and release from macrophages).
  • Multifactorial: low EPO, iron problems, inflammation, shortened RBC survival, blood loss. Check coexisting B12/folate deficiency, hemolysis, marrow disorders (e.g., myelodysplasia).

B5. Anemia of CKD: Clinical Use

Evaluate first: CBC, reticulocytes, ferritin (stores), transferrin saturation (TSAT) (circulating iron); look for B12/folate deficiency, blood loss, inflammation, hemolysis, thyroid/hematologic disease. Ferritin <45 ng/mL: consider a bleeding source.

SettingStart iron when
CKD not on dialysis, or peritoneal dialysisFerritin <100 and TSAT <40%, or ferritin 100–<300 and TSAT <25%
HemodialysisFerritin ≤500 and TSAT ≤30%

Oral vs IV: not on hemodialysis: either (individualize), switch to IV if oral fails or is not tolerated; hemodialysis: IV preferred. Hold routine iron if ferritin >700 or TSAT ≥40%; consider pausing during systemic infection.

Monitoring iron: Hb, ferritin, TSAT about every 3 months (non-dialysis/PD) or every 1–3 months (hemodialysis).

ESA use

  • Correct iron deficiency and other reversible causes first.
  • Weigh benefit (symptoms, fewer transfusions) vs risk. Transfusion is immediate but repeated transfusion causes volume overload, iron accumulation and alloimmunization (complicates transplant).
  • Hemodialysis: consider starting when Hb ≤9–10 g/dL. Non-dialysis: no single threshold; individualize.
  • Do not maintain Hb ≥11.5 g/dL with ESAs; use the lowest effective dose. If Hb rises >1 g/dL in 2–4 weeks, reduce dose 25–50%.
  • ESA monitoring: Hb every 2–4 weeks after start/dose change; adjust no more often than every 4 weeks (except for a rapid rise); then at least every 3 months; also BP, iron status, thrombotic signs.
PrinciplesFix iron and reversible causes first; ESAs improve anemia, they do not normalize Hb; avoid rapid or excessive correction.

B6. CKD-Mineral and Bone Disorder: Physiology

  • Regulators: PTH, FGF23, calcitriol. Liver makes 25(OH)D (calcidiol, the status marker); kidney makes calcitriol; PTH increases, FGF23 decreases renal calcitriol production.
  • PTH: ↑ Ca²⁺, ↑ phosphate excretion, ↑ calcitriol. FGF23: ↑ phosphate excretion, ↓ calcitriol. Calcitriol: ↑ gut Ca²⁺ and phosphate absorption, ↓ PTH.
  • In CKD: phosphate retention → ↑ FGF23 and PTH (compensation fails → hyperphosphatemia); ↓ calcitriol → ↓ gut Ca²⁺ → low calcium and secondary hyperparathyroidism (calcitriol also normally suppresses PTH).
  • Serum calcium can be normal, low or high; high calcium is often treatment-related (calcium binders, active vitamin D).
  • Harm both ways: persistent high PTH → high-turnover bone disease, fractures; over-suppressed PTH → low-turnover (adynamic) bone disease; high Ca and phosphate → vascular and soft tissue calcification (arterial stiffening, cardiovascular risk).

Interpret phosphate, calcium, calcitriol and PTH together; choose therapy by the overall pattern.

B8. CKD-MBD: Clinical Pharmacology

  • PTH (G3a–G5, no dialysis): follow trends, not a single value; a modest rise may be adaptive. Look for modifiable contributors: hyperphosphatemia, high phosphate intake, hypocalcemia, vitamin D deficiency.
  • Phosphate: treat progressive or persistent hyperphosphatemia, not prophylactically when normal. Dietary restriction (processed-food additives are highly absorbable; plant phosphate less so) ± binders; limit calcium-based binder dose (esp. hypercalcemia or calcification); dialysis removes phosphate (longer/more frequent sessions can help).
  • Correct hypocalcemia and treat low 25(OH)D with cholecalciferol or ergocalciferol; this may reduce the stimulus for secondary hyperparathyroidism without PTH-lowering drugs.
  • Non-dialysis: do not routinely use active vitamin D for modest PTH elevation (no clear fracture, cardiovascular or mortality benefit, more hypercalcemia). Consider it in G4–G5 with severe, progressive secondary hyperparathyroidism despite correcting contributors.
  • Dialysis (G5D): KDIGO suggests PTH about 2–9× the assay upper limit: control, not normalization (too high → high-turnover disease; over-suppressed → adynamic bone). Options: calcimimetics, calcitriol/active vitamin D, combinations.
