2. Dehydration vs Volume Depletion

Compensatory responses to dehydration (water loss) vs extracellular fluid (ECF) volume depletion (slide 8).
Dehydration (hypertonicity)ECF volume depletion
What is lostPure water (total body water)Salt and water (extracellular fluid)
Serum Na⁺Rises (hypertonic)Variable, depending on relative water vs Na⁺ loss
Body response↑ osmoreceptor signal → thirst and ADH↓ BP and renal perfusion → ADH, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone (RAAS), norepinephrine; ↓ atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP)
Fluid of choiceFree water (e.g., 5% dextrose in water, D5W)Isotonic saline (0.9% NaCl)

The distinction matters because the fluids and rates differ. All sodium disorder evaluations start with volume status: wet, dry or normal.

3. Hypernatremia, Diabetes Insipidus and Polydipsia

Hypernatremia (dehydration)

With pure water loss, extracellular water leaves cells by osmosis, so cells shrink and serum osmolality rises. Treat with water (enterally or IV) but correct no more than 10 mEq/L per 24 h; rapid correction causes cerebral edema (and seizures). Free water deficit = total body water (TBW) × (Na⁺ − 140)/140, where TBW = weight (kg) × 0.6 (men) or 0.5 (women); in the elderly 0.5 (men) and 0.45 (women). Do not memorize it.

FeatureDetail
SymptomsUsually after Na⁺ ~158; >180 usually fatal. Lethargy, weakness, irritability from brain shrinkage → twitching, seizures, coma
Acute vs chronicChronic = persisting beyond the first 48 h; can lead to brain edema if corrected too fast. Acute hypernatremia can cause demyelinating lesions like central pontine myelinolysis

Diabetes insipidus (DI)

ADH = arginine vasopressin (AVP). Insipidus means “tasteless” (vs mellitus, sweet). The boards still say DI, but clinically the terms are changing.

Arginine vasopressin deficiency (central), resistance (nephrogenic) and primary polydipsia (slide 12).

Presentation: polydipsia, polyuria, possibly polyphagia; 24 h urine >3 L (up to 20 L); serum sodium high in DI. Polyuria is urine output >40–50 mL/kg/24 h.

4. Hyponatremia: Tonicity First, Then Volume, Then Urine

Hyponatremia = Na⁺ <135 mEq/L. Step 1: serum osmolality (measured or calculated). Then hypotonic cases split by volume status and urine studies.

Serum osmolalityTypeCauses
Normal (280–295)Isotonic = pseudohyponatremia (artifact from extra fat/protein)Hyperproteinemia (myeloma), hyperlipidemia (chylomicrons, triglycerides; rarely cholesterol)
High (>295)Hypertonic (extra osmoles pull water out; true shift)Hyperglycemia; mannitol, sorbitol, glycerol, maltose; radiocontrast; ethylene glycol, methanol
Low (<280)Hypotonic (true dilution: too much water)Needs volume status and urine Na⁺
HyperglycemiaIt is pseudohyponatremia and hypertonic: total body sodium is unchanged; osmotic pressure shifts water so serum Na⁺ is diluted (↓ ~2.4 per 100 mg/dL glucose). It can later cause hypernatremia through osmotic diuresis. slide vs talk She says sugars like mannitol/sorbitol reduce GI sodium absorption; the slide lists them simply as osmotically active solutes.

Assessing volume status

HypovolemicHypervolemic
HistoryDiuretics, poor oral intake, GI losses (vomiting, diarrhea), overt bleedingHeart, liver, kidney disease, low albumin
ExamLow JVP (<5 cm), no edema, dry axilla, dry under tongue, tachycardia, orthostasisPitting edema, crackles (bases), high JVP, ascites, pulmonary edema on chest X-ray
LabsBUN:creatinine ratio (slide: “1:10 or less”; the classic prerenal ratio is >20:1), UNa <20, FENa <1%, FEUrea <35%—

Caveats: urine Na⁺ and FENa can be falsely high with diuretics, vomiting or high water resorption, and falsely positive in contrast, pigment or severe glomerular/tubulointerstitial disease. FEUrea still works on diuretics. Use the same units for plasma and urine creatinine.

Hypotonic hyponatremia needs thiazide awareness: thiazides are the diuretic most notorious for hyponatremia; they act in the cortical distal tubule and spare medullary ADH-dependent water retention, while wasting Na⁺ and K⁺. Check a BMP after starting.

5. Hypotonic Hyponatremia by Volume Status

Hypotonic hyponatremia algorithm and treatment (slide 26).
Volume state (share)Urine Na⁺Causes
Hypovolemic (32%): extrarenal salt loss<10–20 mEq/LDehydration, vomiting, diarrhea, third spacing (burns, trauma)
Hypovolemic: renal salt loss>20Diuretics, ACE inhibitors, nephropathies, mineralocorticoid deficiency (Addison), cerebral salt wasting (BNP), partial obstruction, type IV RTA
Euvolemic (48%)>20 (variable)SIADH (35%; urine osm >200), post-op, psychogenic polydipsia (4%; urine osm <100), hypothyroidism, beer potomania (urine osm <100), idiosyncratic drug reactions (7%: thiazides, ACE inhibitors, NSAIDs, SSRIs), endurance exercise, ACTH deficiency (2%; ↓ cortisol = ↓ free water clearance), stress, HIV, idiopathic of the elderly
Hypervolemic (20%): edematous states<10 (nephrosis/heart/liver); >20 in renal failureCongestive heart failure, liver disease, nephrotic syndrome, advanced kidney disease (organ failure)

NSAIDs inhibit prostaglandins (↑ ADH effect); increased serotonin (SSRIs) ↑ ADH. Key concept: the kidney needs a minimum solute load to excrete each liter of urine; when water intake exceeds the solutes available (beer potomania, tea-and-toast), water is retained.

SIADH and other causes

ADH (hypothalamus) acts on the collecting duct to reabsorb water, ↑ urine osmolality and diluting Na⁺.

SIADH causesOther hyponatremia causes
Neurologic/psychiatric disorders; ectopic ADH from tumors; drugs; pulmonary disease (esp. pneumonia); postoperative; prolonged nausea; vasopressin or oxytocin administration; stressAdrenal insufficiency (low aldosterone and cortisol: orthostasis, hyponatremia, hypokalemia, mild metabolic acidosis); hypothyroidism; renal failure; reset osmostat (hard to treat with water restriction; treat underlying cause, e.g., malnutrition); primary polydipsia (psychogenic, dipsogenic)

Symptoms

Cerebral edema. Earliest: nausea and malaise; then headache, lethargy, obtundation; seizures, coma and respiratory arrest (Na⁺ <115–120, especially acute). Also dizziness, vomiting, gait disturbance, muscle cramps. Death or coma is possible near 120.

Treatment: go slowDo not raise Na⁺ more than 12 mEq/L per 24 h. Too-rapid correction causes central pontine myelinolysis (osmotic demyelination syndrome): non-inflammatory demyelination with damage to descending motor tracts → spastic tetraparesis, pseudobulbar paralysis, locked-in syndrome. Therapy by cause: hypovolemic → isotonic saline; SIADH, primary polydipsia, reset osmostat → fluid restriction; hypervolemic → diuresis plus fluid and sodium restriction; renal failure → fluid/sodium restriction, dialysis.

6. Potassium: Shifts and Disease Processes

Potassium is the “minor cation” and is tightly tied to acid–base status and insulin.

Promotes K⁺ uptake into cells (→ HYPOkalemia)Reduces K⁺ uptake (→ HYPERkalemia)
Alkalosis, β-agonists, insulinAcidosis, α-agonists, β-blockers
  • Metabolic alkalosis → hypokalemia (K⁺ into cells for H⁺); metabolic acidosis → hyperkalemia (K⁺ out of cells).
  • Hypokalemia → metabolic alkalosis (low K⁺ stimulates renal H⁺ secretion); hyperkalemia → metabolic acidosis (high K⁺ inhibits renal H⁺ secretion).
ProcessK⁺ effect
Increased cell turnover (rhabdomyolysis, overexertion)HYPERkalemia (cells release K⁺)
Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP/SMX)HYPERkalemia (interferes with K⁺ secretion)
Type 1 and 2 RTAHYPOkalemia (board favorite)
Loop and thiazide diuretics; penicillins (↑ K⁺ secretion); cisplatinHYPOkalemia (K⁺ wasting)
Liddle, Bartter, Gitelman syndromesHYPOkalemia

7. Hypokalemia

Most hypokalemia is iatrogenic (medications) or from GI losses; history and physical drive the workup. Mechanisms: decreased intake, shift into cells (insulin, alkalosis, trauma/adrenergic stimulation, periodic paralysis, barium intoxication), renal loss, or extrarenal loss.

CategoryExamples
Renal lossIncreased aldosterone (mineralocorticoid effect), increased flow to the distal nephron, low Mg²⁺, RTA (type I or II), diuretics (furosemide, thiazides), salt-losing nephropathy, Liddle syndrome
Extrarenal lossVomiting, diarrhea/laxative abuse, villous adenoma, Zollinger–Ellison syndrome
Key question: 24 h urinary K⁺ excretion<15 mmol/day (some guidelines 20): kidneys are conserving K⁺; assess acid–base and shifts/extrarenal loss. >15 mmol/day: renal wasting; assess K⁺ secretion with the transtubular K⁺ gradient (TTKG), which checks the aldosterone effect on the distal tubule. High (>4) = aldosterone effect present; low (<4) = absent.
SyndromeDefectFeatures
LiddleENaC gain of function (epithelial Na⁺ channel)Severe hypertension with hypokalemia and metabolic alkalosis; low aldosterone and renin (pseudohyperaldosteronism). Looks like Conn syndrome with low aldosterone
BartterSalt reabsorption defectHypokalemia; Ca²⁺ loss with osteopenia and nephrocalcinosis
GitelmanSalt reabsorption defectFamilial hypokalemia–hypomagnesemia with hypocalciuria; ↓ Na⁺, K⁺, Mg²⁺, dehydration

8. Hyperkalemia

Critical not to miss: the myocardium is exquisitely sensitive, so it causes ventricular fibrillation or asystole. Get an ECG.

  • Peaked T waves and short QT (first)
  • Progressive lengthening of PR and QRS
  • Loss of P waves, QRS widening into sine waves
  • V fib or asystole

Aldosterone

Aldosterone increases K⁺ secretion in the late distal tubule and cortical collecting duct, so anything that increases aldosterone lowers serum K⁺.

↑ Aldosterone (↓ K⁺)↓ aldosterone release or action (↑ K⁺)
Increased renin (volume depletion, renin-secreting tumor); bilateral adrenal hyperplasia; aldosterone-secreting adenoma; Cushing syndromeType 4 RTA; primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison); K⁺-sparing diuretics; ACE inhibitors and ARBs; NSAIDs; heparins (unfractionated and low molecular weight)

Treatment

StepAction
K⁺ >6.5Insulin + glucose (drives K⁺ into cells), β-agonists (albuterol), NaHCO₃ if acidotic; then a loop diuretic
K⁺ <6.5Loop diuretic and dietary restriction
RefractoryDialysis
Stop K⁺-raising drugsACE inhibitors/ARBs, β-blockers, K⁺-sparing diuretics, heparin, NSAIDs, TMP/SMX
Not on slides beyond sourceIV calcium first for ECG changes (stabilizes the myocardium); K⁺ binders

9. Magnesium

~70% of body Mg²⁺ is in bone; the remaining ~30% is ~70% free and ~30% bound (albumin, citrate, phosphate).

Hypomagnesemia

MechanismCauses
Decreased intake / absorptionMalnutrition, alcoholism, TPN, small bowel bypass, proton pump inhibitors, malabsorption
GI lossChronic diarrhea, laxative abuse
Renal lossDiuretics, hyperaldosteronism, hyperthyroidism, hyperparathyroidism, hypercalcemia, volume expansion, tubulointerstitial disease, drugs, transplant kidney
OtherDiabetes mellitus, hungry bone syndrome, respiratory alkalosis, pregnancy
Mg²⁺ and K⁺Low magnesium exacerbates potassium wasting (via distal K⁺ secretion and Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase). You cannot correct hypokalemia unless Mg²⁺ is repleted first. Low Mg²⁺ also disrupts calcium and phosphate.

Mg²⁺ and parathyroid hormone (PTH)

  • PTH rises with low serum Ca²⁺ (and mimics: mildly low Mg²⁺ stimulates PTH), driving bone resorption (osteoblast RANKL; osteoprotegerin is the decoy receptor), ↑ distal Ca²⁺ reabsorption, ↓ phosphate reabsorption, and ↑ 1,25-vitamin D production (↑ gut Ca²⁺). Mnemonic: osteoclasts are “iconoclasts” that destroy bone.
  • Very low Mg²⁺ paradoxically blocks PTH secretion (mimicking Ca²⁺-sensing receptor activation), producing hypocalcemia. PTH in turn regulates distal tubule Mg²⁺ reabsorption.

Fractional excretion of Mg²⁺ (FEMg)

Same formula as FENa. >0.5% = renal Mg²⁺ wasting; lower = inadequate intake or GI loss.

Hypermagnesemia

FeaturesCausesTreatment
Nausea/vomiting, neurologic impairment, hypotension, flushing, headache; loss of deep tendon reflexes in magnesium infusion for preeclampsia/eclampsiaExcessive intake, renal failure, lithium, hypothyroidism, milk-alkali syndrome, Addison diseaseStop the source; loop diuretic; glucose + insulin (shift into cells); calcium gluconate (antidote for severe effects)

10. Phosphorus, Fanconi Syndrome and Cobalt

Calcium and phosphorus are covered more fully in endocrine.

Hypophosphatemia

MechanismCauses (grouped by me beyond source)
Diminished supply / absorptionStarvation, malabsorption, TPN, small bowel (bariatric) bypass, oral antacids (bind phosphate in the gut), alcoholism, vitamin D deficiency
Intracellular shiftIV glucose, refeeding syndrome, hungry bone syndrome, metabolic or respiratory alkalosis, salicylate poisoning
Renal lossHyperparathyroidism, hypercalcemia, hypomagnesemia, phosphaturic drugs, Fanconi syndrome, heavy metals, myeloma, hyperthyroidism, congenital syndromes
Fanconi syndromeNot Fanconi anemia. Proximal tubule defect: poor reabsorption of phosphate, bicarbonate, and other solutes. Findings: acidosis (bicarbonate loss), dehydration, electrolyte imbalance, rickets, osteomalacia, growth failure. Inherited (usually children) or acquired (usually adults).

Symptoms: impaired diaphragmatic contraction (respiratory failure), confusion, anorexia, fatigue, muscle weakness, seizures, numbness; bone pain and fractures with chronic deficiency; tooth decay or late baby teeth and slow growth in children.

Hyperphosphatemia (>4.5 mg/dL)

Hyperphosphatemia approach and treatment (slide 55).
CategoryCauses
SpuriousParaproteinemia, hyperbilirubinemia, hyperlipidemia, liposomal amphotericin B, thrombocytosis, blood sample contaminated with heparin or tPA

Treatment: dietary restriction and phosphate binders. Rhabdomyolysis releases K⁺, Mg²⁺ and phosphate.

High cobalt

Emerging problem from metal-on-metal hip implants (cobalt, usually with chromium, leaches out). Toxicity: cardiomyopathy, depression, visual impairment (blindness), cognitive impairment, peripheral neuropathy, thyroid problems, hearing loss/tinnitus, rash. Suspect it in sensory loss after joint replacement.
