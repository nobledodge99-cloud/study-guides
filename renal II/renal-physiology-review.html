1. Body Fluids, Osmolarity & IV Fluids

  • Intake = output ≈ 2500 mL/day. The kidney handles imbalance by making dilute (excess water) or concentrated (excess solute) urine.
  • Thirst drivers: ↑plasma osm of 2–3%, angiotensin II, baroreceptor input (↓volume/pressure), dry mouth. Drinking inhibits it.
  • Water moves by osmosis (via aquaporins) to the compartment with more solute. At steady state ICF osm = ECF osm. ECF volume is set by its Na⁺ (and Cl⁻, HCO₃⁻) content.
  • Tonicity: hypertonic solution → cell shrinks (crenation); hypotonic → cell swells/lyses.

2. Renal Blood Flow & GFR

Measuring flow and filtration

  • PAH is filtered and secreted, so nearly all is cleared in one pass → RPF = CPAH. RBF = RPF / (1 − Hct)
  • Inulin = gold standard GFR marker (freely filtered, not reabsorbed or secreted, inert). Cx = Ux·V / Px.
  • Creatinine: freely filtered, slightly secreted → slightly overestimates GFR, but endogenous and convenient. GFR ↑ as plasma creatinine ↓ (inverse curve). GFR <60 (sustained) = CKD range.
  • FF = GFR / RPF (≈0.2).

Filtration barrier & Starling forces

GFR = Kf × [ (PGC − PBS) − (πGC − πBS) ]    (πBS ≈ 0)
  • Barrier = fenestrated endothelium + GBM + podocyte slit diaphragm.
  • Charge: GBM heparan sulfate is negative → cations filter more easily, anions less. (Loss of charge barrier → albuminuria, i.e. minimal change disease.)
  • Small solutes are filtered regardless of charge.
Fraction filtered vs radius — cations > neutral > anions

Why this matters in Renal IIPrerenal (low flow), intrinsic and postrenal (obstruction) AKI all reduce GFR by different Starling terms. Afferent tone depends on prostaglandins; efferent tone on Ang II. So NSAIDs (afferent constriction) and ACEi/ARB (efferent dilation) both lower GFR in a volume-depleted patient beyond source.

Autoregulation

  • RBF and GFR stay ~constant across a wide mean arterial pressure range (roughly 80–180 mmHg) by changing afferent/efferent diameter.
  • Two mechanisms: myogenic response (fast; afferent arteriole contracts when stretched) and tubuloglomerular feedback (slower; §8).
  • Vasoconstrictors: sympathetics/catecholamines, Ang II, (ADH). Vasodilators: ANP, prostaglandins, dopamine.

3. Transport Basics: Glucose & Urea

Filtered load = GFR × Px  |  Excretion = V × Ux  |  Reabsorption (or secretion) = Filtered − Excreted
  • Basolateral Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase drives nearly all apical reabsorption. Transcellular (apical → cell → basolateral) vs paracellular (leaky tight junctions, passive only).

Glucose

  • PCT: SGLT2 (early, most) and SGLT1 (late) apically; GLUT2 basolaterally. SGLT2 inhibitors beyond source block this → glucosuria.
  • Saturable: threshold (~200 mg/dL, glucose first appears in urine; carriers are heterogeneous) comes before Tmax (~350 mg/dL, all carriers saturated) — this "splay" is why.
  • Hyperglycemia → glucosuria → osmotic diuresis (polyuria). Mannitol works the same way.
Glucose titration: filtered, reabsorbed, excreted

Urea (passive)

  • ~50% reabsorbed in PCT by diffusion. Urea is secreted into the thin descending limb.
  • Cortical/outer medullary CD: ADH raises water permeability but not urea permeability → urea concentrates in the lumen.
  • Inner medullary CD: ADH ↑ urea transporter (UT-A1) → urea exits into the medulla = urea recycling → builds the medullary gradient.
corrected The PDF states "70% reabsorbed, leaving 40% excreted" — that doesn't add up. Don't memorize that number; know the mechanism.

4. Sodium & Diuretics

Na⁺ handling by segment
  • Order of reabsorption: PCT > TAL > DCT > CCD.
  • Early PCT: Na⁺ cotransported with glucose, amino acids, phosphate, lactate, citrate + NHE3 (Na⁺/H⁺ exchange; the H⁺ titrates filtered HCO₃⁻). Late PCT: mainly NaCl.
  • Ang II ↑ NHE3 in PCT → ↑Na⁺ (and HCO₃⁻) reabsorption.
  • Thin descending limb: water-permeable, no NaCl. Thin + thick ascending: NaCl-permeable, water-impermeable.

corrected The PDF says hyperosmolarity in diabetes causes hypokalemia. Mechanism given (K⁺ moves out of cells) produces hyperkalemia in serum. Uncontrolled diabetes = insulin lack + hyperosmolarity + acidosis, all shift K⁺ out, despite total-body K⁺ depletion from osmotic diuresis.

External balance (kidney)

  • Freely filtered. PCT ~70% (paracellular), TAL ~20% (NKCC2). Fine-tuning is done only in the DCT/CCD.
  • Principal cells secrete K⁺ via ROMK. Drivers: aldosterone, high plasma K⁺, ↑distal flow, ↑distal Na⁺ delivery, alkalosis. α-intercalated cells reabsorb K⁺ (H⁺/K⁺-ATPase) on a low-K diet.
  • Acidosis lowers K⁺ secretion (inhibits Na/K-ATPase); alkalosis raises it. Not a one-for-one H⁺/K⁺ exchange rule.

6. Water, ADH & Urine Concentration

Water handling

  • ~2/3 reabsorbed in PCT (isosmotic), ~5.5% descending limb, variable in late DCT/CD under ADH.
  • Water deprivation: ↑osm → anterior hypothalamic osmoreceptors → thirst + ADH from posterior pituitary → ↑water reabsorption → small concentrated urine. Water drinking: the reverse → large dilute urine.

ADH: three actions

  1. V2 receptor (Gαs → cAMP) on principal cells of late DCT/CD → insertion of AQP2 in apical membrane → ↑water permeability.
  2. ↑NKCC2 in the TAL → stronger single effect → bigger gradient.
  3. ↑urea permeability (UT-A1) in inner medullary CD only.

Countercurrent system

  • Multiplication (loop of Henle): the single effect = NaCl pumped out of the water-impermeable TAL (NKCC2). Continuous inflow of 300 mOsm fluid pushes the gradient deeper → ~1200 mOsm/L at the papilla.
  • Urea recycling (IMCD) adds to the inner-medullary gradient.
  • Vasa recta = hairpin capillaries doing countercurrent exchange; they preserve the gradient (they don't create it).
  • Gradient washout (↑medullary blood flow, loop diuretics, low urea) → dilute urine beyond source

8. BP, RAAS, Tubuloglomerular Feedback & Heart Failure

Long-term BP = ECF volume = tubular Na⁺ handling

  • ↑Na/water intake → ↑ECF → ↑blood volume → ↑venous return → ↑CO → ↑BP. The kidney corrects this by pressure natriuresis; renal perfusion is sensed by baroreceptors and the macula densa.
  • Low volume/pressure: sympathetics + low distal NaCl → renin (JG cells) → Ang I → (ACE) → Ang II. High volume: ANP/BNP from stretched atria/ventricles.

corrected The PDF's description of Ang II "low concentration → high blood volume" is garbled. The physiologic point: in hypovolemia, Ang II rises and preferentially constricts the efferent arteriole, preserving GFR; at very high levels the afferent also constricts and GFR falls.

Tubuloglomerular feedback (TGF)

  1. ↑GFR or ↑P → ↑NaCl delivered to the macula densa.
  2. Taken up by NKCC2 → ↑intracellular Ca²⁺ → ATP release → adenosine (A1 receptor).
  3. Afferent arteriole constricts → GFR falls back. Negative feedback; slower than the myogenic response.
  4. Reverse in low NaCl delivery: macula densa signals renin release and afferent dilation.
Tubuloglomerular feedback
Macula densa memory aid beyond sourceLoop diuretics block NKCC2 at the macula densa too → it falsely "sees" low NaCl → ↑renin and less TGF constriction.

Heart failure: the kidney's role

  • Low CO → sympathetics (seconds) → RAAS fluid retention (hours–days) → ↑mean systemic filling pressure → ↑venous return → ↑CO. RAAS turns off only when CO returns to ~5 L/min.
  • Compensated: CO returns to ~5 L/min, retention stops; cardiac reserve is reduced.
  • Decompensated: heart too weak to ever reach 5 L/min → RAAS keeps retaining → edema → worse failure.
  • Rx: diuretics, Na/water restriction, ACEi, inotropes (digitalis), transplant.
  • Digitalis inhibits Na/K-ATPase → ↑intracellular Na⁺ → ↓Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchange → ↑intracellular Ca²⁺ → ↑contractility.

10. Acid-Base Physiology

Goal for this unitRecognize a simple primary disorder, predict compensation, and explain what the kidney does: reclaim filtered HCO₃⁻ and make new HCO₃⁻ by excreting acid. Complex mixed disorders, RTA subtypes and delta-gap come with their own sessions.

Three numbers

MeasureNormalMeaning
pH7.35–7.45<7.35 acidemia; >7.45 alkalemia (the PDF uses 7.37/7.42 — use the standard range)
PaCO₂~40 mmHgRespiratory component
HCO₃⁻~24 mEq/LMetabolic component

Acidemia/alkalemia describe the pH; acidosis/alkalosis describe the process. Henderson-Hasselbalch: pH = pK + log[A⁻]/[HA]; HCO₃⁻/CO₂ has pK 6.1 and is the first-line ECF buffer (high [HCO₃⁻], volatile acid, close to ECF pH).

Five-step approach

  1. pH: acidemia, alkalemia, or normal?
  2. Which one explains it: HCO₃⁻ or PaCO₂?
  3. Predict compensation direction in the other component (compensation never overshoots).
  4. If metabolic acidosis: anion gap.
  5. Match to history and kidney function.

A fall in PaCO₂ in metabolic acidosis is expected compensation, not a second primary disorder. Compensation outside the expected range → mixed disorder. Winter's formula (if assigned): expected PaCO₂ = 1.5×HCO₃⁻ + 8 ± 2.

Buffers in the urine

  • Phosphate (HPO₄²⁻ → H₂PO₄⁻): fixed supply, because PCT reabsorbs ~80% and ~35–40 mEq/day is left to buffer. Not adjustable. Titratable acid.
  • Ammonium (NH₄⁺): the adaptable buffer. Liver sends glutamine (instead of making urea) → PCT glutaminase → NH₄⁺ secreted into lumen (via NHE3, substituting for H⁺, or NH₃ diffusion + trapping) → 1 new HCO₃⁻ per NH₄⁺. TAL reabsorbs NH₄⁺ (NKCC2 "uses" it as K⁺) → medullary interstitium → CD secretes it (ion trapping).
  • Acidosis ↑ glutaminase and PEPCK → NH₄⁺ excretion can rise from ~30–50 to ~500 mEq/day over days. This is the main way a chronic acid load is cleared and the main new-HCO₃⁻ source in chronic acidosis.
  • Urine pH alone doesn't measure acid excretion — buffers (esp. NH₄⁺) matter. Urine anion gap (Na + K − Cl) is a proxy for NH₄⁺: negative = high NH₄⁺ (diarrhea); positive/near zero = impaired NH₄⁺ (RTA). beyond source
Renal ammonia handling along the nephron

Clinical links

  • Diarrhea: HCO₃⁻ lost in stool → normal-gap metabolic acidosis; kidney ↑NH₄⁺ production and acid excretion; lungs hyperventilate.
  • Vomiting: gastric HCl loss → metabolic alkalosis; volume contraction + Cl⁻ depletion maintain it (↑NHE3, ↑RAAS, ↑H⁺ secretion, HCO₃⁻ reclaimed). Responds to NaCl.
  • Volume contraction, loop/thiazide diuretics, Cl⁻ depletion cause metabolic alkalosis (CA inhibitors and K⁺-sparing diuretics don't).
  • Failing kidney: can't make enough NH₄⁺ or excrete acid → metabolic acidosis (high gap in advanced CKD; normal gap in RTA).
  • K⁺ link: alkalemia → hypokalemia; acute acidemia → hyperkalemia (shifts + renal effects), though cause matters.

Practice: identify, then explain

1. Several days of diarrhea: pH 7.29, PaCO₂ 30, HCO₃⁻ 14, Na 140, Cl 114Metabolic acidosis, normal gap (140 − 128 = 12). Low PaCO₂ = expected compensation. (Winter: 1.5×14+8 = 29 ± 2, so 30 is appropriate.)
2. Persistent vomiting: pH 7.49, PaCO₂ 47, HCO₃⁻ 35Metabolic alkalosis; ↑PaCO₂ is compensatory hypoventilation.
3. Days of hypoventilation: pH 7.30, PaCO₂ 60, HCO₃⁻ 29Respiratory acidosis; raised HCO₃⁻ = buffering and renal compensation (↑acid excretion, ↑HCO₃⁻ generation). Adequacy depends on timing.
4. HCO₃⁻ 12, Na 138, Cl 102 — gap? Does it name the cause?Gap = 138 − 114 = 24 (high). It narrows the differential (lactate, ketones, uremia, toxin) but doesn't give the cause.
5. H⁺ secreted, combines with filtered HCO₃⁻, HCO₃⁻ returns to blood — reclamation or new HCO₃⁻?Reclamation of filtered HCO₃⁻ (no net acid excreted).
6. H⁺ excreted on phosphate, with a HCO₃⁻ returned to blood — how is that different?Net acid excretion with NEW bicarbonate, replacing base consumed buffering acid.

DiureticsCAI (PCT), loop (NKCC2), thiazide (NCC), amiloride/spironolactone (ENaC/aldosterone). Loops and thiazides → ↓K, metabolic alkalosis; K-sparing → ↑K. Loops waste Ca/Mg, thiazides save Ca.
Tubular syndromesBartter = NKCC2 (loop-like); Gitelman = NCC (thiazide-like); Liddle = ENaC gain (HTN, low renin/aldo).
AldosteronePrincipal cells of late DCT/CCD: ↑ENaC, ↑Na/K-ATPase, ↑ROMK K secretion; α-IC V-ATPase. Retain Na, lose K and H.
K⁺ shiftsIn: insulin, β2, alkalemia. Out: acidemia, hyperosmolarity, β-blockers, exercise, insulin lack.
ADHV2/Gs/AQP2 in late DCT/CD; ↑NKCC2; ↑UT-A1 in IMCD only.
ConcentrationTAL single effect + urea recycling = ~1200 mOsm; vasa recta preserve it.
Ca/PO₄/MgPTH ↑Ca reabsorption (DCT), ↓PO₄ reabsorption (PCT), ↑calcitriol. Mg mostly in TAL (loops waste it).
TGF↑NaCl at macula densa → NKCC2 → ATP/adenosine → afferent constricts → ↓GFR.
Ang IIEfferent > afferent constriction; ↑NHE3, aldosterone, ADH, thirst.
Heart failureRAAS retains fluid until CO = 5 L/min; fails to turn off in decompensated HF → edema.
BladderS2–S4 pelvic (void, M3), hypogastric (store; β3 relax detrusor, α1 close neck), pudendal (external sphincter, nicotinic).
HCO₃⁻ handlingReclaimed: PCT 80%, TAL 10–15%. New HCO₃⁻ = titratable acid + NH₄⁺; NH₄⁺ is the adaptable one (up to ~500 mEq/day).
Intercalated cellsα = H⁺ out (V-ATPase), β = HCO₃⁻ out (pendrin).
Anion gapHigh: lactate, ketones, renal failure, toxins. Normal: diarrhea, RTA.
Source corrections, all in one place
  • Hyperosmolarity/diabetes → hyperkalemia shift, not hypokalemia (PDF wrong).
  • Urea "70% reabsorbed, 40% excreted" is arithmetically inconsistent; UT-A1 in IMCD (ADH) is the point.
  • Aldosterone acts on the late DCT/CCD principal cells, not the ascending limb.
  • Ang II dose/volume wording is garbled; use hypovolemia → efferent constriction preserves GFR.
  • β3 relaxes the detrusor; α1 closes the neck/internal sphincter (PDF says β3 contracts base).
  • Acidemia/alkalemia thresholds: use 7.35 / 7.45.
