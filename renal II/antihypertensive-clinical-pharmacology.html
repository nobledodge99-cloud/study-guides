1. Blood Pressure Determinants and Dysregulation

Blood pressure (BP) = cardiac output (CO) × systemic vascular resistance (SVR). CO = heart rate (HR) × stroke volume; SVR is set mainly by arteriolar tone; both depend on blood volume, controlled by renal Na⁺/water handling under the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), natriuretic peptides and vasopressin.

Time scaleMechanism
Short term (neural)Baroreceptor reflexes adjust vascular tone, HR, contractility
Long term (renal)Pressure–natriuresis: relationship between arterial pressure and Na⁺ excretion
Local (endothelial)Nitric oxide (NO), prostacyclin, endothelin-1 fine-tune resistance to shear stress and metabolic demand

How hypertension develops

DysregulationResult
↑ sympathetic tone↑ HR and peripheral resistance
Inappropriate RAAS activationVasoconstriction and Na⁺ retention
Impaired pressure–natriuresisBlunted Na⁺ excretion at normal pressures
Endothelial dysfunction↓ vasodilator capacity

Together they raise the arterial pressure “set point”, driving vascular remodeling and target-organ injury. Therapy targets blood volume, CO, or SVR.

BP determinantDrug classes
Vascular tone (SVR / afterload)Calcium channel blockers (CCBs), α₁-blockers, direct vasodilators
Intravascular volumeDiuretics, RAAS inhibitors
Cardiac outputβ-blockers, non-dihydropyridine CCBs (and central sympatholytics, which also lower SVR)

2. Goals of Therapy and Non-Pharmacologic Treatment

Goal: lower BP to the level with the least cardiovascular morbidity and mortality; effective control ↓ myocardial infarction (MI), stroke, heart failure (HF) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) progression. Target <130/80 mmHg in most adults (ACC/AHA, JNC) if tolerated.

SettingTarget adjustment
Frail older adults / orthostatic symptomsMore conservative target
Diabetes, CKD, established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD)Stricter control
Lifestyle measureEffect
Sodium restriction <1.5–2.3 g/dayEnhances drug efficacy; may reduce need for combination therapy
Aerobic exercise ~30 min most days↓ systolic BP 5–8 mmHg
Weight loss~1 mmHg per kg lost
DASH diet (fruit, vegetables, whole grains, low-fat dairy)Lowers BP independent of weight loss
Limit alcohol; stop smokingImproves cardiovascular outcomes

Lifestyle is the cornerstone at every stage but may normalize BP only in some stage 1 patients; most need drugs. The goal is not just normal numbers but preserved end-organ function and lower cardiovascular risk.

4. Monotherapy vs Combination Therapy

MonotherapyCombination therapy
WhenStage 1 hypertension without major comorbidities or target-organ damageStage 2, or BP >20/10 mmHg above goal; most patients eventually
ApproachStart low dose, reassess over several weeks, titrate. Raising dose beyond mid-range gives little extra benefit and more toxicity. Choose by comorbidities, age, raceCombine complementary mechanisms (different determinants: tone, CO, volume) for additive or synergistic effect and fewer compensatory responses. Improves efficacy, adherence and tolerability vs dose escalation
RuleComorbidity first, BP profile secondAvoid overlapping mechanisms or toxicities

Recommended and to-avoid combinations

CombinationRationale / caution
Thiazide + ACE inhibitor / ARB recommendedThiazide ↓ plasma volume but stimulates renin; the RAAS inhibitor blocks this compensation → greater efficacy
CCB + ACE inhibitor / ARB recommendedBetter vasodilation; less peripheral edema (balances arterial and venous tone)
Thiazide + CCB recommendedAdditive BP lowering: volume control + vascular relaxation
ACE inhibitor + ARB (or aliskiren) avoidRedundant mechanism: no added benefit, ↑ renal dysfunction, hyperkalemia, hypotension
β-blocker + verapamil/diltiazem avoid / cautionAdditive bradycardia and AV block (overlapping toxicity)
High-dose thiazide + nonselective β-blocker avoid (diabetes/metabolic)Worsens insulin resistance, glucose and triglycerides; blunts hypoglycemia recognition
Direct vasodilator + β-blocker + diureticOffsets reflex tachycardia and fluid retention

Diuretic combinations

  • Loop + thiazide (sequential nephron blockade) for edema/volume states; K⁺-sparing agents added to prevent hypokalemia or counter secondary hyperaldosteronism (see Diuretics guide).

5. Patient-Specific Considerations

  • Response is unpredictable: ~two-thirds of patients respond meaningfully to a given agent. Age, race and comorbidities affect efficacy; genetic polymorphisms may alter pharmacokinetics (modest clinical impact).
  • Nonadherence is a major cause of poor control (patients often feel no symptoms). Simplify regimens, home BP monitoring, lifestyle changes.
  • Substances that raise BP: NSAIDs, decongestants, tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs), estrogen-containing contraceptives, excess caffeine, herbal supplements, cocaine and amphetamines.
  • Atypical or resistant hypertension → look for secondary causes; monitor BP, electrolytes and renal function.

6. Selection by Comorbidity

First-line for essential hypertension without compelling comorbidity: thiazide/thiazide-like diuretics, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, CCBs (effective, long-term cardiovascular protection, good tolerability). Thiazide efficacy persists after volume normalizes (vascular adaptation, not persistent diuresis). Red = preferred agent.

7. Hypertension in Pregnancy

Complicates 5–10% of pregnancies (maternal stroke, HF, placental abruption; fetal growth restriction). Goal: lower BP safely while maintaining uteroplacental perfusion.

TermDefinition
Gestational hypertensionNew-onset BP elevation after 20 weeks, no proteinuria
PreeclampsiaHypertension + proteinuria or other end-organ dysfunction
Eclampsia / HELLPSeizures / hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, low platelets
Severe rangeSystolic ≥160 or diastolic ≥110 mmHg → urgent treatment
Chronic / mild–moderate (target 120–160 / 80–105)Acute / severe (reduce below 160/110)
PreferredMethyldopa (modest effect, excellent fetal safety, slow onset) • labetalol (α + β, preserves placental perfusion; rare hepatotoxicity → monitor liver function) • nifedipine extended-release (gradual, predictable) • diuretics only for volume overload (thiazides may continue if used pre-pregnancy; loops acutely)IV hydralazine (rapid afterload reduction; reflex tachycardia, fluid retention) • IV labetalol (monitor bradycardia, hypotension) • oral immediate-release nifedipine (watch reflex tachycardia and abrupt perfusion changes) • oral hydralazine less suitable (slow onset)
Adjunct–Magnesium sulfate: seizure prophylaxis/treatment in severe preeclampsia/eclampsia; mild vasodilation but not a substitute for antihypertensives

Avoid in pregnancy

DrugReason
ACE inhibitors, ARBsTeratogenic (2nd/3rd trimester): fetal renal dysgenesis, oligohydramnios, malformations
AliskirenSame RAAS-based risk
Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (spironolactone, eplerenone)Antiandrogenic effects: feminization of a male fetus
NitroprussideFetal cyanide toxicity; only for life-threatening emergencies when safer drugs fail
Atenolol (and nonselective β-blockers such as propranolol)Atenolol: intrauterine growth restriction (esp. early exposure); propranolol: impaired placental perfusion, fetal bradycardia, hypoglycemia

Balance maternal safety, fetal well-being and timing of delivery; early delivery may be the definitive treatment for severe refractory disease.

9. Hypertensive Crisis: Urgency vs Emergency

Precipitants: poorly controlled chronic hypertension or abrupt discontinuation; sympathomimetics (cocaine, amphetamines, decongestants, monoamine oxidase inhibitors); Na⁺-retaining drugs (NSAIDs, glucocorticoids); withdrawal (clonidine, β-blockers, benzodiazepines, alcohol); secondary causes (pheochromocytoma, thyrotoxicosis, acute renal failure, cerebrovascular events).

UrgencyEmergency
DefinitionMarked elevation (typically >180/120 mmHg) without acute target-organ injurySevere hypertension with acute organ dysfunction (stroke/encephalopathy, myocardial ischemia, pulmonary edema, renal impairment)
SymptomsNonspecific: headache, dyspnea, anxiety, epistaxisNeurologic deficits, chest pain, pulmonary edema, renal impairment
GoalGradual ↓ mean arterial pressure (MAP) over 24–48 h slide vs talk↓ MAP by 20–25% in the first hour, then to <160/100 within 2–6 h as tolerated
RouteOral agentsIV agents: rapid onset, short duration, titratable
AvoidRapid lowering (hypoperfusion)Excessive/rapid lowering (cerebral, coronary, renal perfusion)
  • Defined by organ injury, not a BP number. The rate of rise matters: chronically hypertensive patients tolerate higher pressures than normotensive patients with a sudden surge.
  • Before treating: assess volume status and comorbidities (HF, CKD, coronary artery disease); avoid excessive afterload reduction (ischemia).

11. Guiding Principles and Summary Tables

Three guiding principles

PrincipleMeaning
1. Comorbidity first, BP profile secondComorbidity picks the drug class; BP stage sets intensity
2. Combine complementary mechanismse.g., thiazide + CCB (volume + vascular relaxation); ACEI/ARB + diuretic (neurohormonal + volume)
3. Avoid overlapping toxicity or redundancyACE inhibitor + ARB (↑ hyperkalemia/AKI, no outcome benefit); β-blocker + verapamil/diltiazem (bradycardia/AV block)

Take-home

  • Start with a thiazide/thiazide-like diuretic, ACE inhibitor/ARB, or CCB; pick by comorbidity.
  • Most patients need combinations: complementary mechanisms; never ACEI + ARB/aliskiren; caution β-blocker + non-DHP CCB.
  • CKD with albuminuria: ACEI/ARB (monitor creatinine, K⁺). Resistant: confirm adherence, then spironolactone fourth-line.
  • Pregnancy: labetalol or nifedipine ER, methyldopa alternative; avoid ACEI/ARB/aliskiren/MRA/nitroprusside.
  • Emergency = acute organ injury; use a titratable IV agent matched to the situation.
