1. Acute Kidney Injury: Definition and Why It Matters

  • Dr. Bell (OB/GYN physician): AKI is some of the sickest medicine you will see. When the kidney fails, other organ systems are not far behind. The goal is to stop further harm and preserve kidney function.
  • AKI is the newer term; it used to be called "acute kidney failure."

Kidney functions (his list)

  • Regulate water and electrolyte balance (and with it acid-base balance)
  • Remove metabolic waste
  • Remove drugs, drug metabolites and other bioactive substances

Definition

AKIAn acute decrease in kidney function that can be multifactorial in origin and involves complex pathophysiologic mechanisms. It is a clinical syndrome, not a single disease, and it is not a moment in time: it evolves, and the longer it persists the more damage it does. Prolonged AKI can lead to chronic kidney disease.

2. Diagnosis and Staging (KDIGO)

  • Staging was unified by the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) group about ten years ago (slide: KDIGO 2012). It uses two parameters: serum creatinine and urine output. Meeting either one puts the patient in that stage.
StageSerum creatinineUrine output
11.5–1.9 times baseline or ≥0.3 mg/dL increase<0.5 mL/kg/h for 6–12 hours
22.0–2.9 times baseline<0.5 mL/kg/h for ≥12 hours
33.0 times baseline or rise to ≥4.0 mg/dL or start of renal replacement therapy or, if under 18 years, eGFR <35 mL/min/1.73 m²<0.3 mL/kg/h for ≥24 hours or anuria for ≥12 hours
Limits of the system (his point)Both inputs are variable. Creatinine moves with hydration (a dehydrated patient can have a falsely high value). Urine output is hard to measure without a Foley catheter. Dr. Kemp's lecture does not stage AKI; it is clinical.

Additional risk factors (second reference, Hertzberg 2017): COPD, obesity, shock, hyperuricemia, cardiovascular disease. Also gout (his narration).

  • Dr. Bell disagrees with CHF being "nonmodifiable": you can treat heart failure and the kidney may improve. Likewise hypertension, anemia (transfusion), hyponatremia, and getting a patient off the ventilator are all fixable. For toxic drugs: stop the drug or adjust the dose. Sepsis is one of the major causes of AKI and is treatable.
  • Shock: the cardiovascular system shuts down and may not perfuse the kidney.

4. Etiology: Pre-renal, Intrinsic, Post-renal

  • Pre-renal: the kidney is not perfused (volume depletion, peripheral vasodilation, blood loss, dehydration, low cardiac output). Any intravascular depletion lowers glomerular filtration.
  • Intrinsic renal: injury to the glomeruli or tubules. The most common problem is acute tubular necrosis (see Dr. Kemp).
  • Post-renal: usually some obstructive process (prostate, bladder, mass pressing on the ureters).
  • A combination of pre-renal and post-renal can occur. Ideally you define the etiology, though it is sometimes hard.

5. Evaluation of AKI

Three arms. History and physical is the most important.

ArmContentValue
HistoryComplete and detailed, with specific attention to medications (toxic drugs)Foundation
PhysicalThorough exam: volume status (dehydrated? fluid overloaded?), cardiovascular statusFoundation. He stresses it is too often skipped, with the consultant left to examine the patient
LaboratoryCBC, complete metabolic profile (BUN, creatinine), urinalysis, creatinine clearanceNo magic test
ImagingUltrasound is limitedUseful only to find obstruction (post-renal) or suggest chronic kidney disease (kidney length <8 cm); does not help pre-renal or intrinsic disease
Compare with Dr. Kemp vs KempKemp's lab emphasis: casts (RBC = glomerular; WBC = pyelonephritis or interstitial nephritis; muddy brown and cellular = ATN) and biopsy for atypical cases. Bell's workup is broader and starts at the bedside.

6. Management of AKI

Treatment objectives

  1. Treat the underlying cause
  2. Limit damage and prevent further loss of function (do it quickly if you hope to return to baseline)
  3. Achieve normovolemia and hemodynamic stability
  4. Correct electrolyte imbalance
  5. Discontinue or adjust the dose of nephrotoxic drugs

Specific management

ToolDetails
Fluid resuscitationIsotonic crystalloids are best (e.g., lactated Ringer’s)
DiureticsOnly if the patient is fluid overloaded ("flogging a dead horse" otherwise)
Vasoactive drugsNorepinephrine shown to be best. Keep mean arterial pressure (MAP) >65 (ideal 65–75); kidney function suffers when MAP is below 65
Nephrotoxic drugsStop them, or adjust the dose if the drug is needed
Renal replacement therapyIf you cannot stabilize: hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis (transplant is for end-stage disease)
MAP formula (slide)MAP = diastolic + ⅓ (systolic − diastolic), or diastolic + ⅓ pulse pressure. Example: 120/60 → pulse pressure 60 → MAP = 60 + 20 = 80.

Algorithm

Slide: management of AKI. Confirm, history and exam, assess volume, labs and urine testing, stop nephrotoxins, determine cause, then treat by category.
CauseAction
Pre-renalIsotonic crystalloid; diuretics only if volume overloaded; consider vasopressors
Intrinsic renalConsider renal biopsy; nephrology consultation
Post-renalRenal ultrasound when obstruction suspected; relieve obstruction; urology consultation
AllTreat the cause, supportive care, monitor volume, acid-base and electrolytes; improvement or consider renal replacement therapy

Indications for nephrology consult

  • Response to supportive care is inadequate
  • No clear cause (may need biopsy)
  • Stage 3 or higher AKI
  • Preexisting stage 4 CKD (the AKI staging only goes to 3)
  • Any patient needing renal replacement therapy
  • Any kidney transplant patient

His rule: even if you can manage it, a complex, serious illness may be better handled by a nephrologist. "Just because you can do something does not mean you should."

Prevention and follow-up

  • Prevention: unclear what works; the best-proven intervention is to identify high-risk patients, follow them closely and correct problems before they deteriorate.
  • Follow-up: goal is return to baseline function in the shortest time (long recovery → higher ESRD risk). No standard guideline, but nephrology referral at 3 months has been recommended (some studies say it does not change outcomes).
  • Three take-homes: history and physical, complete medication history, treat the cause and stabilize.

7. Nephrolithiasis: Epidemiology, Risk, Pathogenesis, Types

  • Definition: a syndrome with solid crystalline masses in the urinary space of the kidney. Once seen as a urologic illness; now a chronic medical condition with genetic (family history), metabolic and environmental factors (hot, humid climates and dehydration).
  • Why it matters in AKI: stones are a post-renal cause of AKI.

Frequency

ItemValue
Lifetime risk1 in 11 individuals (about 10%)
TrendRising: 3.2% (1976–1980) to 8.8% (2014)
Sex ratio2:1 male:female (was 3:1; diabetes and obesity driving women)
AgeIncreases with age; highest in men over 80
Children and adolescentsReversal: more females than males, incidence rising faster than adults (diet, obesity)
Recurrence50% in 10 years, 75% in 20 years
Compare with Dr. Kemp vs KempKemp: by age 70, 11% of men and 5–6% of women. Bell: 1 in 11 overall, ratio now 2:1.

Recurrence risk factors

  • Personal history: 15% risk of a new stone in the first year
  • Family history: 2.5-fold increase
  • Male sex; diabetes, hypertension, gout, metabolic syndrome; obesity
  • Increased urine acidity (uric acid stones); low urine volume; infection

Pathogenesis

  • Stones develop from decreased urine volume and increased excretion of stone-forming components (supersaturation).
Slide: mechanisms. Inhibitors (citrate, uromodulin, magnesium, pyrophosphate, osteopontin) vs promoters (high substrate concentration, slow urinary flow, salting out, Randall plaques/plugs, bacterial products, cell injury), with urine pH, leading to supersaturation, nucleation, growth, aggregation, stone.
  • Randall plaques/plugs: a plaque (usually calcium) forms in the tubule and subtubular tissue, then ruptures through the epithelium, and stone material aggregates on it.
  • Citrate is one of the most important inhibitors (high urine citrate protects).

Stone types

TypeShareUrine pHNotes
Calcium oxalate70–75%AcidicMalabsorption (e.g., gastric bypass) raises risk
Calcium phosphate~10%AlkalineGrow faster and bigger

Compare with Dr. Kemp vs KempKemp (Robbins numbers): calcium oxalate/phosphate 80%, struvite 10%, uric acid 6–7%, cystine 1%. Bell splits calcium oxalate (70–75%) from phosphate (10%), gives uric acid 8–10% and cystine 1–2%, and says oxalate forms in acidic urine, a point Kemp could not confirm. Use whichever your question source uses; the ranking (calcium > uric acid or struvite > cystine) is the same.

8. Clinical Presentation, Evaluation and Differential

Presentation

  • Can be asymptomatic. A stone still in the kidney may cause no symptoms; pain starts as it moves into the ureter.
  • Colicky pain of variable intensity (renal colic) is the most common finding; hematuria is second (gross or microscopic).
  • Microscopic hematuria is found in about 95% on day 1, falling to about 65% by day 3–4.
  • Also dysuria, urgency, nausea, vomiting. A stone can also cause hydronephrosis, producing colic.

Evaluation

LaboratoryImaging
Complete metabolic profileNon-contrast CT (low radiation if available): gold standard
Serum uric acid, serum phosphateAbdominal radiograph (KUB): shows calcium stones; use to follow whether a stone has passed (strain urine to catch it)
Parathyroid hormone (affects calcium)Ultrasound: not very accurate (point-of-care ED ultrasound about 55% positive); test of choice in pregnancy (no radiation)
Urinalysis with microscopy (crystals, bacteria, blood)Intravenous pyelogram: dye in a vein, watch excretion; much higher radiation dose
Urine culture and sensitivity (stone can cause infection)MRI (less radiation; sometimes used in pregnancy); digital tomosynthesis (new, rarely used)
Key contrast with AKIIn AKI, imaging is not very important (history and physical lead). In stones, imaging is very important, and non-contrast CT is the gold standard.

Differential diagnosis (slide)

  • Bleeding into the kidney (clot can obstruct a ureter, mimicking a stone)
  • Pyelonephritis
  • Ectopic pregnancy, ovarian torsion, dysmenorrhea (his OB/GYN list)
  • Appendicitis
  • Aortic aneurysm (important)
  • Biliary colic (colicky, gallbladder lies near the kidney)
  • Mesenteric ischemia, costochondritis, lobar pneumonia
  • Hydronephrosis of pregnancy: the uterus rotates (dextrorotation) and lies on the right ureter, dilating the ureter and kidney and causing colic. This one is not in his references; he adds it himself.

9. Management of Kidney Stones

Slide: acute management of kidney stones. Uncontrolled pain, vomiting, fever or hydronephrosis → consider admission; fever, AKI or anuria → urgent urology; otherwise manage by stone size.
Stone sizeChance of passingManagement
<5 mm>60%Supportive care, hydration, strain urine
5–10 mm~50%As above plus medical expulsive therapy (alpha-blockers, more effective for distal stones)
>10 mm~25%Urology evaluation; almost always will not pass

Medical management

  • Pain control: NSAIDs work as well as opioids and avoid opioid-abuse risk, so use them if possible.
  • Strain the urine (to catch the stone for analysis).
  • Medical expulsive therapy: alpha blockers (tamsulosin) or calcium channel blockers (nifedipine). beyond source They relax ureteral smooth muscle.
  • Long term: dietary modification (hard to sustain; the hallmark of long-term care) and a 24-hour urine to assess crystals and kidney function.
AKI tie-inNSAIDs are good for stone pain, but they are also on the ATN drug list in his AKI lecture. If there is AKI or obstruction with AKI, think twice about continuing them. beyond source

Surgical management

ProcedureNotes
Extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL)Treatment of choice for stones 10–25 mm; patient in a water bath; can be repeated without added risk
Cystoscopy with stent placementDrains the kidney with hydronephrosis
Ureteroscopy with laser lithotripsyScope up the ureter, laser breaks the stone; specialized
Percutaneous nephrostomyTube through the back into the kidney to drain it; local anesthetic, bedside; useful in pregnancy where lithotripsy and ureteroscopy are poor options
Robotic-assisted laparoscopic pyelolithotomyNot done often

Hospital admission

  • Inadequate pain relief with oral analgesics; cannot tolerate oral intake (needs hydration)
  • Obstructed pyelonephritis (stone blocks ureter with infection): needs a stent or drainage and IV antibiotics
  • Transplanted kidney with calculi
  • Calculi with pyelonephritis or sepsis
  • Intractable nausea and vomiting (cannot take fluids or medication)

Complications

  • Acute kidney injury (a post-renal cause; obstruction)
  • Chronic kidney disease if not treated adequately
  • Infection (a blocked ureter)
  • Recurrence
