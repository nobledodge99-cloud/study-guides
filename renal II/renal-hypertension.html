1. Approach to the Patient at Risk

  • History: prior cardiovascular disease (CVD); first-degree relatives with CVD; tobacco; then ROS, exam, labs; calculate 10- and 30-year CVD risk; discuss lifestyle.
  • Hypertension damages the brain, eye, heart and kidney, so the review of systems targets those organs.

2. Measuring and Classifying Blood Pressure (2025 AHA/ACC/ASH)

How to measure

  • Both arms at the first visit; use the higher arm. A difference >20 mm Hg needs investigation.
  • Standing BP at the first visit in the elderly, diabetics, anyone with syncope or risk of orthostatic hypotension (autonomic dysfunction, drugs, treated patients who feel lightheaded on standing). Measure at 1 and 3 minutes after standing.
  • Unattended automated office BP (several readings, patient alone) removes some white-coat effect and may be more accurate.
  • Technique: no caffeine, exercise or smoking for 30 min; empty bladder; validated cuff, correct size; arm supported at heart level; seated, back supported, legs uncrossed, feet on floor; no talking or phone; 2 or more readings, over a minute apart. Ask patients to bring their home cuff to compare.
  • Diagnosis uses an average of 2 or more readings on 2 or more occasions. Kinder notes that real practice is closer to "as the patient comes in" or takes it at home, which better reflects daily life.

3. Out-of-Office Measurement: Home, ABPM, Patterns

  • The 2025 guideline prefers out-of-office BP: home readings or 24-hour ambulatory BP monitoring (ABPM). These are more reproducible, track organ damage and CV events better, and find white-coat and masked hypertension.
  • Elevated or stage 1 office BP: confirm with home or ABPM before labeling the patient hypertensive.
  • Treated patient with high office BP: confirm adherence, then home monitoring or ABPM before changing drugs.
  • ABPM is the gold standard: cuff inflates at preset intervals, stores and timestamps BP and heart rate, patient does usual activities, worn at least 24 hours.

When to get ABPM

  • Suspected white-coat or masked hypertension; resistant hypertension; unreliable or variable readings; assess nocturnal hypertension.
  • Not needed when BP is extremely high or there is clear organ damage that requires prompt treatment (treat first).

5. Essential (Primary) Hypertension: Epidemiology and Determinants

  • ~95% of hypertension is primary; secondary is about 5%. Affects 1.4 billion adults worldwide; rising fastest in low- and middle-income countries; the leading cause of preventable death and the number one reason for ambulatory visits.
  • US prevalence of ≥140/90 is falling, but prevalence in the 130–139/80–89 range is rising.
  • Men > women until about age 65, then women overtake.
  • Age: systolic keeps rising; diastolic plateaus in the 6th decade and then falls. After 65, systolic accounts for most pressure-related risk. Isolated systolic hypertension is typical of older patients.
  • Geography: higher in Eastern/Central Asia, rural US, lower education; lowest in South Asia and with higher education. Nigeria has the lowest prevalence, Germany one of the highest: Black populations do not invariably have higher BP. In the US, Black Americans have more complications (stroke, HF); the southern dietary pattern is part of the explanation.

Behavioral determinants

  • Obesity (visceral fat): sympathetic activation, RAAS activation, cytokines, insulin resistance and hyperinsulinemia, compression of the renal medulla. These impair pressure natriuresis and raise tubular sodium reabsorption. Also intrarenal vasodilation and hyperfiltration (glomerular injury) and obstructive sleep apnea (repetitive arousals and sympathetic activation cause nocturnal hypertension). Weight loss is critical; 5–10% loss lowers BP 5–20 mm Hg (summary slide).
  • Sodium: hypertension is rare when intake is <50 mmol/day (about 1.15 g sodium); societies with very low intake show no age-related BP rise. In resistant hypertension, sodium restriction lowers BP about as much as the best added drug.
  • Tobacco: acutely raises BP 10–15 mm Hg per cigarette; the long-term relationship to hypertension is unclear (the lecture says smokers may have lower office BP; her updated slide says unclear), but smoking clearly raises total CVD risk. Heavy smokers may show masked hypertension. slide vs talk
  • Alcohol: moderate intake does not raise BP; heavy use (3 or more drinks/day) activates sympathetic response, especially in withdrawal. A common East Asian aldehyde dehydrogenase variant slightly raises BP in men.
  • Genetics: BP concordance is greater in relatives, identical > non-identical twins, and biological > adopted siblings, so genes matter more than shared environment. Many small-effect alleles; several Mendelian salt-handling traits cause secondary hypertension (Liddle, below).

Key messagePrimary hypertension usually causes no symptoms, and patients cannot feel their BP. "I feel fine" is not evidence of control. Most CV events occur in adults with BP below 140/90 (naturally or on treatment), so most events happen before hypertension is diagnosed; that is why risk calculators and early lifestyle changes matter. Systolic pressure is the stronger risk determinant in middle and older age.

When symptoms do occur they are non-specific: severe chest pain, altered mental status, headache, blurred vision, shortness of breath, decreased urine output, nocturia.

7. Hypertension-Mediated Organ Damage (HMOD)

  • Brain: stroke, encephalopathy. Eye: hypertensive retinopathy. Heart: HF (often with pulmonary edema), MI, unstable angina, aortic dissection. Kidney: AKI and CKD. Treatment aims to prevent these.
  • Framingham HMOD prevalence (2021): elevated carotid-femoral pulse-wave velocity most common; low ankle-brachial index least common; LVH, microalbuminuria/reduced kidney function, carotid intima-media thickness and abnormal brain imaging in between. Risk rises with each BP category above optimal; organ-injury risk climbs per 20/10 mm Hg above 115/75.

Heart

  • Persistent pressure load → increased cardiac demand → diastolic dysfunction and left ventricular hypertrophy (the main mechanism, not the only one).

Blood vessels

  • Smooth muscle growth, remodeling and hypertrophy narrow the lumen and raise resistance; microdamage lets cholesterol and cells deposit as atherosclerotic plaque. Result: coronary disease, large- and small-vessel stroke, peripheral arterial disease (claudication: exertional limb symptoms from limited perfusion).

Brain

  • Oxidative stress (more ROS, fewer antioxidants) weakens small vessels. Lipohyalinosis thickens the wall; endothelial damage causes wall thickening, ballooning and serum leak; microinfarcts = lacunar strokes ("small lakes"; liquefied on pathology), classically in the thalamus (her images); basal ganglia, internal capsule and pons are the other classic sites beyond source.
  • Hemorrhagic stroke: uncontrolled hypertension causes microaneurysms that rupture.
Slide: CT with circled lacunar infarcts near the thalamus and basal ganglia (images follow radiologic convention, so right on screen is left in the patient).
Slide: hemorrhagic stroke from uncontrolled hypertension, with surrounding edema.

Kidney

  • Narrowing of small renal arteries lowers filtration and may progress to CKD. Hypertension both causes and results from CKD, and is a major risk factor for albuminuria and CKD progression. Lower eGFR goes with resistant hypertension, masked hypertension and nocturnal hypertension.
  • Lowering BP benefits kidney function and albuminuria separately from cardiovascular benefit. Treat if ≥140/90 to a target <130/80 (<140/80 in elderly).
  • First line: ACE inhibitor or ARB (reduce albuminuria as well as BP); add CCB or diuretic (loop diuretic if eGFR <30). Monitor eGFR, uACR and electrolytes.

Eye: hypertensive retinopathy

  • The most common initial organ finding when hypertension is diagnosed; always do a fundus exam in new patients. Asymptomatic for years; diagnosed on exam; 17–84% of hypertensive patients (about 25% at onset, nearly all by the end of life).
  • More in Afro-Caribbean than European patients and in women; smoking is a strong cofactor; correlates with severity and duration, so retinopathy at first visit means hypertension has been present longer than suspected.
  • Findings: arteriolar narrowing, AV nicking, copper wiring, cotton-wool spots, flame/blot hemorrhages, exudates, microaneurysms, disc edema. Eventually vision loss. Try to find AV nicking and exudates first when learning the fundus exam.
Slide: fundus with cotton-wool spots, arteriovenous nicking, retinal hemorrhage and optic disc.

BP>180 systolic and/or >120 diastolic (or MAP >135)Same
Organ damageNone by symptoms or signsPresent: chest pain, severe headache with confusion/blurred vision, nausea and vomiting, severe anxiety, dyspnea, seizures, unresponsiveness (stroke, encephalopathy)
TreatmentNo rapid lowering needed. Restart or intensify oral drugs; follow up within about 2 weeksICU, IV agents, lower BP in a controlled way

First questions in any crisis

  1. Adherence? Ran out, cost, or stretching doses. Abrupt beta-blocker withdrawal causes hypertension with tachycardia.
  2. Sympathomimetics? Prescribed, OTC (cold and diet medications) or illicit (cocaine, amphetamines).
  3. Symptoms or signs of organ damage that need acute intervention?

Conditions to think of in emergencies: aortic dissection, severe preeclampsia/eclampsia, pheochromocytoma crisis, stroke, acute pulmonary edema, ACS.

10. Secondary Hypertension: When to Suspect It

  • An identifiable cause; treating it should fix or improve BP. Bimodal: 70–85% of hypertension in children under 12 is secondary, and ~17% in adults over 64.

Clues

  • Age <25 or >65 with new or sudden onset; diastolic hypertension starting after 65 (diastolic should be falling).
  • Drug-resistant: not controlled on 3 or more agents.
  • Bruits or hard-to-fix edema.
  • Unprovoked hypokalemia (not on a diuretic), or dramatic hypokalemia after a low-dose diuretic.
  • Initial severe hypertension (>160/120 on her slide) or an emergency with organ damage.

11. Renal and Vascular Causes

Renal mechanism: reduced sodium and water excretion plus RAAS activation raise sodium retention and SVR.

Renal artery stenosis (renovascular hypertension)

  • Atherosclerotic RAS: the most common renovascular cause in older (often male) patients. Reduced renal flow → RAAS → angiotensin II and aldosterone → volume and resistance rise.
  • Suspect: new hypertension with other atherosclerosis (MI, stroke), flash pulmonary edema, recurrent HF, ischemic nephropathy, refractory hypertension.
  • Exam: abdominal bruit in about 40%; specific for RAS. Labs: hypokalemia in ~50% (aldosterone, nonspecific); elevated creatinine.
  • Testing: renal artery duplex, CT or MR angiography, or invasive angiography. Beyond source: beyond source a sharp creatinine rise after starting an ACE inhibitor or ARB suggests bilateral RAS.
Slide: MR angiography with proximal renal artery stenosis (arrow).

Fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD)

  • Young to middle-aged women. Non-atherosclerotic, non-inflammatory smooth-muscle overgrowth. Renal and carotid arteries are most often affected; mesenteric involvement can cause abdominal bruits.
  • Imaging: "string of beads" (stenoses alternating with small aneurysms).

Chronic kidney disease

  • Cause and consequence of hypertension. Glomerular disease: more tubular sodium reabsorption expands volume and raises output (tubular resistance to natriuretic peptides and/or increased transport). Low perfusion: RAAS and sympathetic activation.
  • Silent until a CV event. Labs: low eGFR, BUN rising relative to creatinine, hematuria and proteinuria. Slide: CKD diagnosed with uACR >300 mg/g regardless of eGFR; ultrasound may show cysts.
  • Treat with ACE inhibitor or ARB (slow progression). Dialysis: tight BP control is not warranted and may be dangerous; inter-dialytic SBP <90 can cause symptoms.

Liddle syndrome (pseudohyperaldosteronism)

  • Increased ENaC activity in the distal nephron: sodium and water retention, potassium excretion → hypertension, hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis, with low renin and low aldosterone (<5 ng/dL). Rare, familial, any age, both sexes; may unmask in pregnancy. Treatment-resistant.
  • Treatment: amiloride (ENaC blocker, potassium-sparing) plus low-salt diet. Spironolactone does not work because aldosterone is not the driver. beyond source

Coarctation of the aorta

  • Narrowing of the aorta near the left subclavian (usually just distal to it) → outflow obstruction → LV overload, hypertrophy, heart failure. 0.4% of live births. Bicuspid aortic valve in 50–75%; also mitral abnormalities, PDA, ASD/VSD.
  • Exam: brachial-femoral delay, upper-extremity systolic hypertension, low or unobtainable leg BP, weaker leg pulses; systolic ejection murmur at the left upper sternal border and left subscapular area; continuous posterior murmur between the scapulae (intercostal collaterals).
  • Chest X-ray: inferior rib notching. Testing: MR angiography, TEE. Mild cases may present later with high demand.

12. Endocrine Causes

Primary aldosteronism (Conn syndrome)

  • Causes: aldosterone-producing adenoma (70%) or bilateral adrenal hyperplasia (25%).
  • Mechanism: aldosterone acts on mineralocorticoid receptors and ENaC in the distal nephron/collecting duct: sodium retention, kaliuresis, expanded volume, higher output and BP.
  • Suspect: new, severe or refractory hypertension with unexplained hypokalemia and suppressed renin; hypokalemia is present in <40%, or appears dramatically after a diuretic.
  • Labs: correct K to ≥3.5, then early-morning aldosterone and plasma renin activity; aldosterone/renin ratio >20. Pursue if aldosterone >10 ng/dL or renin low (25% of cases have aldosterone <10).
  • Imaging: adrenal CT. Adrenal vein sampling (interventional radiology) if surgery is planned. Unilateral adenoma and age <35 with unprovoked hypokalemia: laparoscopic adrenalectomy, no extensive workup needed. Most incidental adrenal adenomas do not secrete hormone.
  • Adrenalectomy cures hypertension in only ~50%, because many also have essential hypertension.
  • Medical: sodium <1500 mg/day; spironolactone or eplerenone; amiloride; thiazide-like diuretic (chlorthalidone, indapamide); add ACE/ARB, CCB or beta-blocker. Better results when renin rises above 1 µg/L.

Thyroid

  • Hyperthyroidism (more common): beta-receptors more sensitive to catecholamines → faster heart rate, higher output, systolic hypertension; tachycardia, anxiety, agitation.
  • Hypothyroidism: isolated diastolic hypertension (higher peripheral resistance, low output). One of the rare causes of isolated diastolic hypertension. Screen both with TSH.

Parathyroid

  • Rare. Fatigue, muscle weakness, bone pain and fractures, joint pain with calcium crystals, memory problems and confusion; hypertension from arterial stiffening. Labs: calcium, PTH, phosphorus, creatinine, vitamin D; urine calcium and creatinine.

Cushing syndrome (excess glucocorticoid)

  • Symptoms: fatigue, weight gain, weakness. Signs: central obesity, purple/pigmented abdominal striae, easy bruising, buffalo hump, moon facies, proximal weakness; women: amenorrhea; men: erectile dysfunction; cardiac hypertrophy.
  • Mechanism: cortisol acts like aldosterone (sodium and water retention), more angiotensin II receptors, more catecholamine effect on adrenergic receptors, less nitric oxide → higher SVR.
  • Causes: pituitary adenoma, adrenal adenoma, or exogenous glucocorticoids (prednisone, methylprednisolone give the same picture).
  • Diagnosis: late-night salivary cortisol or overnight dexamethasone suppression test; MRI brain for pituitary adenoma, adrenal CT for adrenal adenoma.
  • Treatment: pituitary surgery gives 78% remission, 13% relapse over 10 years; adrenal surgery controls cortisol quickly but can cause adrenal insufficiency (lifelong glucocorticoid replacement); medical: ketoconazole (rapid), mifepristone (glucocorticoid receptor antagonist; preferred when diabetes coexists).

Pheochromocytoma / paraganglioma

  • Rare catecholamine-secreting neuroendocrine tumor; 98% intra-abdominal (adrenal pheochromocytoma), 2% neck or thorax (paraganglioma). May be part of MEN. Test-favorite.
  • Clinical: difficult-to-control hypertension with paroxysmal spells averaging ~20 minutes: tachycardia, tremor, sweating, flushing, headache, agitation, pallor; often misdiagnosed as anxiety or panic disorder. Can also cause orthostatic hypotension (baroreceptor suppression, volume depletion, receptor downregulation).
  • Suspect with hyperadrenergic spells, resistant hypertension, MEN2, neurofibromatosis 1, von Hippel-Lindau, family history, a pressor response during anesthesia or surgery, onset before 20, or idiopathic dilated cardiomyopathy. 10% malignant; 40% familial.
  • Diagnosis: plasma free metanephrines (high false-positive rate; >3× upper limit is usually diagnostic); confirm with 24-hour urine fractionated metanephrines and catecholamines; then CT/MRI of the abdomen, and thorax/neck if abdomen is negative. Stop medications that falsely raise catecholamines before testing.
  • Treatment: surgical removal after α- and β-adrenergic blockade (case: 5-cm right adrenal mass removed laparoscopically). beyond source Alpha-blockade comes before beta-blockade, since unopposed alpha stimulation can precipitate a crisis.
Slide: contrast CT with a 5-cm right adrenal pheochromocytoma (arrow) in a 32-year-old with peripartum hypertension.

Congenital adrenal hyperplasia

  • Hypertension in infancy or childhood, with ambiguous genitalia (virilizing) or salt-wasting forms. Autosomal recessive; more common in Native Americans and Yupik Eskimos.
  • Hypertensive forms: 11β-hydroxylase and 17α-hydroxylase deficiency (excess mineralocorticoid). 21-hydroxylase is the classic form (salt-wasting: hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypoglycemia from low cortisol/aldosterone).

13. Sleep Apnea, Drugs and Neurogenic Causes

Obstructive sleep apnea

  • Common (25–50% of hypertensives). Sympathetic drive rises to stimulate breathing, raising cardiac output and peripheral resistance; nocturnal and non-dipping hypertension.
  • CPAP lowers BP only slightly; weight loss is best; most need medication too.
