A1. Sympathetic Overview and Adrenergic Receptors

The sympathetic nervous system (SNS) adjusts cardiac output and vascular resistance (stress, exercise, standing) through baroreflex pathways: β-receptors in the heart and juxtaglomerular (JG) cells of the kidney, α-receptors on vascular smooth muscle. Chronic overactivity drives hypertension, heart failure, ischemic heart disease and arrhythmias. Sympathoplegics (sympatholytics) lower sympathetic activity at two levels: central (↓ outflow: clonidine, methyldopa) or target organ (receptor blockade: β-blockers, α₁-blockers). Net effect: ↓ heart rate (HR), ↓ contractility, ↓ renin release, ↓ vascular resistance → ↓ blood pressure (BP).

  • All are G protein–coupled receptors; second messengers: cyclic AMP (cAMP) and inositol triphosphate/diacylglycerol (IP₃/DAG).
  • Cardioselective (β₁) blockers spare β₂ effects and are preferred in airway disease or diabetes; no β-blocker is absolutely selective, so high doses can block β₂.
  • Class advantages: β-blockers improve survival in heart failure, reduce post-myocardial infarction (MI) mortality and control arrhythmias; α₁-blockers (prazosin, doxazosin) treat hypertension plus BPH; central α₂ agonists help in resistant and pregnancy-related hypertension.

A4. α₁-Adrenergic Blockers

CORE Prazosin. class-level Doxazosin, terazosin (once-daily dosing: longer half-lives). Selective α₁ blockade, so no presynaptic α₂ block: no reflex NE release and minimal HR change (unlike nonselective α-blockers).

TopicContent
Mechanism / effectsCompetitively block postsynaptic α₁ on vascular smooth muscle → arteriolar and venous relaxation → ↓ systemic vascular resistance, ↓ BP, modest ↓ left ventricular afterload • effect greatest upright or when volume-depleted (depends on sympathetic tone) • ↓ LDL and triglycerides, slight ↑ HDL • relax bladder neck, prostate capsule and prostatic urethra → ↑ urinary outflow
UsesHypertension (usually in combination) and BPH (lower urinary tract symptoms) • less common: Raynaud, PTSD-related nightmares (prazosin), vasospastic disorders • not first-line for primary hypertension: inferior cardiovascular protection (esp. heart failure prevention). Best when hypertension and BPH coexist
PharmacokineticsOral, highly protein bound, hepatic metabolism. Prazosin half-life ~3 h, duration 7–10 h (longer in heart failure). Bedtime dosing minimizes orthostatic hypotension
Adverse effectsFirst-dose phenomenon: marked orthostatic hypotension and syncope after the first dose or dose increase (start low, bedtime, titrate slowly) • dizziness, fatigue, headache, nasal congestion, rare priapism, reflex tachycardia • intraoperative floppy iris syndrome (IFIS) in cataract surgery, even after stopping the drug → pre-operative eye evaluation
CautionsElderly; orthostatic hypotension, hypovolemia, hypokalemia, heart failure; stop if angina appears or worsens; severe heart failure or significant valvular disease (hypotension, ↓ cardiac output)
InteractionsAdditive hypotension with diuretics, ACE inhibitors, other vasodilators and phosphodiesterase-5 (PDE-5) inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil)
First-dose pearlStart low, at bedtime. Check BP and postural vitals at initiation and with each titration.

B1. Regulation of Vascular Tone and Site of Action

Vascular tone is a balance of vasoconstrictors (sympathetic α₁ receptors, the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system [RAAS], endothelin-1) and vasodilators (nitric oxide [NO], prostacyclin, local metabolic factors; they ↑ cyclic AMP/GMP and ↓ intracellular Ca²⁺ → smooth muscle relaxation). In hypertension, angina, heart failure and hypertensive emergencies the balance shifts toward vasoconstriction: ↑ afterload, ↓ tissue perfusion. Vasodilators ↓ systemic vascular resistance and arterial pressure and ↑ cardiac output and perfusion.

Site of dilationHemodynamic effectDrugs
Venous↓ preload → ↓ left ventricular (LV) wall stress and myocardial O₂ demandOrganic nitrates
Arteriolar↓ afterload (↓ systemic vascular resistance)Dihydropyridine (DHP) calcium channel blockers (CCBs), hydralazine, minoxidil, diazoxide, fenoldopam
Balanced (both)↓ preload and afterloadNitroprusside
Why it mattersThe site of action predicts the compensatory response: arteriolar dilators trigger baroreceptor-mediated reflex tachycardia, RAAS activation and Na⁺/water retention (hence combination with a beta-blocker and diuretic); rapid IV agents allow precise titration in emergencies.

B3. Calcium Channel Blockers: Mechanism and Dihydropyridines

All CCBs inhibit L-type voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels (α₁ subunit) in vascular smooth muscle and cardiac cells → ↓ intracellular Ca²⁺ → ↓ contraction. Vasodilation is arterial > venous. DHPs are vasoselective (bind channels in the inactivated state in arterial smooth muscle); non-DHPs are cardioselective.

Dihydropyridines

CORE Amlodipine, nifedipine. class-level Felodipine, isradipine, levamlodipine, nicardipine, clevidipine, nimodipine, nisoldipine.

B4. Non-Dihydropyridine CCBs and DHP Comparison

CORE Verapamil (more cardioselective: stronger AV node and contractility effects), diltiazem (balanced cardiac and vasodilator actions). Act on myocardium and conduction system: negative inotropic, chronotropic, dromotropic → ↓ heart rate (HR), contractility, AV conduction, O₂ demand.

TopicContent
UsesRate control in atrial fibrillation and supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) (IV forms for acute control) • stable angina • vasospastic angina (DHPs preferred) • hypertension only if arrhythmia or angina coexists • verapamil: off-label migraine prophylaxis
PharmacokineticsOral, highly protein bound, extensive first-pass (variable bioavailability); CYP3A4; nonlinear (dose-dependent) kinetics from enzyme saturation → disproportionate levels and prolonged half-life (verapamil ~4–7 h; diltiazem ~3–5 h, longer for controlled-release)
Adverse effectsBradycardia, atrioventricular (AV) block, heart failure exacerbation in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) • constipation (verapamil, up to 25%, dose-related) • dizziness, fatigue, nausea, mild liver enzyme rise • rare hypersensitivity, gingival hyperplasia
Contraindications2°/3° AV block (unless paced), severe bradycardia, HFrEF. Caution with beta-blockers (additive suppression of contractility/conduction)

B5. Organic Nitrates (Nitric Oxide Modulators)

CORE Nitroglycerin (NTG), isosorbide dinitrate (ISDN). class-level Isosorbide mononitrate (ISMN). NO → activates soluble guanylate cyclase → ↑ cyclic GMP → myosin light-chain dephosphorylation → smooth muscle relaxation.

TopicContent

B6. Sodium Nitroprusside (Direct NO Donor)

CORE Nitroprusside. Releases NO directly → ↑ cyclic GMP → relaxation. Balanced arterial and venous dilation (↓ afterload and preload): rapid ↓ systemic vascular resistance and BP, ↓ myocardial O₂ demand, ↑ cardiac output and perfusion. Effects fade within minutes of stopping, so it is precisely titratable.

TopicContent
UsesHypertensive emergencies; acute decompensated heart failure with pulmonary edema; controlled hypotension during surgery. Short-term, closely monitored infusion only
PharmacokineticsIV; onset within seconds. Metabolized to cyanide and thiocyanate (renally excreted): accumulate with prolonged infusion, high dose or renal impairment
Adverse effectsHypotension, reflex tachycardia, ↑ intracranial pressure • cyanide/thiocyanate toxicity: metabolic acidosis, altered mental status, seizures
Contraindications / cautionsSevere anemia; renal impairment if prolonged therapy anticipated; extreme caution with elevated intracranial pressure
InteractionsAdditive hypotension with other vasodilators; avoid with PDE-5 inhibitors
MonitoringContinuous BP and HR, fluid status, arterial oxygenation; cyanide/thiocyanate levels in prolonged infusions

Take-home

  • Venous dilation ↓ preload; arteriolar ↓ afterload; balanced (nitroprusside) ↓ both.
