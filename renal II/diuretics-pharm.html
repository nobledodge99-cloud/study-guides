1. Nephron Physiology: Where Each Transporter Sits

Three terms: a diuretic increases urine volume (usually by blocking electrolyte reabsorption); a natriuretic increases sodium (Na⁺) excretion, lowering total body Na⁺, extracellular fluid (ECF) volume and blood pressure (BP); an aquaretic increases free-water clearance with minimal Na⁺ loss. Site of action in the nephron decides the effect and the toxicity of every diuretic.

SegmentFraction / permeabilityKey transportersDiuretic that acts here
Proximal convoluted tubule (PCT)~65–70% of filtered Na⁺, Cl⁻, bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) and water; nearly all glucose and amino acids; isosmotic, highly water-permeable. Slide vs talkThe narration quotes TAL Na⁺ reabsorption as 25–30%; the slides and notes say ~20–25%. Use the slide value.
Slide: PCT cell. NHE3 secretes H⁺ for luminal Na⁺; CA (luminal and cytoplasmic) converts H₂CO₃ ↔ CO₂ + H₂O; HCO₃⁻ and Na⁺ exit through the basolateral cotransporter; SGLT2 takes up glucose with Na⁺. Red arrows = drug targets (CA inhibitors, SGLT2 inhibitors).
Slide: TAL cell. NKCC2 (loop diuretic target) brings in Na⁺/K⁺/2Cl⁻; K⁺ leaks back through ROMK, creating the lumen-positive potential that pulls Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ through the paracellular route.
Slide: DCT cell. NCC (thiazide target) takes in Na⁺/Cl⁻; Ca²⁺ enters via TRPV5 and leaves via the Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger and Ca²⁺-ATPase; PTH stimulates this.
Slide: collecting duct. Principal cell: ENaC (amiloride target) and the aldosterone receptor “R” (spironolactone target); intercalated cell secretes H⁺ and handles HCO₃⁻/Cl⁻.

Why this matters

  • Fraction reabsorbed predicts power: PCT 65–70% > TAL 20–25% > DCT 5–10% > collecting duct (few %). Loops are the strongest because the TAL moves a large fraction and the medullary gradient collapses; downstream segments cannot fully compensate.
  • Everything downstream compensates. Blocking Na⁺ upstream delivers more Na⁺ to the collecting duct, which raises K⁺ and H⁺ loss (hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis). This is the basis for sequential nephron blockade.

3. Carbonic Anhydrase (CA) Inhibitors

CORE Acetazolamide (oral, intravenous). Topical dorzolamide and brinzolamide for the eye. Site: PCT.

TopicContent
MechanismInhibit luminal and cytoplasmic CA → HCO₃⁻ cannot be reclaimed and intracellular H⁺ falls → less H⁺ to drive NHE3 → ↑ urinary Na⁺, HCO₃⁻ (and indirectly K⁺)
EffectsAlkaline urine; mild metabolic acidosis, which blunts the diuretic effect after 2–3 days (self-limiting, so weak diuretic)
UsesGlaucoma (↓ HCO₃⁻ formation in ciliary epithelium → ↓ aqueous humor; oral or topical) • acute mountain sickness (acidosis stimulates ventilation, counters high-altitude respiratory alkalosis) • metabolic alkalosis when volume expansion is undesirable (e.g., heart failure) • also on her summary slide: idiopathic intracranial hypertension, refractory seizures
PharmacokineticsWell absorbed orally; distributes to red blood cells and ocular tissue; excreted unchanged by kidney (half-life ~6–9 h). Topical: high eye levels, minimal systemic absorption, little acid–base disturbance
Adverse effectsMetabolic acidosis, hypokalemia (more distal Na⁺ delivery), paresthesias, fatigue, drowsiness • sulfonamide hypersensitivity (rash → Stevens–Johnson syndrome, bone marrow suppression) • urine alkalinization: calcium phosphate stones, acetazolamide crystalluria, ↓ uric acid solubility (gout)
AvoidCirrhosis (alkaline urine ↑ ammonia reabsorption → hepatic encephalopathy) • severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) / chronic respiratory acidosis (added metabolic acidosis worsens it) • hyponatremia, hypokalemia • sulfonamide allergy
InteractionsSalicylates, sodium bicarbonate (change urinary pH); K⁺-wasting diuretics (↑ hypokalemia)

4. Osmotic Diuretic: Mannitol

CORE Mannitol (intravenous only; not absorbed orally; stays in the ECF). Site: PCT and descending limb (some effect along the whole tubule).

TopicContent
MechanismFreely filtered, not reabsorbed → ↑ tubular fluid osmolarity → water retained in the lumen, limited passive solute reabsorption → ↑ flow, ↓ solute–tubule contact time → loss of Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Cl⁻, HCO₃⁻, phosphate
Other effects↑ plasma/ECF osmolarity → water leaves cells → less cell swelling (brain, eye) • transient ECF expansion → ↑ renal blood flow and glomerular filtration rate (GFR) → flushes myoglobin (rhabdomyolysis) and free hemoglobin (hemolysis)
Uses↑ intracranial pressure (trauma, neurosurgery) • acute glaucoma (intraocular pressure) • nephrotoxin excretion / possible acute kidney injury (AKI) prevention (limited evidence)
Adverse effectsEarly: ECF expansion with dilutional hyponatremia (can worsen heart failure, cause pulmonary edema) • later: free-water loss > Na⁺ loss → hypernatremia, dehydration, hyperosmolarity • hyperkalemia (intracellular K⁺ shifts out) • accumulates in renal insufficiency → fluid overload, AKI
ContraindicatedSevere renal impairment, anuria, dehydration, active pulmonary edema; rapid or excessive infusion risks cardiovascular and renal complications

5. Loop Diuretics (TAL)

CORE Furosemide (Lasix). class-level Bumetanide, torsemide, ethacrynic acid. The most efficacious class (“high-ceiling”): more diuresis than thiazides.

TopicContent
MechanismBlock NKCC2 in the TAL (they reach the lumen by active secretion via organic anion transporters) → ↓ Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻ reabsorption → ↓ medullary osmotic gradient → less water reabsorption downstream → marked natriuresis and diuresis. Loss of the lumen-positive potential → ↑ Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ excretion. Also ↑ prostaglandin E₂ (PGE₂) → effect blunted by NSAIDs; block at macula densa → ↑ renin
UsesAcute pulmonary edema, heart failure (HF) with volume overload, refractory edema (first line) • hypercalcemia (with IV saline) • toxic ingestion of reabsorbable anions (bromide, fluoride, iodide) • works in advanced renal impairment
PharmacokineticsOral or IV. Furosemide half-life 0.5–2 h (normal kidneys), up to ~9 h in end-stage renal disease (ESRD)
Adverse effectsHypovolemia, hyponatremia, hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis, hypomagnesemia, hyperuricemia, ototoxicity (ethacrynic acid, high-dose IV, aminoglycosides) • significant K⁺ loss → supplementation often needed
CautionsSulfonamide allergy (except ethacrynic acid), cirrhosis, risk of bone demineralization
InteractionsAminoglycosides: additive ototoxicity • hypokalemia potentiates digoxin toxicity • NSAIDs and probenecid reduce efficacy (NSAIDs: ↓ PGE₂; both compete for organic anion transporters)

6. Thiazide and Thiazide-Like Diuretics (DCT)

CORE Hydrochlorothiazide (HCTZ), chlorthalidone, metolazone. class-level Indapamide; chlorothiazide (the only parenteral agent). Chlorthalidone is long-acting (half-life ~47 h); chlorothiazide ~1.5 h beyond source.

TopicContent
MechanismBlock NCC in the DCT (secreted into the proximal tubule via organic anion transporters) → ↑ urinary Na⁺, Cl⁻, K⁺. Natriuresis is modest compared with loops
Calcium↑ Ca²⁺ reabsorption by two routes: (1) mild ECF contraction → ↑ proximal Na⁺/water/Ca²⁺ reabsorption; (2) lower intracellular Na⁺ in DCT cells → stimulates the basolateral Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger → Ca²⁺ enters from the lumen
BP effectShort term: ↓ plasma volume and cardiac output. Long term (after volume normalizes): ↓ peripheral vascular resistance, better endothelial function, mild sympathetic attenuation
UsesFirst-line hypertension (↓ stroke and HF risk; combines with ACE inhibitors or calcium channel blockers) • mild HF with preserved renal function • sequential nephron blockade with a loop • prevent calcium kidney stones • modest protection against osteoporotic fracture • nephrogenic diabetes insipidus
Adverse effectsHypokalemic metabolic alkalosis, hyponatremia, hyperuricemia/gout (compete with uric acid for secretion) • hyperglycemia, hyperlipidemia (benefits outweigh risks) • mild hypercalcemia • weakness, fatigue, paresthesias, sexual dysfunction • rare sulfonamide hypersensitivity • possible non-melanoma skin cancer beyond source
CautionsCirrhosis, advanced renal dysfunction (slide: effective in HF only if GFR > 30–40 mL/min), HF, diabetes
InteractionsNSAIDs blunt effect; probenecid reduces tubular secretion → ↓ efficacy

7. Potassium-Sparing Diuretics (Collecting Duct)

Both subclasses reduce Na⁺ entry through ENaC → blunt the lumen-negative potential → ↓ K⁺ and H⁺ secretion. Diuretic effect is modest; the value is K⁺ conservation and (for MR antagonists) cardiovascular benefit.

MR antagonistsENaC inhibitors
AgentsCORE Spironolactone • class-level eplerenone, finerenoneCORE Amiloride • class-level triamterene
MechanismBlock aldosterone binding to the MR → ↓ transcription/activity of ENaC and Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase. Effect is proportional to endogenous aldosterone (best in hyperaldosteronism)Directly block ENaC in principal cells
UsesAdjunct with loop/thiazide to prevent hypokalemia • heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) (mortality benefit, reduces remodeling and fibrosis, independent of diuresis) • hyperaldosteronism (drug of choice) • cirrhosis with ascites • refractory edema • finerenone: CKD with type 2 diabetes • spironolactone: acne, female-pattern hair lossAdjunct to prevent hypokalemia • Liddle syndrome (gain-of-function ENaC) • lithium-induced nephrogenic diabetes insipidus (lithium enters through ENaC)
Adverse effectsHyperkalemia • mild metabolic acidosis • spironolactone: gynecomastia, impotence, menstrual irregularities (androgen/progesterone receptor effects) beyond sourceHyperkalemia • mild metabolic acidosis • triamterene: poorly soluble, rare kidney stones
CautionsSevere renal impairment, hepatic dysfunction, baseline hyperkalemiaSame
InteractionsNSAIDs blunt effect • CYP3A4 inhibitors (erythromycin, fluconazole, grapefruit juice) raise eplerenone/finerenone levelsNSAIDs blunt effect
PharmacokineticsOral; spironolactone has active metabolites; dose adjust in renal/hepatic impairmentOral; amiloride excreted unchanged by the kidney
Hyperkalemia stackingRisk is highest with renal insufficiency or other K⁺-retaining drugs: ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), renin inhibitors, beta-blockers, NSAIDs, K⁺ supplements.

8. Other Agents: ADH Agonists, ADH Antagonists, SGLT2 Inhibitors

Antidiuretic hormone (ADH) agonists

TopicContent
AgentsCORE Desmopressin (DDAVP; long-acting synthetic analog) • class-level vasopressin (endogenous hormone)
MechanismStimulate V₂ receptors in the collecting duct → ↑ cAMP → aquaporin-2 insertion into the apical membrane → water reabsorption. Desmopressin preferred: longer duration, minimal vasopressor (V₁) effect
UsesCentral diabetes insipidus; nocturnal enuresis (sometimes)
Adverse effects / cautionsHyponatremia (esp. if fluid intake is not restricted), headache, dizziness, dry mouth • caution in HF and uncontrolled hypertension (water overload) • contraindicated in acute renal failure

ADH antagonists (aquaretics)

TopicContent
Agentsclass-level Tolvaptan, conivaptan
MechanismBlock V₂ receptors → no AQP2 insertion → free-water excretion without major electrolyte loss (aquaresis)
UsesEuvolemic or hypervolemic hyponatremia (e.g., syndrome of inappropriate ADH secretion, SIADH) when fluid restriction fails • tolvaptan slows cyst growth in autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD)
Adverse effectsOsmotic demyelination syndrome if sodium is corrected too fast • hepatotoxicity with prolonged use • thirst, polyuria, dry mouth, hypotension • use is duration-limited (narration: conivaptan ≤4 days, tolvaptan ≤30 days)

SGLT2 inhibitors (“gliflozins”)

TopicContent
Agentsclass-level Dapagliflozin, empagliflozin, others (canagliflozin)
MechanismBlock SGLT2 in the PCT → glucosuria (osmotic diuresis) + modest natriuresis
BenefitsDesigned for type 2 diabetes; also ↓ HF hospitalization, slower CKD progression, ↓ cardiovascular mortality; can complement loops/thiazides in diuretic resistance
Adverse effectsGenital and urinary tract infections, initial transient dip in GFR, dehydration. (Covered more in the endocrine course.)

9. Clinical Applications: Choose by Pathophysiology

Selection depends on (1) the underlying pathophysiology, (2) site and magnitude of action, (3) comorbidities and renal function.

ConditionApproach
Acute HF with volume overloadLoop = cornerstone. If response falls, add a thiazide/thiazide-like (sequential nephron blockade: hits distinct segments, overcomes compensatory Na⁺ reabsorption)
Chronic HFrEFMR antagonist (mild diuresis + mortality benefit); loop or thiazide as needed for euvolemia. Full regimen includes ACE inhibitor or angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor (ARNI), beta-blocker, SGLT2 inhibitor beyond source
Cirrhosis with ascitesAldosterone antagonist first (hyperaldosteronism drives retention); add a loop if ascites persists
Nephrotic syndromeLoop first (efficacy reduced by drug binding to urinary protein); loop + thiazide for resistant edema
HypertensionThiazide/thiazide-like first-line; loop if estimated GFR (eGFR) < 30 mL/min or resistant hypertension with volume overload; add K⁺-sparing to prevent hypokalemia or counter secondary hyperaldosteronism
Chronic kidney disease (CKD)Efficacy falls as renal function declines; refractory edema: loop + thiazide
HypercalcemiaLoop + IV saline (↑ Ca²⁺ excretion)
Recurrent Ca²⁺ stonesThiazide (↓ urinary calcium)
Primary aldosteronismMR antagonist (treatment of choice)
Nephrogenic diabetes insipidusThiazide: mild volume contraction → ↑ proximal reabsorption → ↓ urine output (paradoxical)
Central diabetes insipidusDesmopressin (replaces deficient ADH)
SIADHTolvaptan/conivaptan when fluid restriction is insufficient

Take-home principles

  • Site of action → effects and toxicity. Upstream block → K⁺ wasting; collecting-duct block → K⁺ retention.
  • Sulfonamide-like: acetazolamide, loops (not ethacrynic acid), thiazides.
  • NSAIDs and probenecid blunt loops and thiazides (organic anion transport; PGE₂ for loops).
  • Choose by pathophysiology, not just “edema”.
