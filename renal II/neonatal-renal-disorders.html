2. Embryology of the Kidney

StageKey points
PronephrosTransient, rudimentary, nonfunctioning; gone by the end of week 4. Its degeneration is required for normal kidney development
MesonephrosFrom intermediate mesoderm by day 26; by week 5 forms ~20 paired tubules that make a little urine. Contributes to the bladder; in males, the genital system comes from the mesonephric ducts and some tubules
MetanephrosFinal kidney: metanephric mesenchyme + ureteric bud epithelium. First seen at week 5, urine production by week 10
Pronephros, mesonephros and metanephros (5-, 7- and 9-week embryos). Source: UpToDate figure on her slide.
  • The metanephros begins in the pelvis opposite the sacral somites and ascends to the lumbar region by week 8. Failure of this ascent gives an ectopic (pelvic) kidney; fusion gives a horseshoe kidney.
  • Reciprocal induction between the metanephric mesenchyme and the ureteric epithelium forms the nephrons (mesenchyme) and the collecting system (ureteric bud). Signaling and transcription factors are not covered.
  • The bladder comes from a separate but continuous structure, the urogenital sinus.

3. CAKUT: Congenital Anomalies of the Kidney and Urinary Tract

Developmental problemResult
Malformation of developmentRenal agenesis or dysplasia
Environmental exposuresPrenatal teratogens
Abnormal embryonic migrationEctopic (pelvic) kidney; fusion (horseshoe)
Abnormal developing collecting systemDuplicated systems, posterior urethral valves, UPJ obstruction
  • Together with lower urinary tract abnormalities they form the CAKUT spectrum.
  • Occurs in 1–3 per 500 live births; accounts for 40–50% of chronic kidney disease in children (the narration says "up to 50%").

Prenatal USNo renal parenchyma seenParenchyma seen, but no renal function on testing

Incidence and outcome

UnilateralBilateral
Incidence1 in 1,000–3,000; male predominance; left > rightRare, 1 in 7,000–10,000; male predominance

Solitary kidney

  • The contralateral kidney hypertrophies and hyperfiltrates, which can injure it: hypertension, proteinuria, reduced GFR later in life.
  • Both true and functionally solitary kidneys need serial blood pressure checks, urinalysis and ultrasounds as the child grows.
Beyond source beyond sourceBilateral agenesis causes the Potter facies (flat face, low-set ears, limb deformities) because the fetus is compressed without amniotic fluid. Unilateral renal agenesis is often accompanied by other urinary anomalies, so imaging of the remaining kidney matters.

5. Renal Dysplasia and Multicystic Dysplastic Kidney

Renal dysplasia

  • Abnormal differentiation after normal initiation of development; believed to be arrest of the metanephric blastema in the first trimester. One of the most common causes of ESRD in children.
  • Unilateral > bilateral. Small kidneys (hypoplasia) with fewer nephrons plus dysplastic features ("hypodysplasia").
  • ~50% have other urinary tract anomalies: horseshoe kidney, ureteral duplication, hydroureter, UPJ and UVJ obstruction, VUR.
  • Diagnosed on prenatal ultrasound after the third month.
InvolvementPostnatal presentation
UnilateralNormal function (the other kidney compensates)
BilateralDepends on degree of impairment. Neonate: low birth weight, pneumothorax, feeding difficulty, metabolic acidosis, urinary sodium losses, high creatinine. First year: persistent anorexia, vomiting, failure to thrive

Multicystic dysplastic kidney (MCDK)

  • Most common cause of a congenital solitary kidney (unilateral MCDK).
  • 1 in 2,400–4,300; male > female; left > right.
  • Most involute without intervention (~60% by age 10) and have minimal function.
  • Diagnosed on prenatal ultrasound.
  • Serial postnatal renal-bladder ultrasounds watch for contralateral abnormalities; once involution occurs, they are not needed.
  • Malignant transformation (Wilms tumor, RCC) is rare.
  • Screen regularly for hypertension and proteinuria.
Prenatal US of a unilateral MCDK: large anechoic kidney with smaller cysts caudally. Source: UpToDate (Ozcan).

Source noteDr. Kemp's lecture (Renal II pathology) adds the pathology detail: ADPKD has berry aneurysms and a family history; ARPKD has congenital hepatic fibrosis with portal hypertension and cholangitis. The reasoning guide links ARPKD to the hepatobiliary system for the same reason.

7. Hydronephrosis

  • Dilation of the renal pelvis and calyces from urine retention and increased pressure. It is a finding, not a diagnosis.
  • 50–75% of neonatal cases are transient and resolve on their own.
  • 10–30% have another cause: UPJ obstruction, VUR. Less common: UVJ obstruction, MCDK, posterior urethral valves, and a ureterocele blocking ureteral drainage.
  • Get a postnatal renal-bladder ultrasound, typically 2–3 days after birth, with timing depending on antenatal severity.

Society for Fetal Urology (SFU) grading

GradeUltrasound
0No dilation
1Renal pelvis only
2Pelvis plus a few calyces
3Virtually all calyces
4Like grade 3, plus parenchymal thinning compared with the normal side
SFU grades 1–4 on ultrasound (UpToDate).

Higher grade = worse severity and more intervention.

8. Vesicoureteral Reflux (VUR) and VCUG

  • Most common pediatric urologic condition; associated with febrile UTIs.
  • 10–20% of infants with prenatal hydronephrosis have VUR.
  • Primary VUR: embryonic anatomic defect; spontaneously resolves as the intravesical tunnel at the UVJ lengthens with growth.
  • Secondary VUR: raised intravesical pressure pushes urine back past the UVJ: neurogenic bladder, posterior urethral valves, bowel/bladder dysfunction (constipation).
  • Diagnosis: VCUG.
  • Management: antibiotic prophylaxis or surgical correction, based on age, severity, renal scarring, and parental preference (some parents prefer surgery to prolonged antibiotics).
  • Complications: pyelonephritis, renal scarring, hypertension, CKD.
  • A child under 2 with a first febrile UTI gets an imaging work-up looking for VUR.
UPJ (pelvis-ureter, near the kidney), UVJ (ureter-bladder), PUV (posterior urethral valves, at the distal bladder outlet).
Transcript slip correctedThe narration says a "sterile UTI" under age two triggers the work-up. The slide and context mean a febrile UTI.

VCUG

  • Identifies bladder outlet obstruction and detects VUR.
  • Fluoroscopy (a small amount of radiation, well tolerated). A urinary catheter is placed, contrast is instilled, and images are taken while the bladder fills and during voiding.
  • Example: posterior urethral valves show a dilated posterior urethra, a distended bladder, and a dilated ureter with reflux.
VCUG with PUV: arrows = dilated urethra; arrowheads = dilated ureter with VUR (UpToDate).
Beyond source beyond sourcePosterior urethral valves occur in male infants and can cause bilateral hydronephrosis, oligohydramnios and Potter sequence.

9. Duplicated Collecting System

  • From abnormal embryonic origins (more than one ureteric bud).
  • Complete duplication: double ureter, each draining separately into the bladder.
  • Partial duplication: bifid ureter with a single drain into the bladder.
  • <1% of the population; female > male.
  • Complications: VUR, obstruction, ureteroceles.
  • Diagnosis: prenatal and postnatal ultrasound; VCUG to look for VUR.
  • Management varies with VUR grade, anatomy, and function.
Beyond source beyond sourceIn complete duplication the upper-pole ureter inserts inferior and medial and tends to obstruct (ureterocele), while the lower-pole ureter inserts lateral and superior and tends to reflux (the Weigert-Meyer rule).
Normal system (left) vs duplicated left collecting system (right).

10. Migration Anomalies: Pelvic and Horseshoe Kidney

  • Result from disrupted normal migration of the kidneys to their final position.
  • Usually asymptomatic, found incidentally on imaging.
  • Complications: UTI, obstruction, kidney stones.
  • Higher risk of other anomalies, e.g., VUR.
  • Horseshoe kidney: kidneys fused across the midline.
Beyond source beyond sourceHorseshoe kidneys fuse at the lower poles and are held low by the inferior mesenteric artery, which explains the obstruction and stones. They are associated with Turner syndrome (also covered in Dr. Kemp's lecture).
Unenhanced CT of a horseshoe kidney with bilateral calculi (UpToDate).
