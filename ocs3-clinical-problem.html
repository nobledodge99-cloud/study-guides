1. The Diagnostic Process

Why Diagnosis Comes First

Everything done for a new patient or new complaint — history, physical exam, labs, special studies, imaging — exists to answer one question: what is the diagnosis? Once you have the right diagnosis, treatment follows fairly directly (and if you don't know how to treat it, you look it up). But the efficacy and appropriateness of any treatment depends entirely on getting that initial diagnosis right.

OMS II builds on the comprehensive history and physical exam skills from first year, now applying them practically: arriving at a well-reasoned provisional/primary diagnosis plus a good supporting differential diagnosis.

Pearl: patients don't present as "diverticulitis" or "congestive heart failure" — they present with a chief complaint. The same chief complaint can lead down a cardiac, respiratory, vascular, GI, or other path entirely; you won't know which until you start gathering information.
Section 1.1

Q1. A third-year student orders a battery of tests on a patient with fatigue "just to see what comes back," without a working differential in mind. What is the most important principle this violates?

The entire diagnostic process — history, exam, and testing — exists to answer "what is the diagnosis?" Testing should be driven by a differential diagnosis (to rule in or rule out specific possibilities), not ordered reflexively without a clinical question in mind.

The General Approach

The overall flow: gather information (history, physical exam, labs, special studies, imaging) → form an assessment (differential diagnosis + problem list) → develop a plan (targeted diagnostic testing, then treatment) → document (notes and orders). Each step narrows and refines the one before it — you start broad and progressively focus.

Section 1.2

Q2. Which sequence best reflects the intended flow of the clinical reasoning process taught in this session?

The process starts broad (history and physical exam), narrows to an assessment (a prioritized differential diagnosis plus a problem list), develops a plan (testing to rule in/out the differential, then treatment), and is captured in documentation throughout.

2. History & Physical Examination

History Drives the Differential Diagnosis

The history is a strong starting point for everything a clinician does. Specifically, the chief complaint, HPI, and review of systems guide the initial construction of the differential diagnosis. Other elements — PMH, allergies, medications, social history, family history — are important contributory information, but by themselves they will not hand you the diagnosis.

Clinical Pearl — Repeat the Exam Dr. Wilde recounts a resident case: a child with joint pain and fever, extensive history taken, but the diagnosis wasn't clear. An attending asked, "What does the skin look like?" — prompting a repeat exam that revealed a bullseye rash on the trunk (missed on the first pass), leading to a diagnosis of Lyme disease. Lesson: when the history alone doesn't clarify things, go back and examine the patient more thoroughly — don't assume your first pass was complete.
Section 2.1

Q3. A resident obtains a detailed history on a febrile child with joint pain but remains uncertain of the diagnosis. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step, based on the teaching point in this lecture?

When the history doesn't clarify the picture, "examine the patient" — and if still unclear, examine again, more thoroughly. In the illustrative case, a bullseye rash on the trunk (an area not initially examined) revealed the diagnosis of Lyme disease.
Section 2.1

Q4. A patient's past medical history, allergy list, and medication list are thoroughly documented. Which statement best characterizes the role of this information in forming a differential diagnosis?

PMH, allergies, medications, social history, and family history are important contributory information — they inform risk factors and context — but the CC, HPI, and ROS are what primarily guide the initial construction of the differential diagnosis.

Physical Exam: Screening vs. If-Indicated

Essential to every exam: vital signs and general appearance — never skipped. Beyond that, the history tells you what else is relevant. The exam is taught in two "buckets":

  • Routine screening exam: a full screening of the primary system(s) of concern, identified by the chief complaint, plus any additional systems suggested by other reported symptoms.
  • "If indicated" column: added skills/maneuvers triggered whenever you encounter an abnormal screening finding — e.g., detecting a murmur leads you to reposition the patient or apply Valsalva to characterize it further.

Your differential diagnosis should also actively shape your physical exam. If malignancy is on your differential, start looking for evidence of muscle wasting, dehydration, or other supportive findings — even for a differential lower on your list. If there's no physical exam evidence to support it, you can set that differential aside and focus on more likely possibilities.

Additionally: consistently diagnose, treat, and recheck osteopathic (somatic dysfunction) findings based on your differential — but only when the patient is stable (attuned to acuity, and the patient must be cooperative and accepting of these additional osteopathic approaches).

Section 2.2

Q5. During a cardiac screening exam, a physician detects a new murmur. According to the "if indicated" approach described in this lecture, what should happen next?

Any abnormal screening finding (like a new murmur) should prompt additional "if indicated" maneuvers — positional changes, Valsalva, and so on — to further characterize the finding before moving to further testing or referral.
Section 2.2

Q6. A physician is evaluating a patient with unexplained weight loss and includes malignancy on the differential diagnosis, even though it is ranked low in likelihood. How should this affect the physical exam?

The differential diagnosis should actively shape the physical exam — even a less likely diagnosis like malignancy warrants a targeted look for supporting findings (e.g., muscle wasting, evidence of dehydration). If no supporting findings are found, that diagnosis can be reasonably deprioritized.

3. The Assessment: Differential Diagnosis & Problem List

Structuring the Assessment

The assessment = differential diagnosis + problem list. It's structured as:

  1. Chief Complaint/Presenting Problem — summarized into medical terminology (not the patient's own words). For example, "I'm coughing up blood" becomes Hemoptysis, followed by 2–3 differential diagnoses for potential causes.
    1. Differential #1 = your working/provisional diagnosis (listed first, in order of priority)
    2. Differential #2
    3. Differential #3
  2. Problem list — other chronic issues, additional symptoms/complaints, risk factors, family history, or behaviors that will impact care
  3. Somatic Dysfunction (SD)
  4. Social Determinants of Health (SDOH)

For every differential you list, justify it: cite 2–3 items from the history and/or exam that support that diagnosis. This is a step up from simply listing possibilities — you must justify your conclusions as you learn pathology.

Section 3.1

Q7. A patient reports "I'm coughing up blood." How should this be documented as the presenting problem in the assessment?

The chief complaint/presenting problem should be summarized into medical terminology (hemoptysis, not "coughing up blood"), followed by a prioritized list of 2-3 differential diagnoses for potential causes.
Section 3.1

Q8. When listing three differential diagnoses for a presenting problem, which item should be listed first, and what additional requirement applies to each item?

Differentials are listed in order of priority, with the first representing the working/provisional diagnosis. Every differential listed should be justified with 2-3 supporting items from the history and/or physical exam.

Keeping a Broad Differential

A well-reasoned, written-out assessment with rationale helps avoid tunnel vision. As the saying goes: "if you only have a hammer, everything looks like a nail." Building a broad differential means you're less likely to miss less-common diagnoses.

Illustrative Pitfall — Sore Throat A patient has tonsillar exudate, cervical lymphadenopathy, fever, hoarse voice, and fatigue — looks and smells like strep, so an antibiotic is given. The patient returns with a rash. Was it a penicillin allergy? Or — if mononucleosis wasn't on the original differential — the rash may actually reflect the classic reaction to penicillin-class antibiotics in a patient who had mono all along, not strep. Lesson: keep a broad differential so you don't miss diagnoses like this.
Section 3.2

Q9. A patient with tonsillar exudate, cervical lymphadenopathy, fever, and fatigue is treated empirically for streptococcal pharyngitis with amoxicillin and subsequently develops a diffuse rash. Which diagnosis, if it had been included in the original differential, best explains this presentation?

This scenario illustrates the classic teaching point: a sore-throat presentation that looks like strep may actually be mononucleosis, which was left off the differential. Mono classically causes a diffuse rash when patients are treated with amoxicillin/ampicillin — highlighting the importance of keeping a broad differential.
Section 3.2

Q10. Which statement best reflects the rationale for writing out a broad, justified differential diagnosis rather than simply naming the most likely condition?

Writing out a broad, justified differential — rather than fixating on one possibility — helps clinicians avoid tunnel vision ("if you only have a hammer, everything looks like a nail") and reduces the chance of missing important, less-common diagnoses.

4. The Plan: Diagnostic Testing

Baseline & Initial Studies

Unlike history and physical exam (essentially no risk), testing and treatment carry real, inherent risk — every action has a reaction/consequence. This is why getting the diagnosis right matters so much. When testing, think in terms of true positives, false positives, true negatives, and false negatives — be judicious, mindful of priorities, and aware of risk.

The two questions your attending will most often ask: what test do you want to order, and why? "We get a CBC on everybody" is not a good justification — you must be able to say why the result would change the patient's care, and why the cost, discomfort, and risk of an abnormal or false result is worth it.

Section 4.1

Q11. A student justifies ordering a CBC on every patient by saying, "we just get a CBC on everybody." How would this lecture characterize that justification?

"We get a CBC on everybody" is explicitly called out as not a good justification. The clinician should be able to explain why a test's result would make a difference in the patient's care and why the associated cost, discomfort, and risk of abnormal/false results is worthwhile.
Section 4.1

Q12. Which of the following is described as a useful marker of both liver function and anticoagulation status?

The INR (international normalized ratio) is highlighted as a good liver function test, since hepatic synthetic function affects clotting factor production, while it's also the standard marker for anticoagulation status.

Directed / Focused Diagnostic Testing

Once your history and physical have narrowed things to a few likely provisional diagnoses, you move to directed testing: ask yourself, "if I order this test, will it help me rule in or rule out a diagnosis on my differential?" These tests are often less accessible, may involve more risk, radiation, contrast, or cost — factors that affect patient access to the resource.

  • Imaging: CT, MRI, ultrasound (increasingly accessible at bedside)
  • Lab studies: cardiac markers, other serology, blood cultures
  • Diagnostic procedures: colonoscopy, bronchoscopy, additional imaging, biopsy and subsequent pathology results
Section 4.2

Q13. A physician has narrowed a differential diagnosis to three likely conditions after a thorough history and physical exam. What question should guide the choice of the next diagnostic test?

Once a differential is narrowed, the key question for choosing a focused/directed test is whether it will help rule in or rule out one of the specific diagnoses under consideration.
Section 4.2

Q14. A physician is choosing between an accessible point-of-care ultrasound and a contrast CT scan to evaluate a differential that includes both appendicitis and ectopic pregnancy in a young woman. Which factor is explicitly noted as relevant to this decision?

Directed/focused tests often involve considerations of accessibility, risk, radiation, contrast exposure, or cost — all of which affect a patient's access to that resource and should factor into test selection.

5. Building a Differential Diagnosis: Two Approaches

Occam's Razor & the Systems Approach

Occam's razor (principle of parsimony): the answer with the fewest assumptions — the simplest answer — is usually the right one. In medicine: "if you hear hoofbeats, look first for horses before you look for zebras." Look for the most common, most likely condition first — but don't fail to consider more complex or exotic diagnoses when warranted.

Systems approach: work through body systems to see which could explain the complaint — e.g., for abdominal pain, ask what cardiovascular, respiratory, GI, neurologic, musculoskeletal, GU, reproductive, endocrine, and lymphatic conditions could be responsible.

Section 5.1

Q15. A patient presents with a common set of symptoms that could be explained by either a very common, benign condition or an extremely rare syndrome. Applying Occam's razor, which approach is most appropriate?

Occam's razor favors the simplest, most likely explanation first ("horses, not zebras") — but the lecture is explicit that you shouldn't fail to consider more complex or exotic diagnoses when the evidence points that way.
Section 5.1

Q16. A physician evaluating abdominal pain asks, "Could this be cardiovascular? Respiratory? GI? Neurologic? Musculoskeletal?" and works through each body system in turn. Which method for developing a differential diagnosis is being used?

Working through the body systems (cardiovascular, respiratory, GI, neurologic, musculoskeletal, GU, reproductive, endocrine, lymphatic) to see which could explain a complaint is the systems approach to building a differential.

Etiology/Epidemiology Approach

Once you've identified the likely system involved, ask: which process within that system is causing the problem? Categories include: vascular, infectious/inflammatory, neoplastic, degenerative, deficiency/nutritional, drug-induced/iatrogenic, idiopathic, intoxication, congenital, autoimmune/allergic, traumatic (or anatomic variation), endocrine/environmental, and metabolic. Various mnemonics capture this (e.g., VINDICATE, VITAMIN CDE, and others) — you can blend the systems and etiology approaches together.

Section 5.2

Q17. After determining that a patient's complaint most likely stems from the GI system, a physician asks whether the process could be vascular (e.g., ischemic bowel), infectious/inflammatory, neoplastic, or drug-induced. Which approach is being layered on top of the systems approach?

Asking which underlying process (vascular, infectious/inflammatory, neoplastic, drug-induced, etc.) is responsible within an identified system is the etiology/epidemiology approach — often layered on top of the systems approach.
Section 5.2

Q18. A patient develops joint pain shortly after starting a new medication, with no evidence of infection, trauma, or malignancy. Which etiology category best fits this presentation?

Symptoms with a clear temporal relationship to a new medication, in the absence of infectious, traumatic, or neoplastic findings, point toward a drug-induced (iatrogenic) etiology — one of the categories in the etiology/epidemiology approach.

Section 6.1

Q19. Given only the chief complaint "right lower quadrant pain" in a 26-year-old female, which of the following diagnoses should reasonably remain on the initial differential using a systems approach?

With only "RLQ pain" and demographic information, the initial differential should be broad and span systems: GI (appendicitis, gastroenteritis, cholecystitis, etc.), GYN/OB (ectopic pregnancy, PID, ovarian cyst/torsion), and MSK (hip strain, pelvic floor spasm) — narrowed further as more history is obtained.
Section 6.1

Q20. If the same chief complaint (RLQ pain) were presented by a 67-year-old male instead of a 26-year-old female, which change to the differential is most appropriate?

Patient demographics substantially reshape a differential: an older male's RLQ pain differential drops obstetric/gynecologic causes (ectopic pregnancy, ovarian torsion) and shifts weight toward conditions more common with age, like diverticular disease, malignancy, or vascular causes.

Refining the Differential with HPI & Physical Exam

HPI The pain has been present for 1 week, described as a constant cramp that gets worse in waves (colicky — suggesting hollow viscus: bowel, gallbladder, or ureter), radiating at times to the left lower quadrant (raising appendicitis as one possibility), 8/10 at worst. A bowel movement gives some relief; she reports constipation. She's tried Tylenol, Pepto-Bismol, and Tums without relief (arguing against an upper-GI cause). She reports 3–4 bowel movements daily this past week, and yesterday noticed bright red blood on the tissue. She is sexually active (2-year relationship), uses OCPs for contraception, had a bad bronchitis infection ~3 weeks ago treated with a 10-day course of an antibiotic, and recently returned from a medical mission trip to Kenya. Her LMP was 2 months ago (she reports it's common for her to miss periods); she is G0.

How the differential narrows:

  • Bowel obstruction — less likely (loose, not truly obstructive, stools; would want to ask about flatus)
  • Pancreatitis — less likely (no prior history, no alcohol use, no abdominal wall changes)
  • Inflammatory bowel disease — less likely given her younger age and only 1 week of symptoms, though recent travel raises infectious risk (would ask about IBD family history; if pain had been chronic/recurrent, IBD would stay higher on the list)
  • Hepatitis — real risk given recent travel
  • Gastritis — lower priority given no response to Tums/Pepto-Bismol
  • Ectopic pregnancy, PID — remain real possibilities given sexual activity
  • Ovarian torsion/cyst, GYN malignancy — less likely
  • MSK causes — dropped (no exertion/strain, not positional)
  • New consideration — recent antibiotic course raises risk for antibiotic-associated diarrhea/colitis
Pearl: as you refine your differential based on the HPI, you can also add new diagnoses you hadn't initially considered (e.g., asking further UTI-related questions — urinary frequency, urgency, hesitancy, flank pain, fever — to further evaluate cystitis).
Physical Exam BP 118/68 (normal); pulse 104 (elevated — prompting cardiac auscultation and a look for causes like fever or dehydration); RR normal; T 100.8°F (enough alone to explain some tachycardia); SpO2 97% RA. Oriented x3, pale and uncomfortable-appearing. Lungs clear. Heart RRR, no murmurs/S3/S4. Abdomen flat, diminished bowel sounds in all 4 quadrants, RLQ and LLQ tenderness without rebound, no mass. Rectal: no mass, positive heme occult (confirms the reported bleeding). Pelvic: no external lesions; white mucous discharge (common, not alarming unless profuse/malodorous); cervix closed, nulliparous ("pinpoint") os, no cervical motion tenderness (lowers concern for PID); uterus retroflexed/retroverted (a normal anatomic variant, seen in 10–15%, no cause for concern); adnexa non-tender, non-enlarged, no masses bilaterally.
Section 6.2

Q21. The patient describes her abdominal pain as a constant cramp that worsens in waves. What type of pain pattern does this describe, and what does it suggest about the underlying structure involved?

Crampy, wave-like ("colicky") pain reflects the way hollow viscera generate pain as they distend and contract — classically pointing toward bowel, gallbladder, or ureteral processes.
Section 6.2

Q22. On pelvic exam, the patient has no cervical motion tenderness and non-tender adnexa. How should this finding influence the differential diagnosis?

Cervical motion tenderness is a hallmark finding of pelvic inflammatory disease (from inflammation of the pelvic structures causing pain with cervical manipulation). Its absence here lowers, though doesn't eliminate, concern for PID.
Section 6.2

Q23. The patient's uterus is noted to be retroflexed and retroverted on bimanual exam. How should this finding be interpreted?

A retroflexed/retroverted uterus (versus the more common anteflexed/anteverted position) is a normal anatomic variant seen in roughly 10-15% of women — analogous to being right- or left-handed — and is not itself a cause for concern.

Testing & Final Diagnosis

Initial/baseline studies ordered, with rationale:

  • CBC — she is pale (possible anemia) and febrile (possible leukocytosis/infection); also supports the workup for her reported rectal bleeding
  • Basic metabolic panel — diarrhea raises risk of electrolyte disturbance; also evaluates glucose and helps exclude thyroid/metabolic contributions to diarrhea
  • Urinalysis — reasonable given the differential still includes cystitis
  • Liver function tests (AST/ALT) — recent travel keeps hepatitis on the differential; starting with AST/ALT rather than a full hepatitis panel (faster, less expensive) — if normal, hepatitis is not pursued further
  • EKG, chest x-ray — not especially applicable to this presentation

Directed/focused studies depend on which differential item is being pursued: appendicitis or ectopic pregnancy → CT or bedside ultrasound (+ serum β-hCG, either qualitative or quantitative, for ectopic pregnancy); gastroenteritis → stool studies (rotavirus, culture & sensitivity, ova & parasites, C. diff toxin); pancreatitis → amylase/lipase + CT; cholecystitis/cholelithiasis → CT or ultrasound, +/- HIDA scan and LFTs; gastritis → upper endoscopy, H. pylori testing, +/- serum gastrin; IBS/IBD → colonoscopy; cystitis → urinalysis, microscopy, culture & sensitivity; PID → gonorrhea/chlamydia testing (gen probe), +/- testing for bacterial vaginosis, candidiasis, trichomonas (KOH wet mount), +/- CT/ultrasound; GI/GYN malignancy → CT, colonoscopy, or laparoscopy.

Result & Diagnosis The patient's stool tested positive for C. difficile toxin. Combined with the history (recent 10-day antibiotic course for bronchitis) and physical exam, this points to pseudomembranous colitis. Antibiotics kill off or slow certain bacterial colonies, creating a vacuum that allows other colonies (like C. difficile) to proliferate — producing severe cramping and diarrhea.
Pearl: getting the right diagnosis allowed a single, targeted treatment route rather than multiple medications/interventions, each of which would carry its own inherent risk — this is the safest and best care for the patient.
Section 6.3

Q24. The patient's stool tests positive for C. difficile toxin. Which element of her history most directly explains how she acquired this infection?

Recent antibiotic use is the classic risk factor for C. difficile infection (pseudomembranous colitis): antibiotics disrupt the normal protective colonic flora, creating an opportunity for C. difficile to proliferate and produce toxin-mediated colitis.
Section 6.3

Q25. Why did the physician order an AST/ALT rather than a full hepatitis panel as the initial test for possible hepatitis in this patient?

AST/ALT is a faster, less expensive initial screen. If these return normal, the physician may reasonably choose not to pursue the more expensive, slower-turnaround hepatitis panel further — an example of judicious, stepwise test selection.

7. Documentation: Notes & Physician Orders

Types of Notes

Once a diagnosis is reached, treatment decisions follow: admit vs. send home, oral vs. IV antibiotics, specialty consultation, patient education needs — all shaped by the care setting, the patient's support system/access to care, and any comorbid or secondary findings requiring simultaneous treatment.

Common note types, each with its own template (since different elements are critical in each setting): admission notes, progress notes, pre-op notes, operative notes, post-op notes, procedure notes, ICU notes, off-service notes, delivery notes, and discharge summaries.

Section 7.1

Q26. A patient is ready to leave the hospital after a completed admission. Which type of note is most appropriate to summarize the hospital course and post-discharge plan?

The discharge summary is the note type used to summarize a completed hospitalization and outline the post-discharge plan, distinct from notes used during the admission itself (progress notes) or for surgical care (pre-op/operative/post-op notes).

Admission Orders

A mnemonic for hospital admission orders: ADC VAAN DISSEL (or any memory aid that works for you). Every medication must be explicitly listed for a new hospitalization — the hospital will not give a medicine that wasn't ordered.

Section 7.2

Q27. A hospitalized patient's home medication list is not explicitly re-ordered on admission. What is the most likely consequence?

Every medication — new or a home medication being continued — must be explicitly listed in the admission orders. The hospital will not give a medicine that wasn't ordered, so home medications must be reconciled and re-ordered if they are to be continued.
Section 7.2

Q28. Which of the following best fits under the "Vital Signs" component of admission orders?

The vital signs component specifies how often vitals should be checked (e.g., q1hr, q2hr, q4hr, q shift) and sets notification parameters so nursing staff know when to alert the physician.

Pre-Op & Operative Notes

Pre-operative orders typically include: NPO after midnight; IV orders (fluid type and rate); pre-op antibiotics and sedation medications; labs (type & crossmatch, CBC, PT/PTT, electrolytes, BUN/Cr); prepping and shaving the surgical area; antiseptic shower; premedication (as ordered); Foley catheter or having the patient void; and post-operative orders/notes.

Operative notes document: pre-op diagnosis, post-op diagnosis, procedure performed, surgeons and assistants, anesthesia and pain control, operative findings, specimens sent to pathology, drains/tubes/IVs used, estimated blood loss (EBL) and fluids administered, tourniquet time (for orthopedic cases), any complications, and the patient's condition/disposition at the end of the case.

Section 7.3

Q29. Which of the following is a standard component of pre-operative orders?

NPO after midnight is a standard pre-operative order. Estimated blood loss, tourniquet time, post-op diagnosis, and specimens sent to pathology are all documented in the operative note, which is written after (not before) the procedure.
Section 7.3

Q30. An orthopedic surgeon completes a knee arthroscopy and wants to document how long a tourniquet was applied during the case, along with the surgical findings and disposition. Which type of note should capture this information?

Tourniquet time (relevant to orthopedic cases), operative findings, and patient disposition at the end of the case are all documented in the operative note.
