What Is an Illness Script?▸

An illness script is simply a way to organize information in your brain so it can be accessed quickly and easily — an organized list of diagnoses, significant findings, and history elements that you can pull from long-term memory the moment you need them.

Script Theory: How Your Brain Actually Does This

Your brain processes a new scene by taking in relevant information and comparing it against memories of similar things you've seen before, building a mental model as it goes. Brains are very good at picking out what doesn't fit that model.

The restaurant analogy: Walk into a restaurant and your brain immediately expects chairs, tables, plates, silverware, waiters. Those things fade into the background because they're expected. But if a giraffe is standing in the corner, that's what grabs your attention instantly — because it doesn't fit the model.

Illness scripts work the same way. You assemble the mental model of what a disease "should" look like, and then in a real encounter you're hunting for the thing that either confirms that model or is the giraffe that doesn't fit.

Script theory is simply this: organizing and structuring information in your brain so it can be retrieved from long-term memory as needed.

Key Components of an Illness Script▸

Every illness script is built from three broad categories:

CategoryWhat It Captures
FaultThe pathophysiological mechanism — what's actually going wrong
Enabling ConditionsThe contextual/patient features — epidemiology, who gets this disease and under what circumstances
ConsequencesHow the disease presents — key historical elements, signs, symptoms, and physical exam findings
Start classic, then add nuance. When you first build an illness script, start with the classic presentation — the one taught in class, the one in Harrison's, the one in your board-prep resources. As you gain clinical experience, you layer in the atypical variants and edge cases on top of that classic foundation.

There is no single right way to build an illness script — it's a personal tool. Dr. Moore's own organizational system (below) covers the same information as the Fault/Enabling Conditions/Consequences model, just structured differently. Find what works for you.

Dr. Moore's Framework: Who, What, When, Where, Why, How▸

A simple, memorable way to organize the same information:

Who — Demographic information; the epidemiology of the disease
What — The classic symptoms you'd expect and the classic exam findings; includes the presentation group (the single most common presenting symptom)
When — Timing: acute vs. chronic, constant vs. intermittent
Where — Which systems are involved (e.g., only pulmonary, or pulmonary + GI)
Why — What's mechanistically responsible for producing those symptoms and exam findings
How — Four things: (1) how we further evaluate — classic labs/imaging/workup; (2) how we treat; (3) how we follow up; (4) how the disease worsens over time

Check Your Understanding

3. In Dr. Moore's Who/What/When/Where/Why/How framework, a student is asked to identify the single most common presenting symptom for a disease so it can be quickly matched to a chief complaint on a doorway sheet. This falls under which subsection?

Answer: B. The presentation group is a subsection of "What" — it highlights the single most common presenting symptom, since that's typically what appears as the chief complaint or is buried in an exam question stem.
Presentation Groups▸

Within the "What" of every illness script, explicitly highlight the most common presentation — because that's what you'll see as the chief complaint on the chart, or buried in the stem of an exam question.

Example: Under the presentation groups cough, fever, and shortness of breath, you'd list everything from a simple URI through pneumonia (and even allergies) as possibilities.

As you take a history — from a real patient or a test question — you bring the presenting symptoms together like overlapping circles in a Venn diagram. The place where they overlap is your starting point — the illness scripts that share all of those primary symptoms are the ones you start working with first.

The Classic Encounter Flow▸

Here is how Dr. Moore represents a full patient encounter, from doorway sheet to follow-up, using illness scripts at every step:

The "classic encounter" cycle, centered on your working diagnosis and differential list (source: lecture slide 10)
  1. Doorway / initial information. From the chief complaint on the doorway sheet, use your presentation groups to assemble the Venn diagram and figure out an initial working diagnosis with a few differentials.
  2. General history. As you take the general history, you're mentally scanning for the key history elements from your candidate illness scripts — the elements that will help decide which script fits best. Your differential list shifts in real time: reordering, adding new options, changing the working diagnosis.
  3. Specific history. Once you have a good candidate list, ask the pertinent positive and negative history elements that will home in on your working diagnosis.
  4. Other history / conditions. Past medical history and other elements, again reworking your diagnosis list.
  5. Review of systems — your safety valve. All of the above has built-in confirmation bias. ROS is where you cast a wider net: think through a mnemonic like VINDICATE (or similar) and ask about symptoms from systems you haven't already covered, specifically to catch what your bias may have caused you to miss.
  6. Physical exam. Classic findings from your illness script help support or rule out the working diagnosis.
  7. Diagnostics. Labs, imaging, and other workup, applying the same logic.
  8. Assessment. Use clinical reasoning to arrive at a primary working diagnosis plus a short list of differentials.
  9. Plan. Built together with the patient.
  10. Follow-up. Closing the loop — and feeding back into future encounters.
Confirmation bias is built into this process. Because you're actively narrowing toward a working diagnosis from the very first history question, review of systems exists specifically to counteract that bias by re-opening the net before you commit to an assessment.

Check Your Understanding

5. During a history, a student is deliberately asking about symptoms from body systems that have not yet come up, specifically to counteract the narrowing that has already occurred from the general and specific history. Which part of the classic encounter is this?

Answer: B. Review of systems is described as the built-in safety valve against the confirmation bias inherent in the rest of the encounter — it deliberately casts a wider net (e.g., via a mnemonic like VINDICATE) before the working diagnosis is finalized.
Building the Assessment▸

Your assessment should always include a primary working diagnosis plus a short list of differentials — never just a single diagnosis with no alternatives considered, and never a long unfiltered list with no working favorite.

Competency Exam Format▸

You will be given a diagnosis and asked to deliver a three-minute oral overview from memory covering the main parts of that diagnosis's illness script.

Why This Format?

You'll be asked to do this constantly on rounds. You see a patient, come out, and tell your attending your working diagnosis is heart failure — and the attending says "Great. At the end of the day, give me a presentation on heart failure." You read up, organize your thoughts, and present — sometimes the same day, sometimes the next. The illness script format is a ready-made structure for exactly that kind of on-the-fly presentation.

How It Works

  • You will be given a list of diagnoses ahead of time and are expected to build and learn illness scripts for each one.
  • During competency testing, one station requires you to go before your evaluator and present entirely from your illness script — no notes.
  • A diagnosis is pulled at random (e.g., "COPD — go") and you have three minutes to talk through it.

Answer: B. The competency station requires a three-minute oral overview delivered entirely from memory, mirroring how residents are asked to summarize a working diagnosis on rounds.
The Mechanistic Alternative — Why Bother?▸

Illness scripts are a powerful way to organize and retrieve information, but they have a real disadvantage: they can anchor you. Real patients frequently don't fit the classic mold — wrong demographic, overlapping features from a different disease — and if you're locked into a script-based mental model, you may not even notice you're heading in the wrong direction. That's clinically dangerous: the wrong diagnosis risks the wrong treatment.

A mechanistic approach asks you to think about a disease process differently — not through memorized associations, but through the underlying physiology. Instead of starting with "what disease does this look like," you start with "what is normal here, and how could that normal process go wrong?"

This mechanistic approach also tracks much better with how COMLEX/USMLE Step 1 is written. Step 1 is primarily testing correlation with basic sciences — mechanisms — while the clinical-associations and treatment-heavy content that illness scripts are built around is more the domain of Level 2 and Level 3.

A patient who doesn't fit the expected demographic or has overlapping features from another disease can be missed if the clinician is anchored on the script-based model — which is clinically dangerous because it risks the wrong diagnosis and wrong treatment.

The Mechanistic Method, Step by Step▸
  1. Start with the chief complaint and the normal anatomy/physiology of that region. Before thinking about disease at all, lay out what's normally there and how it normally works.
  2. Think mechanisms, not diseases — yet. For each anatomic/physiologic category, aim for roughly 3–6 broad mechanisms that could plausibly produce the chief complaint (e.g., for hematuria: filtration barrier disruption, epithelial barrier disruption, mechanical tissue destruction).
  3. Connect each mechanism to specific physiologic disruptions — what actually happens at the tissue/cellular level under that mechanism.
  4. Apply the mechanisms to specific diseases. Now ask: how does a given disease produce the chief complaint through this particular mechanism? Different diseases under the same broad mechanism, and the same chief complaint can arise through entirely different mechanisms.
  5. Identify the most discriminating test for each mechanism. What single piece of information — a physical exam finding, a lab, an imaging study — best distinguishes one mechanism from another?
  6. Write it up and defend it. An assessment is not "here are my top three differentials." It's picking a working answer and defending it: "I think it's X because of this finding, which fits mechanism A and doesn't fit mechanism B." This is what you'll actually do in clinical practice.
Do this a couple of times on genuinely difficult-to-conceptualize chief complaints (the kidney has plenty) rather than for everything — it's time-consuming, but it pays off specifically where memorized associations break down.

Worked Example: Hematuria▸

Step 1 — Anatomy of the Complaint

Blood in the urine can originate from the kidneys, the ureters, the bladder, the urethra, or even the urethral meatus itself.

Step 2 – 4 — Mechanisms → Physiology → Disease

Broad MechanismPhysiologic DisruptionExample Disease
Filtration barrier disruption Injury at the glomerular filtration barrier allows RBCs to enter the filtrate IgA nephropathy — IgA deposition → local inflammation → podocyte injury → RBCs enter the filtrate
Mechanical / tissue destruction Physical injury damages tissue and disrupts vessels directly Kidney stone — injures epithelial tissue as it passes → vessel disruption → RBCs enter the urine

Both produce the same end result — hematuria — but by completely different mechanisms, which means the workup and treatment diverge sharply.

Step 5 — Discriminating Tests

MechanismBest Discriminator
Filtration barrier disruptionUrine microscopy — look for dysmorphic RBCs and casts
Mechanical disruption (e.g., stone)CT imaging — most stones are calcium-based and visible on CT

Practice Vignette

A 24-year-old man presents with hematuria for two days. His urine is tea-colored. He denies dysuria, frequency, urgency, and suprapubic pain. He has mild, non-severe, non-colicky bilateral flank pain.

Before jumping to a diagnosis, work through the mechanisms first: does this fit a filtration-barrier picture, a mechanical picture, or something else — and what would you order to find out? Once you've reasoned through it, write up your assessment the way you would present it — state your leading mechanism/diagnosis and defend it against the alternative(s), explaining specifically why the alternative(s) fit less well.

Check Your Understanding

11. A 24-year-old man has tea-colored urine for two days, no dysuria/frequency/urgency/suprapubic pain, and mild, non-colicky bilateral flank pain. Using mechanistic reasoning, which single test would best help distinguish a filtration-barrier mechanism (e.g., IgA nephropathy) from a mechanical/tissue-destruction mechanism (e.g., nephrolithiasis) as the cause of his hematuria?

Answer: B. Urine microscopy looking for dysmorphic RBCs and casts is the clinically used discriminator for glomerular (filtration-barrier) bleeding; a mechanical cause like a stone is better identified with CT imaging, since most stones are calcium-based and radiopaque.

12. In IgA nephropathy, which sequence correctly reflects the mechanistic chain that produces hematuria, as described in the lecture?

Answer: B. IgA deposits, triggering local inflammation that injures the podocytes; that injury is what allows red blood cells to cross into the filtrate — a filtration-barrier-disruption mechanism, distinct from a mechanical mechanism like a kidney stone.
Practicing the Mechanistic Write-Up▸

Take a chief complaint or a short vignette, run it through the full mechanistic process above, and write up your reasoning. Then trade write-ups with a peer:

  • Have your peer assess your write-up against the physiologic model — mechanisms, discriminating tests, how you ruled things in and out.
  • Each of you should walk away having learned at least one thing from the other's reasoning.

This is offered as an alternative and complement to illness scripts — not a wholesale replacement.

Summary: Illness Scripts vs. Mechanistic Reasoning▸
Illness ScriptsMechanistic Reasoning
Organizing principle Disease-first: memorized associations (Who/What/When/Where/Why/How) built around the classic presentation Mechanism-first: starts from normal physiology, then asks how it can go wrong
Best for Rapid clinical retrieval; oral presentations; rounds; the classic/typical patient Atypical presentations; understanding why; discriminating between look-alike diagnoses
Exam correlation Clinical associations, treatment — more heavily tested on Level 2 / Level 3 Basic-science correlation — more heavily tested on Step/Level 1
Main risk Anchoring bias — may miss atypical patients who don't fit the script Time-consuming if used for everything; not practical for every single encounter
Built-in safety check Review of systems (cast a wider net against confirmation bias) Explicitly defending the assessment against alternative mechanisms
