1. Cardiac Electrophysiology & the Action Potential

Polarization, Depolarization, Repolarization

  • Resting cardiac cells are electrically polarized: the inside is negative relative to the outside, maintained by membrane pumps controlling K⁺, Na⁺, Cl⁻, and Ca²⁺ distribution
  • Depolarization = loss of internal negativity. In pacemaker cells it occurs spontaneously; in other cells it is triggered by an arriving electrical impulse causing positive ions to cross the membrane
  • Depolarization propagates cell-to-cell as a wave across the entire heart — this wave of electricity is what the EKG records
  • Repolarization restores resting polarity via membrane pumps reversing ion flow — unlike depolarization, this requires active cellular energy expenditure

Three Cell Types

Cell TypeRole
Pacemaker cellsDepolarize spontaneously; determine heart rate (electrical "power source")
Electrical conducting cellsThe "hard wiring" — transmit the impulse rapidly through the heart
Myocardial cellsContractile machinery of the heart
Every cardiac cell has automaticity — the latent ability to act as a pacemaker. This is normally suppressed unless the dominant sinus node fails or something abnormal stimulates it.

The Pacemaker Action Potential vs. Phases of Contraction

  • Pacemaker cells lack a true resting potential: charge drops to only about −60 mV briefly, then spontaneously rises to threshold, triggering the next action potential
PhaseIonic EventCorresponding EKG Feature
Phase 4Resting/diastole; high intracellular K⁺Baseline (TP segment)
Phase 0Rapid Na⁺ influx + slow Ca²⁺ influx — depolarization/contraction beginsQRS complex
Phase 1Na⁺ channels close; K⁺ exits; Cl⁻ entersEnd of depolarization
Phase 2 (plateau)Slow Na⁺/Ca²⁺ influx continues; K⁺ continues to leave; muscle still contractingST segment
Phase 3Ca²⁺ channels close/Ca²⁺ exits; rapid K⁺ efflux; membrane potential becomes negativeT wave
End of Phase 3Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase pump restores resting potentialReturn to baseline
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Which phase of the cardiac action potential corresponds to the QRS complex on the EKG?

Explanation: Phase 0 is the rapid depolarization phase (fast Na⁺ influx plus slow Ca²⁺ influx), which initiates contraction and corresponds to the QRS complex on the surface EKG.

2. A pacemaker cell differs from other cardiac cells in that it:

Explanation: Pacemaker cells lack a true resting potential; their charge briefly drops to about -60 mV before spontaneously rising to threshold, triggering the next action potential without external stimulation.

3. Repolarization differs fundamentally from depolarization in that repolarization:

Explanation: Unlike depolarization, which is a largely passive ionic event, repolarization requires active energy expenditure by membrane pumps (like the Na/K-ATPase) to restore the resting ionic gradients, making it more susceptible to metabolic and pharmacologic influences.

2. EKG Basics: Paper, Leads & Wave Properties

The 12-Lead Setup

  • Two electrodes on the arms, two on the legs → form the six limb leads (three standard + three augmented)
  • Six electrodes across the chest → the six precordial (chest) leads
  • Standard electrode positioning protocols allow valid comparison of EKGs taken at different times/settings on the same patient

EKG Paper: The Grid

Standard EKG paper speed is 25 mm/second. Small squares = 1×1 mm; large (bold) squares = 5×5 mm.
AxisSmall SquareLarge Square
Horizontal (time)0.04 seconds0.2 seconds (5 small squares)
Vertical (voltage)0.1 millivolts0.5 millivolts

Five large squares together = 1 full second. Doubling paper speed to 50 mm/second "stretches out" the tracing — useful for distinguishing arrhythmias, but must be recognized or rate/interval calculations will be wrong.

Three Wave Characteristics

  • Duration — measured in fractions of a second
  • Amplitude — measured in millivolts
  • Configuration — the shape/appearance of the wave
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. On standard EKG paper, how many seconds does one large (bold) box represent?

Explanation: One large box contains five small squares, each 0.04 seconds, totaling 0.2 seconds — a key reference for measuring intervals like the PR interval and QRS duration.

2. A technician doubles the EKG paper speed from the standard 25 mm/sec to 50 mm/sec to better evaluate an arrhythmia. What is the main consequence of this change?

Explanation: Doubling the paper speed to 50 mm/sec stretches the tracing horizontally for the same time interval, which can help distinguish arrhythmias but requires the reader to recognize the altered speed, or rate/interval calculations using standard methods will be inaccurate.

3. Along the vertical axis of EKG paper, one large (5x5 mm) box corresponds to how many millivolts?

Explanation: Each small square vertically represents 0.1 mV; a large box (5 small squares) therefore represents 0.5 mV — used to assess amplitude criteria such as those for ventricular hypertrophy.

3. P Wave, PR Interval & Atrial Depolarization

The P Wave

  • Atrial depolarization begins at the sinus (SA) node: right atrium depolarizes first, left atrium follows a few milliseconds later
  • The vector of atrial depolarization points right to left and slightly inferior
  • Leads viewing this wave of depolarization approaching record a positive deflection; leads viewing it receding record a negative deflection
LeadP Wave DeflectionWhy
I, aVL (left lateral); II, aVF (inferior)PositiveDirectly in line with the atrial depolarization vector
IIIOften biphasicSits at ~+120°, nearly perpendicular to the atrial current
aVRPurely negativeMost rightward lead (~−150°); sees current moving away from it

The Sinus Node

  • Dominant pacemaker cells of the right atrium; fire at 60–100/min, modulated heavily by the autonomic nervous system and metabolic demand

PR Interval vs. PR Segment

PR interval: start of atrial depolarization → start of ventricular depolarization (includes the AV nodal delay). Normal: 0.12–0.20 seconds (~3–5 mm).
PR segment: end of atrial depolarization → start of ventricular depolarization (just the AV nodal delay portion); usually horizontal, along the same baseline as the start of the P wave.
  • The AV node deliberately slows conduction "to a crawl" — this physiologic delay lets the atria finish contracting before the ventricles begin
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Lead aVR characteristically shows a purely negative P wave. This is best explained by which fact?

Explanation: aVR sits at approximately -150 degrees, the most rightward orientation of the frontal plane leads, so it views the right-to-left, slightly inferior vector of atrial depolarization as receding from it, producing a purely negative deflection.

2. What physiologic purpose does the AV nodal delay (reflected in the PR segment) serve?

Explanation: The AV node deliberately slows conduction, creating the physiologic delay reflected in the PR segment. This ensures the atria complete their contraction and empty into the ventricles before ventricular depolarization and contraction begin.

3. A patient's PR interval measures 0.28 seconds. How should this be characterized relative to normal?

Explanation: The normal PR interval is 0.12-0.20 seconds. A PR interval of 0.28 seconds exceeds this range, indicating a delay in conduction from the atria to the ventricles (as seen in first-degree AV block).

4. QRS Complex & Ventricular Conduction

The Ventricular Conducting System

  • Bundle of His emerges from the AV node → divides into right and left bundle branches
  • Right bundle branch carries current down the right side of the interventricular septum
  • Left bundle branch has three fascicles: septal (depolarizes the interventricular septum left-to-right), anterior (anterior LV wall), posterior (posterior LV wall)
  • Purkinje fibers are the terminal fascicles of both bundle branches, delivering current into ventricular myocardium
The earliest part of the QRS represents septal depolarization via the septal fascicle of the left bundle branch (left-to-right). This tiny initial deflection may be visible as a small negative deflection (Q wave) in leads I, aVL, V5, and V6.

Naming QRS Components

WaveDefinition
Q waveFirst deflection, if downward (only the first wave of the complex can be called a Q wave)
R waveFirst upward deflection
R′A second upward deflection
S waveAny downward deflection following an upward deflection (not the first wave)
QS waveEntire complex is a single downward deflection

Normal vs. Abnormal Q Waves & R Wave Amplitude

Normal Q wave: <0.04 sec wide and <3 mm deep.
Abnormal ("pathologic") Q wave: >3 mm deep or >1/3 the height of the QRS complex — suggests prior transmural infarction/scar.
Normal R wave: ≤7.5 mm high (in the small/medium precordial leads before hypertrophy criteria are applied).

R Wave Progression

  • V1 (overlies right ventricle): deep S wave — current moves leftward, away from the right side
  • V5/V6 (overlie left ventricle): tall, positive R waves
  • The progressive increase in R wave amplitude moving right-to-left across the precordial leads is called R wave progression
  • The left ventricle, being far more massive, dominates the remainder of the QRS; the average vector swings leftward, generally 0° to +90°
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A downward deflection can only be labeled a 'Q wave' under which condition?

Explanation: By convention, a downward deflection is called a Q wave only if it is the very first wave of the complex. Any downward deflection that follows an upward (R) wave is instead called an S wave, regardless of its size.

2. Which fascicle of the left bundle branch is responsible for the earliest portion of the QRS complex, depolarizing the interventricular septum left-to-right?

Explanation: The septal fascicle of the left bundle branch depolarizes the interventricular septum in a left-to-right direction first, producing the earliest portion of the QRS complex (sometimes visible as a small Q wave in leads I, aVL, V5, and V6).

3. Why does lead V1 typically show a deep S wave while V5/V6 show tall R waves during normal ventricular depolarization?

Explanation: V1 sits over the right ventricle; as the much more massive left ventricle depolarizes and dominates the overall current, that leftward-moving current moves away from V1 (producing a deep S wave) and toward V5/V6 (producing tall R waves) — the basis of normal R wave progression.

5. ST Segment, T Wave & QT Interval

Repolarization & the T Wave

  • Repolarization usually begins in the last area depolarized and travels backward (opposite direction from depolarization)
  • Both an approaching depolarization wave and a receding repolarization wave produce a positive deflection — so leads with tall R waves normally also show positive T waves
  • Normal T wave amplitude: 1/3 to 2/3 the height of the corresponding R wave
  • Repolarization requires active energy expenditure, making the T wave highly susceptible to cardiac and noncardiac influences (electrolytes, ischemia, drugs)

Segments & Intervals: Precise Definitions

Segment/IntervalSpansNormal Duration
PR intervalStart of atrial depolarization → start of ventricular depolarization0.12–0.20 sec
PR segmentEnd of P wave → start of QRS(part of PR interval)
QRS interval/durationDuration of the QRS complex alone0.06–0.10 sec
ST segmentEnd of QRS → start of T wave (end of ventricular depolarization → start of repolarization)Usually horizontal/gently upsloping
QT intervalStart of QRS → end of T wave (all ventricular electrical events)QTc ≤0.44 sec

The QT Interval & Heart Rate

QT duration is inversely proportional to heart rate: faster rates → shorter QT (repolarization must keep pace); slower rates → longer QT. Repolarization accounts for ~40% of the normal cardiac cycle (measured R-to-R). The QTc (corrected QT, adjusted for heart rate) is normally ≤0.44 seconds — critical when evaluating arrhythmia risk or drug-induced QT prolongation.
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Why do leads that record tall R waves during depolarization normally also record positive T waves during repolarization?

Explanation: Both an approaching wave of depolarization and a receding wave of repolarization generate a positive deflection on the EKG in the same lead, which is why leads with tall R waves typically also show upright, positive T waves.

2. As heart rate increases, what happens to the QT interval and why?

Explanation: The QT interval is inversely related to heart rate: as the heart beats faster, repolarization must occur more quickly to be ready for the next contraction, shortening the QT interval; at slower rates, the QT interval lengthens.

3. A patient's corrected QT interval (QTc) is measured at 0.52 seconds. This value is significant primarily because:

Explanation: The normal QTc is <=0.44 seconds. A QTc of 0.52 seconds is prolonged, which is clinically important because prolonged QT increases the risk of dangerous ventricular arrhythmias such as polymorphic ventricular tachycardia/torsades de pointes, particularly with certain medications.

6. Calculating Heart Rate

Three Methods

MethodFormulaExample
Small square method1500 ÷ (number of small squares between consecutive R waves)15 small squares → 1500/15 = 100 bpm
Large square method300 ÷ (number of large squares between consecutive R waves)4 large squares → 300/4 = 75 bpm
"300-150-100" sequence method (preferred by the lecturer)Find an R wave falling on/near a bold line; count down the next bold lines as 300, 150, 100, 75, 60, 50...Next R falls between 100 and 75 → estimate ~85–86 bpm
Practical Tip Always confirm the rhythm strip is running at the standard 25 mm/sec before calculating rate — paper speed is sometimes doubled to 50 mm/sec to better visualize arrhythmias, which will throw off any rate calculation if not recognized.
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Using the small square method, if there are 20 small squares between two consecutive R waves, what is the heart rate?

Explanation: The small square method divides 1500 by the number of small squares between R waves: 1500 / 20 = 75 beats per minute.

2. Using the large square ('300 method') sequence, if the next R wave falls exactly on the third bold line after the starting R wave, what is the heart rate?

Explanation: The sequence for the large square method is 300 (1st line), 150 (2nd line), 100 (3rd line), 75 (4th line), and so on. An R wave falling on the third bold line corresponds to a rate of 100 bpm.

3. Before applying any of the standard rate calculation methods, what must first be confirmed about the EKG tracing?

Explanation: Since paper speed is sometimes doubled to 50 mm/second to help evaluate arrhythmias, confirming the standard 25 mm/second speed is essential before applying the small square, large square, or sequence methods — otherwise the calculated rate will be inaccurate.

📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. An EKG shows a positive QRS deflection in lead I and a negative QRS deflection in aVF. What is the axis?

Explanation: Positive in lead I with negative in aVF indicates left axis deviation, corresponding to an axis between -30 and -90 degrees.

2. A patient presents with acute shortness of breath and is found to have a new right axis deviation on EKG. Which diagnosis should be strongly considered given this finding?

Explanation: Right axis deviation can result from acute lung disease including pulmonary embolism (via acute right heart strain), as well as COPD, right ventricular hypertrophy, and sodium channel blocker toxicity.

3. Which lead is described as the only true right-sided limb lead, with an angle of orientation of approximately -150 degrees?

Explanation: aVR is the most rightward of all frontal plane leads (~-150 degrees) and is considered the only true right-sided limb lead, which is why it normally records a purely negative P wave and QRS.

8. Approach to Rhythm & Causes of Arrhythmia

The Four Rhythm Questions

Every time you assess rhythm, ask: (1) Are there normal P waves present? (2) Is the QRS narrow or wide? (3) What is the relationship between the P wave and the QRS? (4) Is the rhythm regular or irregular?

Normal Rate Ranges (Adult)

CategoryRate
Normal sinus rhythm60–100 bpm, P wave before every QRS
Tachycardia>100 bpm
Bradycardia<60 bpm

Pediatric patients (newborns, children) have faster normal resting rates than adults.

Causes of Arrhythmia

S — Sympathetic stimulation (enhanced sympathetic tone from any cause)
H — Hypoxia (oxygen-deprived myocardium becomes irritable)
E — Electrolyte disturbances (hypo/hyperkalemia especially)
B — Bradycardia (slow rates predispose to arrhythmia)
I — Ischemia (an ischemic heart is irritable; seen in acute infarction)
D — Drugs/toxins (including the very antiarrhythmics used to treat arrhythmias)
S — Stretch (chamber enlargement/hypertrophy predisposes to arrhythmia)

Symptoms of Arrhythmia

  • Many arrhythmias are asymptomatic, found incidentally
  • Palpitations — awareness of one's own heartbeat (accelerations, decelerations, or sustained rapid rate)
  • Decreased cardiac output symptoms — lightheadedness, syncope
  • Increased myocardial O2 demand — angina-type pain
  • Can precipitate CHF in a patient with underlying cardiac disease
  • Rarely, the first manifestation is cardiac arrest or sudden death
Important Not every arrhythmia is abnormal or dangerous — e.g., trained athletes may have normal resting heart rates as low as 35–40 bpm.
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A patient in the ICU with a serum potassium of 6.8 mEq/L develops new ventricular ectopy. Which letter of the arrhythmia mnemonic does this best represent?

Explanation: Hyperkalemia is a classic electrolyte disturbance that predisposes to various arrhythmias, represented by the 'E' in the arrhythmia causation framework.

2. A trained marathon runner is found to have a resting heart rate of 38 bpm on a routine physical. What is the most appropriate interpretation?

Explanation: Not every arrhythmia (including bradycardia) is abnormal or dangerous. Highly trained athletes can have normal resting heart rates as low as 35-40 bpm due to increased vagal tone, without underlying pathology.

3. When systematically assessing cardiac rhythm, which of the following is NOT one of the four key questions to ask?

Explanation: The four systematic rhythm questions are: are P waves present, is the QRS narrow or wide, what is the P-QRS relationship, and is the rhythm regular or irregular. Blood pressure, while clinically relevant, is not one of these four EKG-focused questions.

9. Sinus Rhythms & Escape Rhythms

Sinus Arrhythmias

RhythmRateNotes
Sinus tachycardia>100 bpmP wave before every QRS; consider sepsis, hypovolemia, PE, MI, hyperthyroidism, heart failure, pain, fear
Sinus bradycardia<60 bpmP wave before every QRS, just slower; often a disorder of impulse formation
Sinus arrhythmiaNormal rangeMild irregularity, often respiratory (varies with breathing)

Escape Rhythms

When the sinus node fails to fire, subsidiary pacemakers "escape" and take over, each with its own intrinsic rate — progressively slower the lower in the conduction system:
Escape PacemakerIntrinsic Rate
Sinus node60–100 bpm
Atrial pacemaker60–75 bpm
Junctional pacemaker40–60 bpm
Ventricular (idioventricular) pacemaker30–45 bpm
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A patient's EKG shows a narrow QRS at a rate of 50 bpm with no discernible P wave associated with any QRS complex. Which escape rhythm is most consistent with this rate and QRS width?

Explanation: A junctional pacemaker fires at 40-60 bpm and produces a narrow QRS (since conduction still proceeds through the normal His-Purkinje system) with no associated P wave, matching this presentation.

2. Which of the following differential diagnoses should be considered in a patient found to have new sinus tachycardia?

Explanation: Sinus tachycardia has a broad differential including sepsis, hypovolemia, pulmonary embolism, myocardial infarction, hyperthyroidism, heart failure, pain, and fear — it should prompt a search for an underlying cause rather than being dismissed.

3. If the sinus node fails and both the atrial and junctional escape pacemakers also fail to activate, what is the expected intrinsic rate of the resulting ventricular (idioventricular) escape rhythm?

Explanation: The idioventricular (ventricular) escape pacemaker, the lowest and slowest in the hierarchy, has an intrinsic rate of only about 30-45 bpm, activating only when all higher pacemakers (sinus, atrial, junctional) fail.

10. Supraventricular Arrhythmias

Atrial Flutter

  • Characteristic sawtooth pattern, visible in all leads (not lead-specific)
  • Narrow QRS; regularly abnormal rhythm; atrial rate classically ~150 bpm-range flutter waves with variable AV conduction ratios

Atrial Fibrillation

  • Irregularly irregular rhythm; rate variable (fast or slow if there's an associated block)
  • Risk factors: hypertension, heart failure, diabetes, advanced age, hyperthyroidism, intrinsic cardiac disease

Multifocal Atrial Tachycardia (MAT)

Also called "wandering atrial pacemaker" when non-tachycardic — a chaotic atrial rhythm from increased automaticity within multiple atrial foci. Fairly uncommon; classically seen in elderly patients with COPD or CHF.
  • Narrow QRS, variable/tachycardic rate, with ≥3 distinct P wave morphologies (multiple atrial foci)
  • Seldom life-threatening; consider theophylline toxicity, COPD, CHF
  • Key pearl: when MAT occurs without underlying COPD or CHF, strongly consider acute pulmonary embolism — acute right heart strain makes the right atrium irritable

Supraventricular Tachycardia (SVT) & Reentry

  • SVT = regular, narrow-complex tachycardia originating above the ventricle
  • Paroxysmal SVT (PSVT) starts and stops abruptly; P wave often buried in the preceding T wave; causes include WPW, anemia, dehydration/fatigue, excess caffeine, pseudoephedrine, and hyperthyroidism
Reentry mechanism: a premature beat arrives while one of two conduction pathways (A, fast) is still refractory; the impulse travels down the other pathway (B, slow), which has recovered, then circles back up pathway A once it too has recovered — creating a self-sustaining reentry loop.

AVRT vs. AVNRT

AVRT (AV Reentry Tachycardia)AVNRT (AV Nodal Reentry Tachycardia)
MechanismReentry circuit between AV node and an anatomical accessory pathwayReentry circuit looping within the AV node itself
RateVariable150–250 bpm, regular
Orthodromic AVRTConducts via AV node → regular, narrow complex—
Antidromic AVRTConducts via accessory pathway → regular, wide complex—
P waveRetrograde, long R-P intervalRetrograde; may see "pseudo R′" in V1; often buried in QRS
EpidemiologyMost common SVT in children (~30%)Most common SVT in adults (~50%)
Critical Clinical Point A wide-complex, regular tachycardia from antidromic AVRT is very difficult to distinguish from ventricular tachycardia. Treat it as VT — do NOT give AV nodal blockers (beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers), which can be dangerous if this is actually pre-excited AFib/VT.

Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW) Syndrome

  • Congenital accessory pathway (bundle of Kent) causing pre-excitation; affects <0.2% of the population
  • Baseline sinus rhythm EKG findings: short PR interval (<0.12 sec), delta wave (slurred upstroke of the QRS), slight QRS prolongation, ST/T discordance, possible pseudo-infarct pattern
  • Associated tacky arrhythmias: paroxysmal SVT (via reentry through the bundle of Kent) or atrial fibrillation
  • Small but real risk of sudden cardiac death
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A 65-year-old with COPD develops a narrow-complex tachycardia with at least three distinct P wave morphologies on the rhythm strip. What is the most likely diagnosis?

Explanation: Multifocal atrial tachycardia (MAT) is defined by narrow QRS complexes with a variable, tachycardic rate and at least three distinct P wave morphologies, reflecting multiple atrial foci. It is classically associated with COPD, CHF, and theophylline toxicity.

2. A patient with no history of lung or cardiac disease develops multifocal atrial tachycardia. Which diagnosis should be strongly suspected?

Explanation: When MAT occurs in a patient without underlying COPD or CHF, acute pulmonary embolism should be strongly suspected — the acute strain on the right heart from pumping against a pulmonary clot burden makes the right atrium irritable.

3. A patient with known WPW develops a regular, wide-complex tachycardia (antidromic AVRT). What is the appropriate management approach?

Explanation: A wide-complex, regular tachycardia from antidromic AVRT is difficult to distinguish clinically from ventricular tachycardia, so it should be treated as VT. AV nodal blocking agents (beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers) should be avoided, as they can be dangerous in pre-excited rhythms.

11. Premature Complexes & Junctional Rhythm

Premature Atrial Contraction (PAC)

  • A P wave arrives earlier than expected in the sinus cycle, typically followed by a normal, narrow QRS

Premature Junctional Contraction (PJC)

  • A narrow QRS complex arrives early with no preceding P wave — the impulse originates above the ventricle (narrow QRS) but not from the sinus node

Junctional Rhythm

Defining features: narrow QRS (impulse originates above the ventricle) with no P wave associated with any QRS complex. Rate is typically 40–60 bpm, consistent with the junctional pacemaker's intrinsic rate.
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. An EKG shows an early, narrow QRS complex with no P wave preceding it, followed by a return to normal sinus rhythm. What is this best described as?

Explanation: A PJC presents as an early, narrow QRS complex with no preceding P wave, since the impulse originates in the AV junction (above the ventricle, producing a narrow complex, but not from the sinus node).

2. A sustained rhythm shows narrow QRS complexes at a rate of 45 bpm with no P waves associated with any QRS. What is this rhythm?

Explanation: A junctional rhythm shows a narrow QRS (impulse originates above the ventricle) with no associated P waves, at a rate consistent with the junctional pacemaker's intrinsic rate of 40-60 bpm.

3. What key feature distinguishes a PAC from a PJC?

Explanation: A PAC is characterized by a P wave arriving earlier than expected (from an ectopic atrial focus), typically followed by a normal QRS, whereas a PJC has no preceding P wave since the impulse originates in the AV junction.

12. Ventricular Arrhythmias

Premature Ventricular Contraction (PVC)

  • Most common ventricular arrhythmia; wide, bizarre QRS (≥0.12 sec) because depolarization bypasses the normal conduction pathway
  • Usually followed by a compensatory pause; if it falls between two normal beats with no pause, it's an interpolated PVC
  • Isolated PVCs are common in normal hearts and rarely need intervention — but a PVC during acute MI is more ominous (impending ischemia; can trigger VT/VF)
  • Bigeminy = 1 normal beat : 1 PVC pattern; trigeminy = 2 normal beats : 1 PVC pattern

Ventricular Tachycardia (VT)

  • ≥3 consecutive PVCs at a regular rate >120 bpm (usually 150–220 bpm); wide QRS; can show AV dissociation
  • Classified as sustained (>30 sec or hemodynamically unstable) vs. nonsustained, and as monomorphic (single QRS morphology) vs. polymorphic (varying morphology)
  • Fusion beats: sinus impulse partially conducts and collides with the ventricular impulse → hybrid QRS morphology
  • Capture beats: sinus impulse fully "captures" the ventricle → a normal-appearing QRS amid the VT
  • Polymorphic VT: multiple QRS morphologies; higher risk in patients with prolonged QT; treat with IV magnesium
  • Torsades de pointes: uncommon form of polymorphic VT with gradual amplitude change and "twisting" of the QRS around the isoelectric line; associated with prolonged QT

Ventricular Fibrillation (VF)

A terminal rhythm — fine or coarse chaotic zigzag with no true QRS complexes, no organized electrical activity, and no cardiac output. Requires immediate CPR and/or defibrillation.

Accelerated Idioventricular Rhythm (AIVR)

  • Rate 50–100 bpm, wide QRS; seen during acute MI or after reperfusion (angioplasty/stenting or thrombolytics)

📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A PVC falls between two normally conducted sinus beats without disrupting the underlying rhythm or causing a pause. What is this called?

Explanation: An interpolated PVC occurs between two normally conducted beats without a compensatory pause, distinguishing it from the typical PVC pattern that is followed by a pause.

2. A patient with a known history of prolonged QT syndrome develops a wide-complex tachycardia with multiple, constantly changing QRS morphologies and a twisting appearance around the isoelectric line. What is the diagnosis and first-line treatment?

Explanation: This description is classic for torsades de pointes, a form of polymorphic VT associated with prolonged QT interval; first-line treatment is IV magnesium, in addition to correcting any underlying electrolyte abnormality and discontinuing QT-prolonging drugs.

3. A wide-complex, irregular tachycardia with constantly varying QRS morphology (not a single consistent wide morphology) is most consistent with which rhythm?

Explanation: Using the width/regularity framework, a wide, irregular QRS tachycardia with varying morphology corresponds to polymorphic ventricular tachycardia, distinct from monomorphic VT (wide, regular).

13. Conduction Blocks

Three Categories of Block

  • Sinus node block: sinus node fires normally, but the impulse is blocked from spreading through atrial tissue — looks like a pause on the EKG
  • AV block: any block between the sinus node and terminal Purkinje fibers (includes AV node and bundle of His)
  • Bundle branch block: block in one or both ventricular bundle branches; partial block of one fascicle = hemiblock

📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. An EKG shows progressive lengthening of the PR interval with each beat until one P wave is not followed by a QRS, after which the pattern repeats. What is this rhythm?

Explanation: Progressive PR interval lengthening culminating in a dropped beat, with the cycle then repeating, is the hallmark of Mobitz I (Wenckebach) second-degree AV block, typically due to a block within the AV node.

2. A patient has a fixed, normal PR interval with a sudden, unpredicted dropped QRS complex (no progressive PR lengthening beforehand). This finding is concerning because it suggests:

Explanation: A fixed PR interval with a sudden, unwarned dropped beat is characteristic of Mobitz II second-degree AV block, usually due to disease below the AV node (in the bundle of His). This carries a much higher risk of progressing to complete heart block and often requires pacing, unlike the more benign Mobitz I.

3. On EKG, the P waves and QRS complexes appear to occur at completely independent, unrelated rates with no relationship between them. What is this finding called, and what does the patient need?

Explanation: Complete independence between atrial (P wave) and ventricular (QRS) activity defines third-degree AV block, also called AV dissociation. The ventricles generate their own slow escape rhythm, and a pacemaker is required.

14. Pacemakers on EKG

Indications for Pacemaker Placement

  • Third-degree AV block/AV dissociation
  • Sick sinus syndrome (a form of AV block or bradycardia)
  • Acute MI causing AV block or bundle branch block
  • Recurrent ventricular tachycardia (overdrive pacing to prevent VT)
  • Symptomatic Mobitz II

Recognizing Pacer Spikes

Pacing ModeEKG Appearance
Atrial pacingSpike → P wave → normal PR interval → normal narrow QRS
Ventricular pacingSpike before a wide QRS resembling LBBB (RV electrode depolarizes the right ventricle first, then spreads cell-to-cell to the left ventricle)
Sequential (dual-chamber) pacingAtrial spike → P wave → ventricular spike → wide QRS
Board Tip A ventricular pacer produces a wide QRS that looks like LBBB because the right ventricle (where the electrode sits) depolarizes first, and the current then spreads to the left ventricle by slower cell-to-cell conduction — mimicking the sequence seen in native LBBB.
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A ventricular pacemaker's paced QRS complex characteristically resembles which native conduction abnormality, and why?

Explanation: Because the ventricular pacing electrode typically sits in the right ventricle, that chamber depolarizes first, with the left ventricle depolarizing afterward via slower cell-to-cell conduction — mimicking the sequence of a native left bundle branch block on EKG.

2. Which of the following is an indication for pacemaker placement?

Explanation: Third-degree AV block (complete heart block/AV dissociation) is a clear indication for pacemaker placement, unlike the generally benign first-degree block or transient Mobitz I, or the normal variant of respiratory sinus arrhythmia.

3. On an EKG with sequential (dual-chamber) pacing, what is the expected sequence of findings?

Explanation: Sequential (dual-chamber) pacing produces an atrial spike followed by a P wave, and then a separate ventricular spike followed by a wide QRS complex, reflecting pacing of both chambers in sequence.

15. Ischemia & Infarction

STEMI vs. NSTEMI

STEMINSTEMI
ST segmentElevated, classic "tombstone" morphologyNormal or minor changes (T wave inversion, ST depression)
PathophysiologyComplete coronary occlusion — true emergencyPartial occlusion; less myocardium involved
Diagnosis basisEKG criteria (see below)Primarily troponin/clinical presentation, since EKG changes are subtle

Diagnostic Criteria (ESC/ACC/AHA)

  • STEMI: new ST elevation at the J point in two contiguous leads meeting these cut-points:
Lead GroupMinimum ST Elevation
All leads except V2–V3≥1 mm
V2–V3, males ≥40 years≥2 mm
V2–V3, males <40 years≥2.5 mm
V2–V3, females (any age)≥1.5 mm
  • NSTEMI/unstable angina: new horizontal or downsloping ST depression in contiguous leads

Evolution of STEMI Changes (Granular Sequence)

1. Hyperacute T wave
2. Elevation of the J point with ST segment concavity
3. ST elevation becomes more pronounced, turns convex (rounded upward) — the classic "tombstone"
4. ST segment and T wave merge into one complex
5. (After reperfusion) ST returns to baseline; initial Q wave develops; loss of R wave amplitude
6. T wave inverts, or may eventually return to baseline — but Q waves persist as a permanent marker of prior infarction/scar

Reciprocal Changes

  • ST depression in leads opposite the infarcting territory reflects ischemia (not injury) in that region and helps localize/confirm a STEMI
  • Example: anterolateral STEMI (I, aVL, V2–V6) with reciprocal ST depression in the inferior leads (III, aVF)
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A patient presents with chest pain and an EKG showing new ST elevation with a 'tombstone' appearance across the anterior precordial leads, plus ST depression in the inferior leads. How should the inferior ST depression be interpreted?

Explanation: ST depression in leads opposite the infarcting territory represents reciprocal changes, reflecting diminished blood flow/ischemia (not acute injury) in that region, and helps confirm the diagnosis and localize the true area of infarction.

2. Weeks after a STEMI, an EKG shows resolution of ST elevation and normalization of T waves, but Q waves remain present in the previously affected leads. What does this finding represent?

Explanation: As the infarcted myocardium heals and scars, Q waves develop and persist as a permanent marker of the region of prior infarction, even after the ST segment normalizes and the T wave returns upright.

3. A patient with classic anginal symptoms has an EKG showing T wave inversions and mild ST depression in the lateral leads, without significant ST elevation. Troponin is elevated. This presentation is most consistent with:

Explanation: Subtle EKG changes (T wave inversion, mild ST depression) without ST elevation, in the setting of elevated troponin and anginal symptoms, is classic for NSTEMI, which is diagnosed primarily by biomarkers and clinical presentation rather than dramatic EKG findings.

16. Chamber Enlargement & Ventricular Hypertrophy

Atrial Enlargement (Best Seen in Leads II and V1)

Right Atrial EnlargementLeft Atrial Enlargement
P wave change↑Amplitude of the first part of the P wave ("P pulmonale")↑Amplitude/notching of the second part of the P wave
Cause (pressure/volume overload)Tricuspid regurgitation, pulmonic valve stenosis, pulmonary hypertension, cor pulmonale (chronic lung disease)Mitral stenosis, LV dysfunction (pressure overload); mitral regurgitation, ventricular septal defect (volume overload)

Right Ventricular Hypertrophy (RVH)

  • Most common feature: right axis deviation; QRS remains narrow (<120 ms)
  • Tall R wave in V1 — R>S, >7 mm tall, or R/S ratio >1
  • Dominant S wave in V6 — >7 mm deep, or R/S ratio <1
  • Right ventricular strain pattern (V1–V3, II, III, aVF): T wave inversion, ST depression, ± RBBB or incomplete RBBB

Left Ventricular Hypertrophy (LVH)

Voltage criteria (Sokolow-Lyon): S wave in V1 + R wave in V5 or V6 (whichever is taller) > 35 mm (7 large boxes).
  • Non-voltage criteria: increased R wave peak time >50 ms in V5/V6; ST depression and T wave inversion in the left-sided leads (a "left ventricular strain" pattern)
  • Increased R wave amplitude in leads overlying the left ventricle is the underlying basis for the voltage-based diagnosis
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. An EKG shows notching and increased amplitude of the second component of the P wave in lead II. This finding suggests:

Explanation: Left atrial enlargement is reflected by increased amplitude or notching of the second (terminal) portion of the P wave, often due to pressure overload from conditions like mitral stenosis or LV dysfunction with mitral regurgitation.

2. Using the Sokolow-Lyon voltage criteria, LVH is diagnosed when the sum of the S wave depth in V1 and the R wave amplitude in V5 or V6 exceeds which threshold?

Explanation: The Sokolow-Lyon criteria diagnose LVH when the S wave depth in V1 plus the R wave amplitude in whichever of V5/V6 is taller totals more than 35 mm (7 large boxes).

3. An EKG shows right axis deviation, a tall R wave (taller than the S wave) in V1, and a dominant S wave in V6. This pattern is most consistent with:

Explanation: Right axis deviation with a tall R wave (R>S) in V1 and a dominant S wave in V6 is the classic EKG pattern of right ventricular hypertrophy, reflecting the electrically dominant, hypertrophied right ventricle.

17. Metabolic & Other EKG Manifestations

Hypothermia

  • Sinus bradycardia (the body "slows down")
  • J wave (Osborn wave): a distinctive abrupt notch/hump at the J point — ascent then plunge back to baseline. Different in appearance from the "tombstone" ST elevation of STEMI
  • Tremor artifact may mimic atrial fibrillation (patient shivering)
  • Treatment: rewarm the patient — the J wave resolves with rewarming

Hyperkalemia (Progressive Changes)

Peaked/tented T waves (earliest change) → widened QRS + flattened P waves → sine wave pattern → asystole (unless treated).

Hypokalemia

  • ST segment depression, flattened T wave, and a U wave (a small notch appearing after the T wave, before the next P wave)

Digoxin Effect

  • Characteristic scooped ST segment depression with flattened/inverted T wave — the "Salvador Dali mustache" appearance

Pericardial Effusion

Low QRS voltage + electrical alternans (beat-to-beat alternating QRS amplitude, best seen on the rhythm strip) together strongly suggest pericardial effusion — the heart is "swinging" within a fluid-filled pericardial sac.
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A hypothermic patient's EKG shows a distinctive notch at the J point with an abrupt ascent followed by a plunge back to baseline. What is this finding called, and what is the treatment?

Explanation: The J wave (Osborn wave) is a hallmark EKG finding of hypothermia, appearing as an abrupt notch at the J point. It resolves once the patient is rewarmed.

2. Which sequence correctly describes the progressive EKG changes of worsening hyperkalemia?

Explanation: Hyperkalemia progresses in a classic sequence: peaked/tented T waves appear earliest, followed by QRS widening and P wave flattening, then a sine wave pattern, and finally asystole if untreated.

3. An EKG shows low QRS voltage along with beat-to-beat alternation in QRS amplitude on the rhythm strip. This combination of findings is most suggestive of:

Explanation: Low QRS voltage combined with electrical alternans (alternating tall and short QRS complexes) strongly suggests a pericardial effusion, as the heart swings within the fluid-filled pericardial sac, alternately moving closer to and farther from the recording electrodes.
