1. General Appearance & Inspection

General Appearance

Before any hands-on exam, assess these general features:

  • Color: cyanotic (usually hypoxemia), pink/flushed (can suggest CO₂ toxicity or emphysema), or pallor (can indicate anemia)
  • Respiratory rate: over 20/min or under 10/min are both notable
  • Respiratory effort: is the patient hunched over, standing, or tripoding?
  • Accessory muscle use
  • Cough or wheeze
  • Clubbing or nicotine staining of the fingers
Section 1.1

Q1. A patient with a long smoking history presents with a pink, flushed complexion rather than cyanosis, despite significant respiratory distress. What should this coloring prompt consideration of?

A pink or flushed complexion (as opposed to cyanosis) can suggest CO2 toxicity or emphysema — sometimes described as the "pink puffer" phenotype in COPD, in contrast to cyanosis reflecting hypoxemia.
Section 1.1

Q2. A patient sits leaning forward with hands braced on their knees, using visible accessory muscles to breathe. What is this positioning called, and what does it suggest?

Tripoding — leaning forward with hands braced on the knees or another surface — is a posture patients adopt to optimize accessory muscle use and mechanics, signaling significant respiratory distress.

Inspection: Trachea & Chest Wall Deformities

Tracheal position can be displaced by a neck mass, pneumothorax, or a lung mass — always assess for symmetry when visualizing the patient.

Three classic deformities of the thorax:

Section 1.2

Q3. A 68-year-old with a 40-pack-year smoking history has a chest wall in which the lateral-to-AP diameter ratio is less than 2:1. Which deformity is this, and what is its underlying cause?

Barrel chest — with a lateral-to-AP diameter ratio under 2:1 — is caused by chronic air trapping in the alveoli, and has increased incidence with age and in COPD.
Section 1.2

Q4. A child is found to have a chest wall deformity with the sternum displaced anteriorly and an increased AP diameter, with the adjacent costal cartilages depressed. What is this deformity called?

Pigeon chest (pectus carinatum) is characterized by anterior sternal displacement with increased AP diameter and depression of the adjacent costal cartilages — the opposite deformity pattern from pectus excavatum.
Section 1.2

Q5. A patient has severe pectus excavatum. Beyond the cosmetic chest wall depression, what serious complication can this deformity cause in severe cases?

In severe cases, the depressed sternum in pectus excavatum can compress the heart and great vessels, which may produce audible murmurs.

2. Palpation, Auscultation & Pulmonary Anatomy

Palpation

Palpate while the patient breathes:

  • Rib tenderness: may indicate a rib fracture
  • Decreased motion with respiration (felt on one side or the other): usually indicates bronchial obstruction or pleural effusion (fluid in the lungs)
  • Subcutaneous emphysema: usually seen with pneumothorax
  • Tactile fremitus: helpful in diagnosing consolidation
Section 2.1

Q6. On palpation of the chest wall, a physician feels a crackling, bubble-like sensation under the skin. This finding is usually seen with which condition?

Subcutaneous emphysema — a crackling, bubble-like sensation under the skin from air tracking into subcutaneous tissue — is usually seen with pneumothorax.
Section 2.1

Q7. A physician notices decreased chest wall motion with respiration on the right side compared to the left. What two conditions should this finding raise concern for?

Decreased motion with respiration felt on one side usually indicates bronchial obstruction or pleural effusion (fluid in the lungs) on that side.

Auscultation & Pulmonary Anatomy

Auscultation provides the majority of information in the pulmonary exam. Being aware of the underlying anatomy beneath your stethoscope is essential to an accurate diagnosis — you are listening to moving air, and anything that alters the architecture of the pulmonary tree will alter that airflow.

Reference: the Bates' chart on pages 339–340 describes changes in pathology well and is worth reviewing separately.
  • Assess the volume and character of breath sounds
  • Percussion: is it resonant, flat, or dull?
  • Special tests: bronchophony, egophony, and whispered pectoriloquy — know what a positive/abnormal result means for each

Pulmonary Anatomy (Surface Landmarks)

Anteriorly, the apex of the lung extends just above the clavicle. On the right side:

  • Right upper lobe (RUL) is separated from the right middle lobe (RML) by the horizontal fissure
  • Right middle lobe (RML) is separated from the right lower lobe (RLL) by the oblique fissure

On the left side, there is a left upper lobe (LUL) and left lower lobe (LLL) (no middle lobe).

Posteriorly, the spinous process of T3 marks the level differentiating the right/left upper lobes from the right/left lower lobes.

On the sides: on the right, listen slightly inferiorly and anteriorly for the right middle lobe; on the left, focus on the left upper and left lower lobes (there is no left middle lobe to account for).

Pearl: the lobe diagrams referenced in lecture come from Bates' Guide to Physical Examination — worth pulling up directly alongside this table, since a diagram makes the fissure boundaries much easier to visualize than text alone.
Section 2.2

Q8. Which structure separates the right upper lobe from the right middle lobe?

The horizontal fissure separates the right upper lobe from the right middle lobe; the oblique fissure then separates the right middle lobe from the right lower lobe.
Section 2.2

Q9. A student is auscultating the posterior chest and needs to identify the level separating the upper from the lower lobes. Which landmark should they use?

Posteriorly, the spinous process of T3 marks the level differentiating the right and left upper lobes from the right and left lower lobes.
Section 2.2

Q10. Why is knowledge of the underlying pulmonary anatomy described as essential to accurate auscultation?

Since auscultation involves listening to moving air, and anything altering the pulmonary tree's architecture alters that airflow, knowing which lobe lies beneath your stethoscope at a given location is essential to correctly interpreting what you hear.

3. Pneumonia

Etiology & Diagnosis

Pneumonia can be bacterial (Streptococcus, Haemophilus, Legionella, among others), viral, or aspirational — and can be alveolar, lobar, or present with a pleural effusion.

Diagnosis

  • Chest x-ray
  • Blood cultures
  • Sputum gram stain/culture of lower tract secretion, strep urine antigen test
  • CBC (likely elevated white count) and CRP (likely elevated)
  • Chest CT if the patient appears severe or has a compromised immune system
Section 3.1

Q11. A patient with suspected pneumonia has a CBC and CRP drawn. What result pattern would be expected if the diagnosis is correct?

Pneumonia typically produces an elevated white count on CBC and an elevated CRP, reflecting the underlying infectious/inflammatory process.
Section 3.1

Q12. A patient with suspected pneumonia appears severely ill and is immunocompromised. Beyond the standard workup, which additional imaging study should be considered?

A chest CT should be considered if the patient with suspected pneumonia appears severe or is immunocompromised, providing more detail than a plain chest x-ray.

Exam Findings & Treatment

On physical exam, suspected pneumonia may show:

  • Positive bronchophony: transmitted voice sounds become louder, as air-filled spaces have become airless (replaced by fluid/consolidation)
  • Tactile fremitus will also be present (increased over consolidation)
Pearl: compare this to pleural effusion, where tactile fremitus is decreased because sound transmission is reduced trying to pass through fluid — the opposite direction from consolidation.

Treatment

Empiric antibiotics for community-acquired pneumonia. Penicillins are preferred; if the patient is penicillin-allergic, consider cephalosporins, clindamycin, doxycycline, a fluoroquinolone, or a macrolide.

Section 3.2

Q13. A patient with consolidative pneumonia has increased transmission of spoken voice sounds through the stethoscope, along with increased tactile fremitus. What explains these findings?

In pneumonia, air-filled alveolar spaces become airless as they fill with inflammatory fluid/consolidation, which paradoxically transmits sound and vibration (voice sounds, tactile fremitus) more effectively than normal aerated lung — producing positive bronchophony and increased fremitus.
Section 3.2

Q14. A patient with community-acquired pneumonia reports a penicillin allergy. Which of the following is an appropriate alternative antibiotic class?

For penicillin-allergic patients with community-acquired pneumonia, alternatives include cephalosporins, clindamycin, doxycycline, a fluoroquinolone, or a macrolide.

4. COPD

Pathophysiology

In COPD, alveoli are destroyed, resulting in an enlarged air space and hyperinflated lungs. It's caused by smoking, smoke, or noxious particles/gases inhaled long enough to cause inflammation and destruction of the airways. Patients usually have chronic bronchitis, sometimes with associated emphysema (and sometimes without).

Section 4.1

Q15. What is the underlying structural change in COPD that leads to hyperinflation of the lungs?

In COPD, the alveoli are destroyed, which enlarges the airspace and results in hyperinflated lungs — distinct from the fluid-filled consolidation seen in pneumonia.
Section 4.1

Q16. Which exposure is the classic underlying cause of COPD?

COPD is caused by chronic exposure to smoking, smoke, or other noxious particles/gases in the air over a long enough period to cause inflammation and destruction of the airways.

Treatment

Lifestyle: smoking cessation and pulmonary rehabilitation.

Medications: long-acting bronchodilators, inhaled steroids, long-acting anticholinergics, and sometimes supplemental oxygenation. Oral steroids and mucolytics (guaifenesin, large dose) can help with acute exacerbations.

Prevention: ensure patients are properly immunized, especially for influenza and pneumococcus.

Section 4.2

Q17. A COPD patient asks which single intervention would most improve their long-term disease course. What is the most appropriate answer?

Smoking cessation (along with pulmonary rehabilitation) is the key lifestyle intervention for COPD, since ongoing exposure to the causative noxious agent continues to drive airway inflammation and destruction.
Section 4.2

Q18. Which two vaccinations are specifically emphasized for COPD patients?

COPD patients should be properly immunized, especially for influenza and pneumococcus, given their increased vulnerability to respiratory infections.

5. Pneumothorax

Mechanism & Presentation

A pneumothorax is a tear in the pleural lining that acts as a one-way valve, admitting air into the pleural space from the lung but not letting it return. The tear can result from trauma, severe lung disease, or mechanical ventilation in a patient with underlying bad lung disease (it can also occur spontaneously).

This increases pressure against the lung, diaphragm, and surrounding structures (including the heart) — it is a positive pressure event on the affected side.

Exam Findings

  • The trachea shifts away from the affected side
  • Can cause compression of the vena cava and cardiorespiratory collapse
  • Patients may present hypotensive, hypoxic, with low levels of consciousness or even unconscious
  • Palpable subcutaneous emphysema (like small popping bubbles)
  • Absent lung sounds and significant asymmetry on percussion
  • Decreased bronchophony/egophony and tactile fremitus (air does not transmit sound/vibration the way consolidated lung does)
Section 5.1

Q19. In a tension pneumothorax, which direction does the trachea shift relative to the affected side, and why?

A pneumothorax's one-way valve mechanism admits air into the pleural space without letting it escape, creating a positive pressure event on the affected side that pushes the trachea and other mediastinal structures away from that side.
Section 5.1

Q20. A patient with a tension pneumothorax develops hypotension and cardiorespiratory collapse. What is the underlying mechanism for this deterioration?

The escalating positive pressure in a tension pneumothorax can compress the vena cava and surrounding structures (including the heart), impairing venous return and cardiac output, leading to hypotension and cardiorespiratory collapse.
Section 5.1

Q21. On exam, a patient with suspected pneumothorax has absent breath sounds and marked asymmetry on percussion of the affected side. What would you expect to find regarding tactile fremitus on that side?

In pneumothorax, air in the pleural space (rather than consolidated, fluid-filled lung) transmits sound and vibration poorly, so tactile fremitus (like bronchophony/egophony) is decreased on the affected side.

Management

Emergent need for high-flow oxygenation and decompression with either a needle or thoracostomy tube insertion.

Patient counseling: patients should avoid high altitude until well healed, and diving is contraindicated if a spontaneous pneumothorax has not resolved.
Section 5.2

Q22. A patient recovering from a recently resolved spontaneous pneumothorax asks if they can go scuba diving on an upcoming vacation. What is the appropriate counseling?

Diving is contraindicated if a spontaneous pneumothorax has not resolved, given the risk of recurrence or complication with pressure changes underwater. Patients should also avoid high altitude until well healed.

6. Pleural Effusion

A pleural effusion is fluid in the pleural space, where fluid should not normally be. If purulent, it's called an empyema, suggesting an infectious cause.

Common causes: heart failure, pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, malignancy.

Management: the fluid can be removed via thoracentesis and then tested/cultured to determine the cause. Treat the underlying cause; consider pleurodesis for recurrence.

Pearl (contrast with pneumonia): tactile fremitus is decreased over a pleural effusion — sound transmission is reduced trying to pass through the fluid — the opposite pattern from the consolidation of pneumonia, where fremitus is increased.
Section 6

Q23. A pleural fluid sample is found to be purulent on thoracentesis. What term describes this finding, and what does it suggest?

A purulent pleural effusion is called an empyema, suggesting an infectious cause.
Section 6

Q24. A patient has a recurrent pleural effusion despite treating the underlying cause. Which procedure can be considered to prevent further recurrence?

Pleurodesis (deliberately inducing adhesion between the pleural layers) can be considered for recurrent pleural effusions, beyond simply removing fluid via thoracentesis.
Section 6

Q25. A patient with a pleural effusion has decreased tactile fremitus over the affected area. How does this compare to the fremitus pattern expected in lobar pneumonia?

Pneumonia's consolidation transmits sound/vibration more effectively than normal aerated lung, increasing fremitus, while a pleural effusion's fluid layer dampens transmission, decreasing fremitus — opposite patterns for two different causes of dullness to percussion.

7. Croup & Bronchiolitis

Croup

Croup produces a characteristic barking sound from swelling and narrowing of the trachea and bronchial tree. It's more common in children than adults.

Potential causes: parainfluenza, influenza, adenovirus, RSV, and other viruses — it is very rare for croup to be associated with a bacterial infection.

Treatment: dexamethasone, racemic epinephrine, and supportive care (hydration, sometimes oxygenation).

Section 7.1

Q26. A 2-year-old presents with a characteristic barking cough. Which category of pathogen is by far the most likely cause?

Croup is usually caused by viruses — parainfluenza, influenza, adenovirus, RSV, and others — and it is very rare for croup to be associated with a bacterial infection.
Section 7.1

Q27. Which two treatments are specifically named for managing croup?

Croup is treated with dexamethasone, racemic epinephrine, and supportive care (hydration, sometimes oxygenation) — not antibiotics, since it's very rarely bacterial.

Bronchiolitis

Bronchiolitis is characterized by inflammation and congestion of the bronchioles, more commonly than not due to RSV. It creates hyperinflation, with the hallmark of bronchial inflammation near the hilum.

Labs and chest x-ray are not routinely recommended. If obtained, the x-ray classically shows a generalized prominence of the bronchial/vascular markings in a perihilar distribution, without consolidation and without lung collapse.

Treatment: depending on stability, some patients require admission for supportive care. Steroids are usually not recommended; a trial of albuterol may be considered if the presentation is very severe.

Section 7.2

Q28. Which pathogen is most commonly responsible for bronchiolitis?

Bronchiolitis is, more commonly than not, caused by RSV (respiratory syncytial virus).
Section 7.2

Q29. A chest x-ray is obtained in an infant with suspected bronchiolitis. Which finding pattern would be expected, and which findings would argue against the diagnosis?

Bronchiolitis classically shows generalized prominence of bronchial/vascular markings in a perihilar distribution, without consolidation or lung collapse — though labs and CXR are not routinely recommended for typical presentations.
Section 7.2

Q30. Which statement best reflects the recommended medication approach to bronchiolitis?

Steroids are not routinely recommended for bronchiolitis; a trial of albuterol may be considered only if the presentation is very severe, and treatment is otherwise largely supportive.
