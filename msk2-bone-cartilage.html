1. Bone Composition & Remodeling Basics

Bone is a dynamic, living tissue, not a static structure — it is constantly being resorbed and re-formed throughout life. Three cell types drive this: osteoblasts (form bone, lay down osteoid), osteocytes (maintain bone), and osteoclasts (resorb bone).

Bone is composed of an organic matrix (osteoid, mostly type I collagen, laid down by osteoblasts) and an inorganic mineral phase (calcium hydroxyapatite). Bone stores about 99% of the body's calcium, 85% of its phosphorus, and 65% of its sodium.

Woven vs. Lamellar Bone

Normal (lamellar) bone shows an organized, layered architecture. Woven bone is disorganized and irregular, without visible layering — this is the type of bone seen in a healing fracture callus and in several pathologic states (e.g., Paget disease) where bone is laid down quickly and haphazardly.

Three Features of Bone Remodeling

Key concepts — remodeling is described by three parameters
  • Turnover — the balance of resorption rate vs. formation rate (ratio of osteoclast to osteoblast activity)
  • Mineralization — how well osteoid becomes calcified; depends on vitamin D status and is impaired by acidosis
  • Volume — affected by age, sex, genetics, and nutrition

Bone also adapts its structure to mechanical stress over time (e.g., cranial muscle-marking changes with age/activity level). Acidic environments leach mineral from bone (a principle used at autopsy to decalcify bone for sectioning).

Practice Question

Which of the following best distinguishes woven bone from normal lamellar bone on histology?

Woven bone is disorganized, lacking the layered architecture of lamellar bone
Woven bone is found only in the epiphysis of long bones
Woven bone contains no osteoclasts or osteoblasts
Woven bone is mineralized but architecturally disorganized (no lamellar layering) — it is the bone type seen in fracture callus and rapid bone turnover states like Paget disease.

2. Vitamin D & Calcium-Phosphate Homeostasis

A useful framework: vitamin D's job is to mineralize bone, so it raises both calcium and phosphate. PTH's job is to protect plasma calcium, so it raises calcium but lowers phosphate. The two hormones work synergistically on calcium but oppositely on phosphate.

Vitamin D Synthesis Pathway

Steps in active vitamin D production
StepProcess
17-dehydrocholesterol → cholecalciferol (vitamin D3) via UV light in skin (~90%) or absorbed from diet (~10%)
2Cholecalciferol → 25-hydroxyvitamin D via 25-hydroxylase in the liver
325-hydroxyvitamin D → 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (active form) via 1α-hydroxylase in the kidney

Hypercalcemia stimulates 1α-hydroxylase (via calcitonin); hypophosphatemia also activates 1α-hydroxylase, since active vitamin D is needed to reclaim phosphate.

Functions of Active Vitamin D

  • Stimulates intestinal absorption of calcium
  • Stimulates renal distal tubule reabsorption of calcium and phosphate
  • In hypocalcemia, increases bone resorption; in normocalcemia, promotes calcium deposition into bone
  • Enhances mineralization by stimulating osteoblasts to produce osteocalcin, a calcium-binding protein
Best lab test25-hydroxyvitamin D is the most accurate/stable marker of overall vitamin D supply (measured clinically rather than the active 1,25-dihydroxy form, which has a much shorter half-life).

3. PTH & the RANK/RANKL/OPG Axis

PTH is released in response to hypocalcemia. Its net effect is to raise serum calcium at the expense of phosphate:

  • Activates renal 1α-hydroxylase → increases active vitamin D
  • Mobilizes calcium from bone (via osteoclast activation, indirectly through osteoblasts)
  • Decreases renal calcium excretion, but increases renal phosphate excretion

RANK / RANK-Ligand / Osteoprotegerin (OPG)

The core resorption pathway
  • RANK (Receptor Activator of Nuclear factor Kappa-B) sits on osteoclasts (and precursors)
  • RANK-ligand (RANKL) is expressed on osteoblasts; binding RANK activates osteoclasts → bone resorption
  • Osteoprotegerin (OPG) is produced by osteoblasts and acts as a decoy/competitive inhibitor of RANKL — it blocks the RANK–RANKL interaction and thereby suppresses resorption

This axis explains postmenopausal bone loss: falling estrogen levels reduce OPG production, removing the competitive brake on RANK–RANKL binding, so osteoclast activation (and bone resorption) proceeds unopposed.

Practice Question

A researcher develops an antibody that binds to and promotes the function of osteoprotegerin. Long-term administration to a patient would most likely produce a clinical picture resembling which disease?

Paget disease of bone
Osteopetrosis
Osteomalacia
Hyperparathyroidism
Boosting OPG function would more strongly inhibit RANK-RANKL binding, suppressing osteoclast activation. Reduced bone resorption with continued formation mimics osteopetrosis (dense, "marble" bone from defective osteoclast-mediated resorption).

4. Congenital Bone Disorders: Overview & Terminology

Congenital bone disease arises from abnormal migration/condensation of cells (dysostosis) affecting one bone or a localized group, or from defects intrinsic to bone/cartilage growth and maintenance (dysplasia, affecting the skeletal system more broadly).

Vocabulary
  • Dysostosis — localized abnormality from abnormal cell migration/condensation (affects one bone or a regional group)
  • Aplasia — absence of bone
  • Dysplasia — a mutation that interferes with bone or cartilage growth/maintenance; osteodysplasia if bone, chondrodysplasia if cartilage
Important distinction from other organ systemsIn adults, "dysplasia" (e.g., cervical dysplasia, GI dysplasia) usually implies a pre-neoplastic process. Skeletal dysplasias in children are not pre-neoplastic — the term simply describes abnormal formation.

5. Osteogenesis Imperfecta

Also called brittle bone disease. Caused by a defect in the synthesis of type I collagen, resulting in too little (and structurally poor) bone formation. Most forms behave as a dominant-negative mutation — a defect in one portion of the collagen chain disrupts the entire triple helix. Overall incidence is about 1 in 20,000.

Osteogenesis imperfecta subtypes
TypeInheritanceKey features
Type I (classic, most common)Autosomal dominantFractures after birth; blue sclera (type I collagen is a normal scleral component); progressive hearing loss (fragile ossicles)
Type IIAutosomal recessiveNeonatal fractures at birth; frequently stillborn
Type IIIAD or ARProgressive/deforming; ~2/3 of newborns have fractures; can be stillborn
Type IVAutosomal dominantSclera and teeth can be normal or abnormal; can be mistaken for child abuse (lacks the classic blue sclera)

Microscopically, cartilage and a fracture line with fibrin and disorganized (undermineralized) calcification can be seen — the cartilage model is present, but mineralization is inadequate.

Practice Question

A 15-year-old competitive athlete has a history of multiple fractures (tibia, radius, humerus). Exam notes blue sclera and hearing below normal for age. Which is the most likely diagnosis?

Osteogenesis imperfecta type II
Osteogenesis imperfecta type I

Recurrent fractures with blue sclera and hearing loss in an otherwise ambulatory patient is the classic presentation of OI type I. Types II and III typically present perinatally with numerous fractures and are often lethal.

6. Achondroplasia & Thanatophoric Dysplasia

Both result from activating mutations in fibroblast growth factor receptor 3 (FGFR3). FGFR3 normally inhibits growth-plate chondrocytes; an activating mutation over-suppresses growth plate cartilage proliferation, impairing endochondral (long bone) growth, while membranous ossification (e.g., the cranial vault) is largely unaffected.

Achondroplasia

  • The most common form of dwarfism; usually autosomal dominant, frequently a spontaneous (new) mutation
  • Short stature from shortened proximal (rhizomelic) extremities; normal-length trunk
  • Frontal bossing with normal parietal bossing (forehead prominent because cranium forms relatively normally via membranous ossification)
  • Normal intelligence and normal life expectancy in most cases

Thanatophoric Dysplasia

  • Same gene (FGFR3) — lethal form
  • Extreme shortening of the limbs, severe frontal bossing, "cloverleaf" skull
  • Very small thorax → fatal respiratory failure

7. Cleidocranial Dysplasia

"Cleido" = Greek for clavicle. Often sporadic, autosomal dominant.

  • Small thorax with partial or complete aplasia of the clavicles (can sometimes fold the shoulders together in the midline)
  • Late eruption of permanent teeth with numerous supernumerary (extra) teeth
  • Open fontanelles, wormian bones (extra small ossicles within cranial sutures)
  • Long second metacarpal
Test-taking pearlRecognizing clavicle involvement alone is usually enough to identify this diagnosis on an exam — the disorder's name itself flags the two systems involved (clavicle + cranium).

8. Osteopetrosis

"Marble bone disease" — defective bone resorption by osteoclasts, either from impaired ability to create the acidic resorption pit or from a lack of functional osteoclasts. Despite having more bone than normal, the bone is not properly remodeled and is therefore paradoxically weak and brittle ("breaks like chalk").

  • Autosomal recessive forms tend to be more severe; autosomal dominant forms (Albers-Schönberg disease) tend to be milder
  • Fractures despite increased bone density
  • Overgrowth of bone can impinge on cranial nerve foramina → cranial nerve deficits
  • Bone overgrowth can obliterate the medullary cavity → marrow failure, increasing risk of infection, anemia, and bleeding
Molecular mechanisms
  • Mutations in carbonic anhydrase II (generates protons from CO2 + H2O needed for the acidic resorption environment)
  • Mutations in CLC-7 (a chloride/proton pump on the osteoclast surface needed to acidify the resorption pit)

9. Osteoporosis

An acquired reduction in bone mass; the bone that is present is qualitatively normal (normal mineralization, normal microscopic appearance) — there is simply not enough of it. Can be localized (e.g., disuse osteoporosis from prolonged immobilization) or generalized.

Osteopenia vs. osteoporosis Both terms describe decreased bone mass. Osteoporosis specifically means the decrease is severe enough to significantly increase fracture risk; a patient can have osteopenia without yet meeting that threshold.

Primary vs. Secondary Causes

Primary: postmenopausal/age-related (by far the most common).

Secondary causes (know the categories, not an exhaustive list): endocrine disorders (hyperparathyroidism, hyperthyroidism, pituitary tumors, diabetes mellitus, Addison disease, Cushing syndrome), vitamin C/D deficiency, malnutrition/malabsorption, corticosteroids, chemotherapy, alcohol use, immobilization, anemia, paralysis, and neoplasia.

Peak Bone Mass & Aging

Peak bone mass is determined by genetics, physical activity, and nutrition during growth — the higher the peak, the longer the delay before osteoporosis develops. With aging: decreased replication of osteoprogenitor cells, decreased osteoblast synthetic activity, decreased matrix growth factors, decreased physical activity (less mechanical stress signaling for remodeling), and (in women) decreased estrogen → decreased OPG → unopposed RANK-RANKL signaling.

Diagnosis & Complications

  • Plain radiographs require 30–40% bone loss before osteoporosis becomes visible — the diagnostic standard is dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DEXA)
  • Vertebral compression fractures → kyphoscoliosis, lumbar lordosis
  • Hip fractures; rib fractures (painful breathing → poor inspiration → pneumonia risk); reduced mobility → DVT risk
Practice Question

A 67-year-old woman is admitted for cough, fever, and dyspnea and diagnosed with pneumonia. Chest x-ray incidentally shows healing callus on six right-sided ribs and two thoracic vertebral compression fractures. She has no family history of fracture and an adequate diet; her only past medical history is hypertension. What is the most likely underlying diagnosis?

Osteogenesis imperfecta type IV
Primary (postmenopausal/age-related) osteoporosis
Paget disease of bone
Osteomalacia
Older postmenopausal age, no family history, and adequate nutrition point to primary osteoporosis. The rib and vertebral compression fractures are classic complications, and the rib fractures explain the subsequent pneumonia (splinted, shallow breathing).

10. Paget Disease of Bone

Also called osteitis deformans. A possible (theorized) paramyxovirus-related etiology has been proposed, suggesting a component of chronic inflammation. Characterized by excessive, disorganized bone production — bone is thicker but structurally unsound and prone to fracture.

Phases

  1. Osteolytic phase — osteoclastic bone resorption predominates
  2. Mixed phase — combined osteoclastic resorption and osteoblastic formation
  3. Osteosclerotic phase — net bone formation predominates, with elevated alkaline phosphatase
Classic histology A mosaic ("jigsaw puzzle") pattern — thickened bony trabeculae laid down in a disorganized fashion, packed tightly against one another. This is one of the most recognizable histologic patterns in bone pathology.

Occurs typically in middle-to-late adulthood; affects roughly 2.5% of the US population but is rare in Scandinavian, Chinese, and Japanese populations (suggesting a genetic component). Can be monostotic or polyostotic; the skull, pelvis, and femur are commonly involved.

Complications

  • High-output cardiac failure (from increased arteriovenous shunting through abnormal bone vasculature)
  • Cranial nerve impingement (bone overgrowth around foramina)
  • Back pain from vertebral column overgrowth
  • Secondary osteosarcoma — an important, testable association

11. Rickets & Osteomalacia (Vitamin D Deficiency)

Both result from vitamin D deficiency and failure to mineralize osteoid ("malacia" = bad/soft). Rickets occurs in children (before growth plate closure); osteomalacia occurs in adults (after growth plate closure).

Causes: reduced UV light exposure, and/or dietary calcium or vitamin D deficiency. Since hypocalcemia stimulates vitamin D production and both hormones promote calcium retention (but have opposing effects on phosphate), deficiency of either substrate impairs mineralization. 25-hydroxyvitamin D level is the best test for vitamin D supply.

12. Hyperparathyroidism, Brown Tumor & Renal Osteodystrophy

Skeletal Effects of Excess PTH

  • Increases RANKL expression on osteoblasts → osteoclast activation → bone resorption
  • Increases renal calcium reabsorption; increases urinary phosphate excretion
  • Increases active vitamin D production
Primary, secondary, tertiary
  • Primary: parathyroid adenoma (most common single-gland cause) or hyperplasia
  • Secondary: a chronic stimulus drives PTH up — most commonly chronic renal failure, where impaired renal calcium handling and active vitamin D production drive compensatory PTH release
  • Tertiary: after prolonged secondary stimulation, parathyroid tissue becomes autonomous and continues excess PTH secretion even after the original stimulus is corrected

Brown Tumor

A collection of osteoclasts (grossly brown from hemorrhage) that forms where excess PTH has driven focal bone resorption; can be cystic. The overall skeletal picture of cystic, resorptive lesions with fibrous replacement is called osteitis fibrosa cystica. Microscopically: increased osteoclast numbers with decreased overall bone.

Renal Osteodystrophy

The umbrella term for the alteration of bone morphology seen in chronic kidney disease. It is classified along three axes (the same three parameters from bone remodeling basics):

  • Turnover — low, normal, or high
  • Mineralization — normal or abnormal
  • Volume — low, normal, or high

The underlying concept: chronic kidney disease disrupts calcium/phosphate/vitamin D handling and secondarily drives PTH abnormalities, producing a spectrum of bone findings rather than one single pattern.

13. Fracture Classification & Terminology

Fractures are one of the most common pathologic conditions of bone, with over 100 named terms in use (many eponymous). A useful general framework describes any fracture with five descriptors:

Five-part fracture description framework
DescriptorCategories
ExtentComplete (through the entire thickness/width of the bone) vs. incomplete
Integrity of overlying skinClosed (skin intact; formerly "simple") vs. open (skin breached; formerly "compound")
DirectionTransverse (straight across), oblique (angled), spiral (spirals around the shaft)
LocationNamed by the specific bone/segment involved (e.g., diaphyseal, metaphyseal)

Practice Question

A rock climber falls and fractures the left radius; the skin is intact. X-ray shows a single spiral fracture line traversing the entire width of the bone, with no fragmentation and the fracture ends in alignment. How is this fracture best characterized?

Closed, incomplete, spiral, non-displaced
Closed, complete, spiral, non-displaced
Open, complete, oblique, displaced
Closed, complete, comminuted, non-displaced
Skin intact = closed; traversing the entire width of the bone = complete (not incomplete); the description states a spiral orientation with no fragmentation (rules out comminuted) and ends in alignment (non-displaced).

14. Bone Biomechanics & Fracture Patterns

Forces on Bone

  • Compression (pushing), tension (stretching), shear (forces moving in opposite directions), and combinations (bending, rotation)
  • When a long bone is struck from one side, that side is placed under compression while the opposite side experiences tension first — fractures often begin at the site of trauma or an adjacent location under the greatest tensile stress, not necessarily directly at the point of impact

Elastic vs. Plastic Deformation

Bone is viscoelastic — part glass-like (brittle) and part rubber-like (pliable). It can bend roughly 13% and spring back (elastic deformation), or bend further without fracturing and not return to its original shape (plastic deformation). Which behavior dominates depends on how quickly the force is applied: slowly applied force allows bending/plastic deformation; rapidly applied high-energy force (e.g., gunshot) causes brittle, glass-like fracture without bending.

Forensic pearlNot all spiral fractures in children indicate abuse — many "pathognomonic" abuse indicators from older literature have turned out not to be specific. Context of the reported mechanism matters as much as the fracture pattern itself.

Pathologic Fracture

A fracture occurring at the site of a pre-existing disease process (tumor, Paget disease, a brown tumor from hyperparathyroidism, etc.). Interestingly, fractures purely attributable to primary (age-related) osteoporosis are conventionally not labeled "pathologic fractures" in most usage, likely because osteoporosis is treated as an expected consequence of aging rather than a distinct disease process.

15. Fracture Healing, Complications & Forensic Pearls

Steps of Fracture Repair

  1. Hemorrhage and fibrin deposition at the fracture site, triggering inflammation
  2. Soft callus — a cartilage model is laid down (similar to endochondral ossification)
  3. Hard callus — the cartilage model mineralizes and is bridged by woven bone
  4. Remodeling — woven bone is gradually reorganized into normal lamellar bone

Radiologic and pathologic appearance can give a rough time course of a fracture, but overlapping/re-injured fractures make this imprecise — interpret cautiously.

Factors That Impair Healing

  • Displaced or comminuted fractures (fragments not end-to-end, requiring more remodeling)
  • Inadequate immobilization — continued motion/strain can prevent proper union and produce a pseudoarthrosis ("false joint" forming at the non-union site)
  • Infection, particularly with open or comminuted fractures
  • Nutritional deficiencies (calcium, phosphate, vitamins C and D)
  • Diabetes and vascular disease (e.g., atherosclerosis) — impaired blood flow to the fracture site
Interesting exceptionPseudoarthrosis is uncommonly seen in rib fractures despite ribs constantly moving with respiration — possibly because chest wall musculature helps keep the fracture ends relatively aligned.

Forensic Pathology Pearl: Manner of Death

If a person falls and fractures a hip, and subsequently dies (even from a downstream complication such as pneumonia or inability to wean from a ventilator), the manner of death is accident, not natural — because the fall/fracture set the fatal chain of events in motion, even though the immediate cause of death may be something else (e.g., bronchopneumonia). These cases should be reported to the coroner/medical examiner and are commonly under-referred.

Practice Question

A 73-year-old woman with hypertension, diabetes, and osteoporosis falls down stairs and fractures her femoral neck. Hip repair is uncomplicated, but she cannot be weaned from the ventilator and dies of bronchopneumonia. What is the correct manner of death?

Natural
Accident
Could not be determined

The fall and resulting fracture initiated the sequence of events leading to death, even though the immediate cause of death (bronchopneumonia) occurred later. This makes the manner of death accident, not natural.

16. Osteonecrosis (Avascular Necrosis)

Ischemic death of bone, also called aseptic or avascular necrosis. Distinct from the focal necrosis that can accompany other bone diseases (e.g., localized necrosis in osteomyelitis) — osteonecrosis is its own specific disease process, most classically affecting the femoral head.

Mechanisms (Usually Acting Together)

  • Obstruction of the vascular lumen (fat emboli, sickled red cells, gas bubbles in decompression/Caisson disease)
  • Increased intramedullary/bone marrow pressure
  • Direct blood vessel damage (trauma)

Whether ischemia is reversible or irreversible depends on its duration.

Subchondral vs. Medullary Infarcts

LocationPresentation
SubchondralUsually painful (periosteum is involved) and can lead to collapse of the articular surface, since the cortex here is thinner/subarticular
MedullaryUsually silent; bone remains relatively stable because the load-bearing outer cortex is spared even if the marrow cavity infarcts

Risk Factors

Corticosteroid use, idiopathic, systemic lupus erythematosus/antiphospholipid antibody syndrome, sickle cell disease, Caisson (decompression) disease, trauma, and fat embolism (e.g., following long bone fracture).

Named Forms

  • Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease — avascular necrosis of the femoral head in children
  • Bisphosphonate-related osteonecrosis of the jaw

Osteonecrosis of the Femoral Head

Roughly 20,000–30,000 cases per year in the US, accounting for about 10% of hip replacements — a surprisingly large share given it typically affects young, active patients (commonly in their 20s–40s) rather than the typical osteoarthritis population.

Practice Question

Six days after a closed, non-displaced femur fracture from a skiing accident, a 44-year-old woman develops confusion, a petechial rash, and dyspnea. What long-term musculoskeletal complication is she now at increased risk for?

Osteomyelitis of the fracture site
Avascular necrosis of the femoral head
Paget disease of bone
Pseudoarthrosis
The delayed confusion, petechial rash, and dyspnea after a long bone fracture describe fat embolism syndrome. Fat emboli can obstruct the microvasculature of bone, predisposing to avascular necrosis, classically of the femoral head.

17. Osteomyelitis

Inflammation of bone ("osteo") and marrow ("myelo"), most commonly infectious. Bacteria reach bone by three routes: exogenous (open fracture, trauma, surgery), hematogenous spread (from bacteremia), or contiguous spread (from an adjacent infected joint or soft tissue).

Acute vs. Chronic

Defining feature: presence of dead bone
  • Acute: neutrophilic infiltrate; typically follows hematogenous seeding (especially in children); usually presents suddenly and does not necessarily progress to chronic disease
  • Chronic: defined pathologically by the presence of dead (necrotic) bone; usually results from open/comminuted fractures or continuous local spread (e.g., non-healing diabetic foot ulcers); tends to be an insidious, low-level process rather than a sudden presentation

Children vs. Adults

ChildrenAdults
Typical courseAcute, hematogenousChronic more common; hematogenous spread is rare because growth plates are closed
Typical siteMetaphysis (highly vascular, near the growth plate) — femur, tibia, fibula most commonVertebrae (when acute/hematogenous); otherwise wherever contiguous spread or vascular disease occurs
PresentationPain, limp, fever, swelling, erythema (classic acute inflammatory picture)Chronic pain, sinus tract, poor wound healing, may have fever/elevated CRP if acute
Most common organismStaphylococcus aureusS. aureus most common overall; S. epidermidis with contiguous spread from skin; Pseudomonas/E. coli in specific settings; consider fungal/mycobacterial if immunocompromised
Classic associationSalmonella osteomyelitis is strongly associated with sickle cell disease.

Risk Factors

Lymphedema, venous stasis/insufficiency, impaired circulation (small vessel disease from hypertension, diabetes, neuropathy), malnutrition, renal or liver failure, immune compromise, neoplasia, and substance abuse — essentially, anything that impairs local blood flow, drainage, or host defense.

Pathologic Findings

  • Sequestrum — a fragment of necrotic bone that becomes "sequestered" away from viable tissue
  • Involucrum — a shell of new bone growth that forms around the sequestrum
  • Sinus tract — a chronic draining connection between the site of bone inflammation and the skin surface

Complications

Pathologic fracture, amyloidosis (chronic inflammatory state), endocarditis (from seeding bacteremia), sepsis, and (rarely) squamous cell carcinoma arising within a chronic sinus tract or secondary osteosarcoma at the infection site.

Specific Forms

  • Tuberculous osteomyelitis — about 1–3% of TB cases; favors long bones and vertebrae; vertebral TB is called Pott disease
Practice Question

A 12-year-old girl develops progressive left leg pain and a limp over three days, with fever (101°F), and swelling/erythema over the affected area on exam. What is the most likely diagnosis?

Chronic osteomyelitis with sequestrum formation
Acute (hematogenous) osteomyelitis
Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease
Ewing sarcoma
Rapid onset of fever, limp, localized swelling, and erythema in a child is classic for acute hematogenous osteomyelitis, typically seeding the highly vascular metaphysis. Sequestrum formation is a feature of chronic, not acute, disease.

18. Syphilitic Bone Disease

Syphilis can involve bone directly, producing a layer of new periosteal bone on top of existing cortex (periostitis). This is now uncommonly seen clinically because most cases are treated, but classic findings remain testable.

Congenital Syphilis: Characteristic Features

  • Hutchinson teeth — notched central incisors
  • Mulberry molars — molars with a berry-like, malformed cusp pattern
  • Saddle nose deformity — from destructive nasal cartilage/bone inflammation
  • Saber shins — anterior bowing of the tibia

Tertiary Syphilis

  • Periostitis can affect the skull, shoulder, and long bones, causing bone pain
  • Gumma — a focus of central necrosis surrounded by a lymphocytic and epithelioid cell infiltrate; can occur in bone as part of tertiary disease
