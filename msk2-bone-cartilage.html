SECTION 1

Bone Composition & Remodeling Basics

Bone is a dynamic, living tissue, not a static structure — it is constantly being resorbed and re-formed throughout life.

Components of Bone

OrganicInorganic
Osteoblasts, osteocytes, osteoclasts; osteoid (protein matrix)Calcium hydroxyapatite

Bone stores 99% of the body's calcium, 85% of its phosphorus, 65% of its sodium, and 65% of its magnesium.

Framework for the whole unitVitamin D's function is to promote mineralization of new bone; PTH's function is to maintain the plasma concentration of calcium.

Woven vs. Lamellar Bone

Normal (lamellar) bone shows an organized, layered architecture. Woven bone is disorganized and irregular, without visible layering — this is the type of bone seen in a healing fracture callus and in several pathologic states (e.g., Paget disease) where bone is laid down quickly and haphazardly.

Three Features of Bone Remodeling

Key concepts — remodeling is described by three parameters
  • Turnover — determined by resorption rate vs. formation rate; affected by osteoclast/osteoblast activity, hormones, and mechanical stimuli
  • Mineralization — how well collagen becomes ossified; affected by vitamin D concentration, mineral deficiency, acidosis, and aluminum toxicity
  • Volume — affected by age, sex, genetic factors, and nutrition

Bone also adapts its structure to mechanical stress over time. An incidental autopsy finding illustrating this kind of structural change is hyperostosis frontalis — a benign, wrinkly overgrowth of bone on the inner surface of the frontal bone, most often seen in middle-aged and older women.

SECTION 2

Vitamin D & Calcium-Phosphate Homeostasis

Vitamin D Synthesis Pathway

Steps in active vitamin D production
StepProcess
17-dehydrocholesterol → cholecalciferol (vitamin D3) via UV light in skin (~90%) or absorbed from diet (~10%)
2Cholecalciferol → 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25-OH-D) via 25-hydroxylase in the liver
325-OH-D → 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (active form) via α1-hydroxylase in the kidney

Hypocalcemia stimulates PTH, and PTH stimulates α1-hydroxylase; hypophosphatemia also directly activates α1-hydroxylase. (Note: α1-hydroxylase and 1α-hydroxylase are used interchangeably in the literature for the same enzyme.)

Functions of Active Vitamin D

  • Stimulates intestinal absorption of calcium
  • Stimulates calcium and phosphorus resorption in the renal distal tubules
  • Works together with PTH: if hypocalcemic, increases resorption from bone; if normocalcemic, promotes calcium deposition in bone
  • Enhances mineralization of bone by stimulating osteoblasts to produce the calcium-binding protein osteocalcin, causing calcium deposition
Overall flow (diet/skin → bone)
  • 7-dehydrocholesterol (skin) + diet → cholecalciferol (vitamin D) → liver → 25-hydroxycholecalciferol → kidney (via α1-hydroxylase) → 1,25-hydroxycholecalciferol
  • Active vitamin D acts on the intestine (↑ absorption of calcium and phosphate) and the kidney (↑ reabsorption of calcium and phosphate)
  • ↓ calcium, ↓ phosphate, and ↑ PTH all drive this pathway forward
  • At the level of bone, this axis promotes osteoclast activity as part of turning "old bone to new bone"
Best lab testTesting for 25-hydroxyvitamin D most accurately reflects the body's overall vitamin D supply (rather than the active 1,25-dihydroxy form, which has a much shorter half-life).

SECTION 3

PTH & the RANK/RANKL/OPG Axis

PTH Hormone

Production is stimulated by hypocalcemia. Functions:

  • Activates α1-hydroxylase, increasing the amount of active vitamin D
  • Mobilizes calcium from bone
  • Decreases renal calcium excretion
  • Increases renal excretion of phosphate

Under normal conditions, increased blood calcium inhibits PTH secretion (negative feedback).

RANK / RANK-Ligand / Osteoprotegerin (OPG)

The core resorption pathway
  • RANK (Receptor Activator of Nuclear factor Kappa-B) — on the cell membrane of osteoclasts and osteoclast precursors; leads to stimulation of osteoclast formation, function, differentiation, and survival
  • RANKL (RANK-ligand) — on the cell membrane of osteoblasts and marrow stromal cells; binds RANK, activating NF-κβ and the genes for osteoclast differentiation, function, and survival
  • OPG (osteoprotegerin) — produced by osteoblasts/stromal cells and other cells; binds RANK and competitively inhibits RANKL, blocking it from binding and thereby suppressing osteoclast activation

This axis explains postmenopausal bone loss: falling estrogen levels increase IL-1, IL-6, and TNF, which stimulate RANK–RANKL signaling and suppress OPG — promoting bone breakdown.

SECTION 4

Congenital Bone Disorders: Overview & Terminology

Two broad categories
  • Abnormal migration
    • Dysostoses (condensations) — affect one bone or a group of bones
    • Aplasia — absence of bone
    • Extra bones
    • Abnormal fusions
  • Mutations interfering with bone or cartilage growth/maintenance
    • Osteodysplasia (bone) / chondrodysplasia (cartilage)
Important distinction from other organ systemsThis is not like dysplasia of the cervix or colon. In adults, "dysplasia" in those settings usually implies a pre-neoplastic process. Skeletal dysplasias are not pre-neoplastic — the term simply describes abnormal formation.

Two illustrative dysostosis examples from autopsy material: a bifid rib (abnormal division of a single rib) and aplasia of a single transverse process of a vertebra — both localized findings affecting only one bone, without a broader systemic pattern.

SECTION 5

Osteogenesis Imperfecta

Also called brittle bone disease. Defect in the synthesis of type I collagen — results in too little amount of bone. This is a dominant-negative mutation: a defect in a portion of the collagen chain affects the entire chain. Overall incidence is 1/20,000; Type I is the most common.

Osteogenesis imperfecta — four types
TypeInheritanceKey features
Type IAutosomal dominantFractures, blue sclerae, presenile deafness
Type IIAutosomal recessiveNewborns present with neonatal fractures
Type IIIAD or ARTwo-thirds of newborns have fractures
Type IVAutosomal dominantCan have either normal teeth or dentinogenesis imperfecta; normal sclerae; can be confused for child abuse

Microscopically, a fracture site shows cartilage with calcification present but disorganized/inadequate mineralization — the cartilage model is present, but mineralization is inadequate.

SECTION 6

Achondroplasia & Thanatophoric Dysplasia

Both result from mutations in fibroblast growth factor receptor 3 (FGFR3), which normally inhibits growth-plate chondrocytes.

Achondroplasia

  • Basic description: no growth of cartilage; the most common form of dwarfism
  • Activating mutation in FGFR3 — FGFR3 normally inhibits growth plate chondrocytes, so growth of the epiphyseal plates is suppressed and the length of long bones is decreased
  • Autosomal dominant; many cases are spontaneous mutations
  • Affects bones formed by endochondral ossification (mostly long bones; not cranial bones)
  • Features: short stature; shortening of proximal extremities; frontal bossing (reflects the disproportion between normally-formed membranous cranium and the underdeveloped endochondral skull base/midface); midfacial hypoplasia

Thanatophoric Dysplasia

  • A variant of dwarfism — mutation in the extracellular domain of FGFR3
  • Features: extreme shortening of the limbs; frontal bossing of the cranium (described as a "cloverleaf" skull shape); very small thorax — causes fatal respiratory failure

SECTION 7

Cleidocranial Dysostosis (Cleidocranial Dysplasia)

"Cleido-" is Greek for clavicle. Often sporadic; autosomal dominant; localized to chromosome 6p21.

  • Small thorax can cause respiratory difficulties in infancy, but survival into adulthood is typical
  • Short stature
  • Partial to complete aplasia of the clavicles
  • Late eruption of permanent teeth; supernumerary (extra) teeth; unerupted teeth and malformed roots
  • Brachycephaly (flattening of the posterior head) with bossing of the frontal, parietal, and occipital bones
Additional radiographic findings
  • Open anterior fontanelle and presence of wormian bones (extra ossicles within cranial sutures)
  • Delayed ossification of the pubic bone
  • Broad femoral head with short femoral neck
  • Long second metacarpal
Test-taking pearlRecognizing clavicle involvement alone is usually enough to identify this diagnosis on an exam — the disorder's name itself flags the two systems involved (clavicle + cranium).

SECTION 8

Osteopetrosis

Basic description: defective resorption of bone by osteoclasts — either a defect in function (inability to maintain an acidic pH) or a defect in osteoclast formation. Other names: marble bone disease, Albers-Schönberg disease. The bone becomes weak and can break like chalk.

FormInheritanceSeverity
Adult formAutosomal dominantMild
Infant formAutosomal recessiveSevere

Features

  • Fractures — bones are brittle
  • Cranial nerve palsies — from thickening of bone at the cranial nerve foramina
  • Infections (decreased bone marrow) — loss of the medullary cavity
Molecular mechanisms
  • General: impaired acidification of the resorption pit
  • Mutations in carbonic anhydrase 2 (CA2) — generates protons from carbon dioxide and water
  • Mutations in CLCN7 — encodes a proton pump on the surface of osteoclasts

SECTION 9

Osteoporosis

An acquired disease characterized by reduction in bone mass; can be localized or diffuse (generalized). The bone that is present is qualitatively normal — there is simply not enough of it.

Osteopenia vs. osteoporosisOsteopenia = decreased bone mass. Osteoporosis = osteopenia significant enough to represent an increased risk for fracture.

Primary vs. Secondary Causes

Primary: postmenopausal; senile (age-related).

Secondary causes of osteoporosis
CategoryExamples
EndocrineHyperparathyroidism; thyroid dysfunction (hyper- or hypo-); pituitary tumors; diabetes mellitus; Addison disease; Cushing disease/syndrome
GIMalnutrition, malabsorption, vitamin C and D deficiencies
DrugsAlcohol, corticosteroids, chemotherapy
MiscellaneousImmobilization, anemia, pulmonary disease, neoplasia

Development of Osteoporosis

Peak bone mass is determined by genetic factors, physical activity, and nutrition. The greater the peak bone mass reached in young adulthood, the longer the delay until osteoporosis develops.

Factors affecting development
  • Menopause — decreased estrogen exacerbates bone loss: decreased serum estrogen causes increased IL-1, IL-6, and TNF, which stimulate RANK-RANKL and suppress OPG, promoting bone breakdown
  • Aging — decreased replication of osteoprogenitor cells; decreased osteoblast synthesis; decreased matrix growth factors; decreased physical activity

Pathology, Diagnosis & Complications

The gross and microscopic appearance of the bone itself is normal — there is just not enough of it (widely spaced trabeculae on gross specimens).

  • Complications: fracture; compression fractures of vertebrae (leading to kyphoscoliosis or lumbar lordosis); secondary to fractures — pneumonia, pulmonary embolism
  • Diagnosis: 30–40% of bone must be absent before the condition is identifiable on plain x-ray; the standard for measuring bone mineral density is dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DEXA)

SECTION 10

Paget Disease of Bone (Osteitis Deformans)

A disease characterized by increased production of bone that is structurally weak. There is an osteolytic stage, followed by a mixed osteoclastic-osteoblastic stage, followed by an osteosclerotic stage. Bone growth is tracked by alkaline phosphatase.

There is a net gain in bone mass, but the increased bone is not well-organized and is therefore weak and prone to fracture.

Classic histology A mosaic pattern of bone — thickened bony trabeculae laid down in a disorganized, jigsaw-puzzle-like fashion. One of the most recognizable histologic patterns in bone pathology.
Proposed etiologyPossibly induced by paramyxovirus infection (unconfirmed) — can induce IL-1 and IL-6, which are found at high levels in diseased bone. About 10% of cases carry an SQSTM1 mutation, which increases osteoclastogenesis through activation of RANK.

A disease of mid-to-late adulthood, affecting 2.5% of the US population (rare in Scandinavia, China, and Japan — suggesting a genetic component).

  • Monostotic (15%) — tibia, ilium, femur, vertebra, skull, humerus; femur and axial skeleton most common
  • Polyostotic (85%)

Complications

Many patients have none. When present: high-output cardiac failure, nerve impingement, back pain, and secondary osteosarcoma (an important, testable association).

SECTION 11

Rickets & Osteomalacia (Vitamin D Deficiency)

Vitamin D deficiency causes rickets in children (epiphyses still open) and osteomalacia in adults (epiphyses closed) — both reflect impaired bone mineralization.

Causes

  • Decreased exposure to sun
  • Dietary deficiency of calcium and vitamin D
Mechanism Hypocalcemia stimulates PTH; PTH stimulates production of vitamin D. Both PTH and vitamin D promote calcium retention and mobilization — but PTH also promotes loss of phosphate. The net effect is that bone cannot be properly mineralized.

Diagnosis: testing for 25-hydroxyvitamin D most accurately reflects the body's vitamin D supply.

Rickets: Gross Findings

Skeletal manifestations of rickets
FindingDescription
CraniotabesParietal bones buckle inward with pressure and snap back into place
Frontal bossingFrom excess (unmineralized) osteoid
Rachitic rosaryOvergrowth of cartilage or osteoid at the costochondral junction
Pigeon breastRibs bend inward
Harrison grooveFrom the inward pull of the diaphragm
Lumbar lordosis, bowing of the legsFrom weight-bearing on undermineralized long bones

Microscopic: poorly mineralized bone.

SECTION 12

Hyperparathyroidism, Brown Tumor & Renal Osteodystrophy

PTH Functions (Skeletal)

  • Increases RANKL expression on osteoblasts
  • Increases calcium resorption from kidney tubules
  • Increases urinary excretion of phosphates
  • Increases active vitamin D

Under normal conditions, increased blood calcium inhibits PTH secretion.

Primary, secondary, tertiary
  • Primary: associated with parathyroid gland adenomas or hyperplasia
  • Secondary: a stimulus on the parathyroid glands from disease in another system leading to hypocalcemia — most commonly renal failure
  • Tertiary: autonomous functioning of an overly stimulated parathyroid gland

Pathology

  • Microscopic: increased osteoclasts; amount of bone is decreased (replaced with connective tissue)
  • Gross — Brown tumor: a collection of osteoclasts, reactive giant cells, and hemorrhage; can be cystic, in which case the process is called osteitis fibrosa cystica

Renal Osteodystrophy

The alteration of bone morphology seen in patients with chronic kidney disease. Classified along three axes (the same three parameters from bone remodeling basics):

  • Turnover — low, normal, or high
  • Mineralization — normal or abnormal
  • Volume — low, normal, or high

Chronic kidney disease disrupts calcium/phosphate/vitamin D handling and secondarily drives PTH abnormalities, producing a broad spectrum of bone findings (decreased bone formation, marrow fibrosis, altered resorption) rather than one single pattern.

SECTION 13

Fracture Classification & Terminology

Robbins notes that "fractures rank among the most common pathologic conditions of bone," yet lists only a minimal four-part classification scheme (complete/incomplete, closed, comminuted, displaced). Fracture terminology is actually far richer than that.

Over 100 fracture terms are in use
  • 18 eponyms (e.g., Shepherd fracture — the external tubercle of the talus)
  • 25 non-eponyms that describe specific fracture locations (e.g., boxer's fracture — the 5th metacarpal, or sometimes described as the 4th)
  • 16 terms synonymous with other terms (simple = closed; compound = open, though "simple/compound" is no longer preferred usage)
  • 7 fractures named after scenarios (e.g., birth fractures)
  • 4 fractures named after clinical results (stable vs. unstable fractures)

General Classification Framework

Five-part fracture description framework
DescriptorCategories
ExtentComplete or incomplete (through, or not through, the full diameter of the bone)
Integrity of overlying skinClosed or open (the older terms "simple"/"compound" are not preferred)
DirectionTransverse, oblique, spiral
LocationEpiphyseal, metaphyseal, diaphyseal
AlignmentDisplaced or non-displaced fracture ends

SECTION 14

Bone Biomechanics & Fracture Patterns

Forces on Bone

  • Compression (pushing), tension (stretching), shear (forces moving in opposite directions), and combinations (bending, rotation)
  • When a long bone is struck from one side (bending), that side is placed under compression while the opposite side experiences tension — fractures often begin at the site of trauma or an adjacent location under the greatest tensile stress, not necessarily directly at the point of impact
  • Direct trauma — fracture occurs at the location of the trauma. Indirect trauma — fracture occurs at a site removed from the point of trauma (force is transmitted through the bone)

Elastic vs. Plastic Deformation

Bone is viscoelastic — part glass-like (brittle) and part rubber-like (malleable). Plotting stress (force/area) against strain (new length/original length) produces a curve with a yield point: below it, bone springs back to its original shape (elastic deformation); past it, bone can bend further without breaking but does not return to its original shape (plastic deformation), and eventually fracture occurs. A brittle substance like glass has a steep, nearly straight stress/strain line with little give before failure; a malleable substance like rubber has a long, shallow curve. Bone's curve falls in between and depends on how quickly the force is delivered: slowly applied force allows more plastic deformation before failure, while rapidly applied high-energy force causes brittle, glass-like fracture with little to no bending.

Forensic correlateA skull that was beaten (force delivered relatively slowly, over multiple blows) shows plastic deformation — the fragments do not re-approximate cleanly. A skull struck by a rifle round (very high-speed, high-energy force) shatters in a brittle, glass-like fashion, and the fragments can often be re-approximated, since there was little plastic bending before failure.

Forensic pearlNot all spiral fractures in children indicate abuse — many "pathognomonic" abuse indicators from older literature have turned out not to be specific. Context of the reported mechanism matters as much as the fracture pattern itself.

Pathologic Fracture

A fracture occurring at the site of a pre-existing disease process (a bone tumor, a metastatic tumor, Paget disease, a brown tumor from hyperparathyroidism, etc.). Fractures occurring purely in a bone with osteoporosis are conventionally not termed "pathologic fractures."

SECTION 15

Fracture Healing, Complications & Forensic Pearls

Steps of Fracture Repair

  1. Hemorrhage and fibrin deposition at the fracture site, triggering inflammation
  2. Soft callus — a cartilage model is laid down (similar to endochondral ossification)
  3. Hard callus — the cartilage model mineralizes and is bridged by woven bone
  4. Remodeling — woven bone is gradually reorganized into normal lamellar bone

Radiologic and pathologic appearance can give a rough time course of a fracture, but overlapping/re-injured fractures make this imprecise — interpret cautiously.

Factors That Impair Healing

  • Displaced or comminuted fractures (fragments not end-to-end, requiring more remodeling)
  • Inadequate immobilization — continued motion/strain can prevent proper union and produce a pseudoarthrosis ("false joint" forming at the non-union site)
  • Infection, particularly with open or comminuted fractures
  • Nutritional deficiencies (calcium, phosphate, vitamins C and D)
  • Diabetes and vascular disease (e.g., atherosclerosis) — impaired blood flow to the fracture site
Interesting exceptionPseudoarthrosis is uncommonly seen in rib fractures despite ribs constantly moving with respiration — possibly because chest wall musculature helps keep the fracture ends relatively aligned.

Forensic Pathology Pearl: Manner of Death

If a person falls and fractures a hip, and subsequently dies (even from a downstream complication such as pneumonia or inability to wean from a ventilator), the manner of death is accident, not natural — because the fall/fracture set the fatal chain of events in motion, even though the immediate cause of death may be something else (e.g., bronchopneumonia). These cases should be reported to the coroner/medical examiner and are commonly under-referred.

SECTION 16

Osteonecrosis (Avascular Necrosis)

Ischemic death of bone, also called aseptic or avascular necrosis. In osteonecrosis, necrosis is the dominant change in the bone itself — this distinguishes it from the focal/incidental necrosis that can accompany other bone diseases (e.g., necrotic bone within osteomyelitis is not, by itself, called "osteonecrosis"). Technically the term reflects systemic factors, though necrosis of bone can also occur purely locally (e.g., from local ischemia after trauma).

Mechanisms (Usually Acting Together)

  • Obstruction of the vascular lumen (fat, thrombi, gas bubbles)
  • Increased intramedullary/bone marrow pressure
  • Direct blood vessel damage (trauma)

These converge on ischemia, which may be reversible or irreversible depending on its duration. Important modifying factors include genetics, vulnerable anatomic areas (e.g., the epiphysis), and load placed on the bone.

Subchondral vs. Medullary Infarcts

LocationPresentation
SubchondralUsually painful (periosteum is involved) and can lead to collapse of the articular surface, since the cortex here is thinner/subarticular
MedullaryUsually silent unless large; bone remains relatively stable because the load-bearing outer cortex is spared even if the marrow cavity infarcts

Risk Factors

Glucocorticoid administration (e.g., in organ transplant recipients or SLE), sickle cell disease, Caisson (decompression) disease, fat emboli, alcohol use, idiopathic causes, antiphospholipid antibodies, Gaucher disease, and trauma.

Named Forms

  • Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease — avascular necrosis of the femoral head in children
  • Bisphosphonate-related osteonecrosis of the jaw
  • Osteonecrosis of the medial femoral condyle
  • Other bones affected: humeral head, scaphoid, lunate, talus

Osteonecrosis of the Femoral Head

Basic description: decreased blood flow to the femoral head, leading to cell death, fracture, and eventual collapse of the articular surface. Roughly 20,000–30,000 cases per year in the US, accounting for about 10% of hip replacements — a surprisingly large share given it typically affects young, active patients (commonly 20–40 years of age) rather than the typical osteoarthritis population.

SECTION 17

Osteomyelitis

Inflammation of bone ("osteo") and marrow ("myelo"), most commonly infectious. Bacteria reach bone by three routes: exogenous (open fracture, trauma, surgery), hematogenous spread (from bacteremia), or contiguous spread (from an adjacent infected joint or soft tissue).

Acute vs. Chronic

Defining feature: presence of dead bone
  • Acute: neutrophilic infiltrate; typically follows hematogenous seeding (especially in children); usually presents suddenly and does not necessarily progress to chronic disease
  • Chronic: defined pathologically by the presence of dead (necrotic) bone; usually results from open/comminuted fractures or continuous local spread (e.g., non-healing diabetic foot ulcers); tends to be an insidious, low-level process rather than a sudden presentation

Children vs. Adults

ChildrenAdults
Typical courseAcute, hematogenousChronic more common; hematogenous spread is rare because growth plates are closed
Typical siteMetaphysis (highly vascular, near the growth plate) — femur, tibia, fibula most commonVertebrae (when acute/hematogenous); otherwise wherever contiguous spread or vascular disease occurs
PresentationPain, limp, fever, swelling, erythema (classic acute inflammatory picture)Chronic pain, sinus tract, poor wound healing, may have fever/elevated CRP if acute
Most common organismStaphylococcus aureusS. aureus most common overall; S. epidermidis with contiguous spread from skin; Pseudomonas/E. coli in specific settings; consider fungal/mycobacterial if immunocompromised
Classic associationSalmonella osteomyelitis is strongly associated with sickle cell disease.

Risk Factors

Lymphedema, venous stasis/insufficiency, impaired circulation (small vessel disease from hypertension, diabetes, neuropathy), malnutrition, renal or liver failure, immune compromise, neoplasia, and substance abuse — essentially, anything that impairs local blood flow, drainage, or host defense.

Pathologic Findings

  • Sequestrum — a fragment of necrotic bone that becomes "sequestered" away from viable tissue
  • Involucrum — a shell of new bone growth that forms around the sequestrum
  • Sinus tract — a chronic draining connection between the site of bone inflammation and the skin surface

Complications

Pathologic fracture, amyloidosis (chronic inflammatory state), endocarditis (from seeding bacteremia), sepsis, and (rarely) squamous cell carcinoma arising within a chronic sinus tract or secondary osteosarcoma at the infection site.

Specific Forms

  • Tuberculous osteomyelitis — about 1–3% of TB cases; spreads hematogenously and favors long bones and vertebrae; classically affects the synovium, which has relatively higher oxygen tension favorable to Mycobacterium tuberculosis; vertebral TB is called Pott disease

SECTION 18

Syphilitic Bone Disease

Syphilis can involve bone directly, producing a layer of new periosteal bone on top of existing cortex (periostitis). This is now uncommonly seen clinically because most cases are treated, but classic findings remain testable.

Congenital Syphilis: Characteristic Features

  • Hutchinson teeth — notched central incisors
  • Mulberry molars — molars with a berry-like, malformed cusp pattern
  • Saddle nose deformity — from destructive nasal cartilage/bone inflammation (due to gummas)
  • Saber shins — anterior bowing of the tibia
  • Metaphyseal lucencies and periostitis on imaging

Adult (Acquired) Syphilis

  • Bone involvement is rare in secondary syphilis; it is most frequent in tertiary syphilis
  • Periostitis most commonly affects the skull, shoulder, and long bones, causing bone pain
  • Gumma — a focus of central necrosis surrounded by a lymphoplasmacytic and epithelioid cell infiltrate; can occur in bone as part of tertiary disease

Anatomic Location of Primary Bone Tumors

Location within the long bone — epiphysis, metaphysis, or diaphysis — is one of the highest-yield clues for narrowing a bone tumor differential on imaging.
