1. Cardiac Embryology & ASD: Types & Etiology

Septation Basics

  • The interatrial septum forms from two overlapping partitions: the septum primum (first, more superior/posterior) and the septum secundum (second, forms to its right)
  • Each septum has its own opening — the foramen primum and foramen secundum — that normally close or overlap appropriately during development
  • The atrioventricular endocardial cushions are critical for both septal fusion and formation of the mitral/tricuspid valves

Epidemiology

ASDs occur in 6–8% of all children born with congenital heart disease. Females have ASDs twice as often as males.

The Three (Four) Types of ASD

TypeFrequencyMechanismKey Association
Ostium secundum80% — most common type of ASDForamen secundum remains open, due to excessive resorption of the septum primum or interrupted septum secundum development—
Ostium primum15%Foramen primum remains open — incomplete fusion of the septum primum and the AV endocardial cushionsMore common in Down syndrome (disrupted endocardial cushion development)
Patent foramen ovale (PFO)Common, but not a true persistent shuntIncomplete fusion of the septum secundum and septum primum — septal overlap exists, but non-closureCan function like a one-way valve
(Sinus venosus ASD)Less commonDefect near the SVC/pulmonary vein connectionsOften associated with anomalous pulmonary venous return
Key Conceptual Distinction Primum and secundum ASDs form a persistent, structurally open shunt between the atria. A PFO, by contrast, is covered by a flap that can function as a valve — it may open and close intermittently rather than remaining fixed open.

Why PFOs Are Usually Asymptomatic

  • Most PFO patients are asymptomatic because the defect remains functionally closed due to normally higher left atrial pressure (which holds the flap shut)
  • Spontaneous emboli formation can occur, especially with atrial arrhythmias (e.g., atrial fibrillation)
  • Hypercoagulable states (e.g., pregnancy) + a transient increase in right atrial pressure (Valsalva, childbirth, constipation) can push emboli through the PFO into the systemic circulation → paradoxical embolism/stroke risk
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Which type of atrial septal defect is the most common overall, accounting for approximately 80% of ASD cases?

Explanation: Ostium secundum is the most common type of ASD (~80% of cases), resulting from excessive resorption of the septum primum or interrupted development of the septum secundum.

2. An ostium primum ASD is more commonly seen in which of the following conditions?

Explanation: Ostium primum ASD, resulting from incomplete fusion of the septum primum and the atrioventricular endocardial cushions, is more commonly seen in Down syndrome due to disruptions in endocardial cushion development.

3. A pregnant patient with a known PFO develops a stroke shortly after a difficult vaginal delivery. What is the most likely mechanism?

Explanation: Pregnancy is a hypercoagulable state, and events like childbirth transiently raise right atrial pressure; this combination can push a venous embolus through a PFO into the systemic circulation, causing a paradoxical embolic stroke.

2. ASD: Clinical Presentation, EKG & CXR

Clinical Presentation

Most patients are asymptomatic before age 40.
SymptomsPhysical Exam Findings
Exertional dyspnea; heart failure (when present)Systolic ejection murmur — from turbulent flow across the pulmonic valve (not the ASD itself); fixed split S2
Why "Fixed" Split S2? The left-to-right shunt through the ASD keeps right-sided volume (and thus pulmonic valve closure timing) relatively constant across the respiratory cycle, so the normal respiratory variation in the S2 split is lost — the split becomes fixed rather than varying with inspiration/expiration.

Classic Case Presentation

A 15-year-old female presents with fatigue. Exam: widely split S2 + pulmonary flow murmur. EKG: incomplete RBBB, right axis deviation, RVH.

EKG Findings

FindingNote
Incomplete or complete RBBBCommon
Right axis deviationSeen in secundum ASD
Left axis deviationSeen in primum ASD
Right ventricular hypertrophyFrom chronic RV volume overload
Right atrial enlargement—
Atrial fibrillation or atrial flutterMay develop, especially with long-standing atrial stretch
Board Tip Axis deviation direction is a quick way to distinguish ASD subtype on EKG: secundum = right axis, primum = left axis.

Chest X-Ray Findings

  • Enlarged pulmonary arteries; increased pulmonary vascularity (increased hilar markings)
  • Enlarged right ventricle; enlarged right atrium
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A patient with an atrial septal defect has a split S2 that does not vary normally with respiration. What is this finding called, and why does it occur?

Explanation: A fixed split S2 occurs in ASD because the left-to-right shunt maintains relatively constant right-sided volume and pulmonic valve closure timing regardless of the respiratory cycle, eliminating the normal respiratory variation in the S2 split.

2. On EKG, a patient with a suspected atrial septal defect shows right axis deviation. Which type of ASD is this most consistent with?

Explanation: Right axis deviation on EKG is characteristically seen with ostium secundum ASD, while left axis deviation is more typical of ostium primum ASD -- a useful distinguishing EKG clue.

3. Which chest X-ray findings would be expected in a patient with a significant, longstanding atrial septal defect?

Explanation: ASD produces chronic right-sided volume overload, leading to enlarged pulmonary arteries, increased pulmonary vascularity, and enlargement of the right atrium and right ventricle on chest X-ray.

3. ASD: Diagnostic Imaging, Complications & Treatment

Diagnostic Imaging Modalities

ModalityUse
Transthoracic echocardiographySaline injection with bubble contrast demonstrates a right-to-left shunt; pulsed and color flow Doppler show the shunt directly
Transesophageal echocardiographyUsed when transthoracic image quality is suboptimal
CT and MRIBetter elucidate atrial septal anatomy
Cardiac catheterizationDefines size/location of the shunt; determines pulmonary pressure and pulmonary vascular resistance

Complications of Untreated ASD

Atrial fibrillation · Heart failure · Paradoxical systemic embolization (e.g., embolic/hemorrhagic stroke)

Treatment & Patient Selection

Shunt SizePrognosis/Approach
SmallNormal lifespan without treatment
LargeCan lead to disability by age 40 if untreated
Who should be treated: left-to-right shunts with a ratio ≥1.5:1 — left untreated, this leads to RV volume overload and eventually Eisenmenger syndrome (shunt reversal from chronic pulmonary hypertension).
Critical Cutoff for Closure Eligibility Pulmonary hypertension precludes closure if the pulmonary systolic pressure is greater than 2/3 of the systemic systolic pressure — beyond this point, the right heart has already adapted to high pulmonary pressures, and closing the shunt could acutely overload/fail the right ventricle.
  • Treatment options: percutaneous device closure or open surgery
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Which imaging technique uses saline injection with bubble contrast to demonstrate a right-to-left shunt in a suspected ASD?

Explanation: Transthoracic echocardiography with saline (bubble) contrast injection is used to demonstrate a right-to-left shunt, often paired with pulsed and color flow Doppler for further characterization.

2. At what left-to-right shunt ratio should closure of an atrial septal defect generally be considered?

Explanation: A left-to-right shunt ratio of 1.5:1 or greater is generally the threshold at which ASD closure should be considered, since larger shunts lead to progressive RV volume overload if left untreated.

3. A patient being evaluated for ASD closure is found to have a pulmonary systolic pressure that is 75% of their systemic systolic pressure. What is the clinical implication?

Explanation: Pulmonary systolic pressure exceeding two-thirds of systemic systolic pressure indicates significant pulmonary hypertension that precludes safe closure, since the right heart has adapted to high pulmonary pressures and closure risks acute right heart overload.

4. VSD: Epidemiology, Types & Size Classification

Epidemiology

VSDs are the most common type of congenital heart defect overall, accounting for about half of all congenital heart disease cases.
Down Syndrome (Trisomy 21) — Heart Defect BreakdownFrequency
Atrioventricular septal defect45%
Ventricular septal defect35%
Atrial septal defect8%
Patent ductus arteriosus7%
Tetralogy of Fallot4%
Board Tip ~40–50% of children with Down syndrome have a congenital heart defect, most commonly from disrupted endocardial cushion development — this is why AVSD tops the list.

Classic Case

A 5-year-old male with trisomy 21 presents for a routine exam: grade IV/VI holosystolic murmur, loudest at the left lower sternal border, radiating throughout the precordium — classic for VSD.

Inlet/AV canal type VSDNear the AV canal, directly beneath the tricuspid/mitral valvesOften seen as part of an atrioventricular septal defect

📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Which type of ventricular septal defect is the most common overall, located in the muscular portion of the septum, and often closes spontaneously?

Explanation: Muscular VSD is the most common type overall, located in the muscular (lower) portion of the ventricular septum, and a large number close spontaneously without requiring surgery.

2. A membranous VSD is notable for which of the following characteristics?

Explanation: Membranous VSDs, located in the upper septum near the valves, are the type most commonly operated on because most do not close spontaneously, unlike the more common muscular VSDs.

3. A VSD is measured to be 60% of the aortic root diameter. How is this classified?

Explanation: A VSD measuring 25-75% of the aortic root diameter is classified as moderately restrictive, distinct from small/restrictive (<25%) and unrestricted (>75%) categories.

5. VSD: Signs, Eisenmenger Syndrome & Presentation by Age

Signs by Shunt Size

Shunt SizeFindings
Small shuntsLoud, harsh holosystolic murmur in the left 3rd/4th interspaces along the sternum; systolic thrill
Larger shuntsRV volume and pressure overload; pulmonary regurgitation; late finding: cyanosis from right-to-left shunting — Eisenmenger syndrome

Eisenmenger Syndrome — Pathophysiology

Uncorrected left-to-right shunt (since systemic pressure > pulmonary pressure) → increased pulmonary blood flow → pathologic pulmonary vascular remodeling → pulmonary arterial hypertension → RV hypertrophy as a compensatory mechanism (chronically straining to pump against abnormally high pulmonary pressures) → the shunt reverses to right-to-left → cyanosis with pulmonary hypertension and an intracardiac shunt = Eisenmenger syndrome.
Board Tip Eisenmenger syndrome can complicate any uncorrected left-to-right shunt lesion — it is not unique to VSD. It has now appeared as a potential complication of both ASD (Section 3) and VSD in this guide.

Presentation by Age

Age GroupPresentation
BabyPoor eating, failure to thrive; fast/tachypnea, dyspnea; easy tiring
ChildDyspnea on exertion; not gaining weight; dyspnea when eating or crying
AdultDyspnea on exertion, orthopnea; irregular heartbeat; fatigue/weakness
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A patient with a long-standing, uncorrected VSD develops cyanosis. Workup reveals pulmonary hypertension with a shunt that has reversed from left-to-right to right-to-left. What is this condition called?

Explanation: Eisenmenger syndrome describes the reversal of a chronic left-to-right shunt to a right-to-left shunt due to progressive pulmonary arterial hypertension, resulting in cyanosis -- it can complicate any uncorrected left-to-right shunt lesion, including VSD and ASD.

2. Why does the right ventricle hypertrophy in the pathophysiologic progression toward Eisenmenger syndrome?

Explanation: As pulmonary vascular remodeling and pulmonary arterial hypertension develop from chronic increased pulmonary blood flow, the right ventricle hypertrophies as a compensatory mechanism to continue pumping against these elevated pressures.

3. An infant with a moderate VSD is brought in for poor eating and failure to thrive, with fast breathing and easy tiring. Which age group's typical presentation does this match?

Explanation: Poor eating, failure to thrive, tachypnea/dyspnea, and easy tiring are the classic presentation of VSD in babies, distinct from the dyspnea on exertion and weight concerns seen in children or the orthopnea and fatigue seen in adults.

6. VSD: EKG, CXR, Imaging & Management

EKG Findings

May be normal, or show right, left, or biventricular hypertrophy depending on shunt size/direction.
Applying Sokolow-Lyon Criteria to VSD Example: S wave in V1 = 15 mm (3 large boxes) + R wave in V6 = 30 mm (6 large boxes) = 45 mm total, which exceeds the 35 mm threshold → meets voltage criteria for LVH, consistent with a VSD causing left heart volume overload.

Chest X-Ray Findings

  • Left ventricular hypertrophy; left atrial enlargement
  • Enlarged pulmonary arteries and increased pulmonary vascularity

Imaging Modalities

ModalityUse
Echocardiography (with Doppler)Chamber sizes; magnitude of shunting; estimation of RV systolic pressure; valvular regurgitation
MRI or CTAdjunct for cardiac anatomy
Cardiac catheterizationPA pressure/systemic pressure ratio
Critical Inoperability Cutoff A PA pressure/systemic pressure ratio >0.67 on cardiac catheterization is considered inoperable — analogous to (though numerically distinct from) the 2/3 systemic pressure cutoff used for ASD closure eligibility.

Neonatal Monitoring & Management Approach

  • Degree of shunting varies during the first week of life as the infant transitions from intrauterine to extrauterine circulation (changing pulmonary/systemic vascular resistance)
  • Small VSD: likely to remain asymptomatic; reasonable chance of spontaneous closure
  • Moderate/large VSD: often become symptomatic in the first months of life as pulmonary vascular resistance normally declines (unmasking the shunt); monitor for tachypnea, increased work of breathing, poor weight gain/failure to thrive

Treatment

ApproachDetail
Direct patch closure (cardiopulmonary bypass)Procedure of choice for surgical candidates; excellent outcomes with low mortality/reoperation rates
Transcatheter closureReported for muscular, peri-membranous, and residual VSD after surgical repair; technically challenging with a higher complication rate than surgery
  • Patients with repaired VSD and normal pulmonary artery pressures generally have normal lifespans; late problems are uncommon (occasional aortic or tricuspid valve issues)
  • Lifelong follow-up with an adult congenital heart disease cardiologist is recommended after repair
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. On cardiac catheterization, a patient being evaluated for VSD closure has a PA pressure/systemic pressure ratio of 0.75. What is the clinical significance?

Explanation: A PA pressure/systemic pressure ratio greater than 0.67 is considered the threshold beyond which a VSD is deemed inoperable, reflecting pulmonary vascular disease too advanced for safe correction.

2. Why do infants with moderate or large VSDs often become symptomatic specifically in the first few months of life, rather than immediately at birth?

Explanation: At birth, pulmonary vascular resistance is still relatively high, which limits left-to-right shunting through a VSD. As pulmonary vascular resistance normally declines over the following weeks to months, the shunt increases, unmasking symptoms of volume overload.

3. What is the procedure of choice for surgical closure of a significant VSD, and how does its outcome compare to transcatheter closure?

Explanation: Direct patch closure under cardiopulmonary bypass is the procedure of choice for VSD, with excellent outcomes and low mortality/reoperation rates, whereas transcatheter closure -- while reported for select cases -- remains technically challenging with a higher complication rate.

7. Complete Atrioventricular Septal Defect (AVSD)

Complete AVSD = a central endocardial cushion defect that allows blood to flow between all four chambers of the heart, with one common AV valve instead of separate mitral and tricuspid valves.

Embryologic Mechanism

  • In the embryo, a single common AV valve normally separates into two distinct valves — the tricuspid and mitral valves
  • In complete AVSD, this common valve fails to separate, and the septae between the atria and ventricles fail to fuse
  • The resulting common valve has leaflets that may be malformed or fail to close tightly, contributing to regurgitation
Connecting the Dots Recall from Section 4 that AVSD is the most common heart defect in Down syndrome (45%) — this direct link to disrupted endocardial cushion development explains why Down syndrome, ostium primum ASD, and complete AVSD are all closely associated with one another on exams.
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. What is the fundamental embryologic defect underlying a complete atrioventricular septal defect?

Explanation: Complete AVSD results from a central endocardial cushion defect: the common AV valve fails to separate into distinct tricuspid and mitral valves, and the septae between the atria and ventricles fail to fuse, allowing blood flow between all four chambers.

2. Complete AVSD is most strongly associated with which congenital condition, accounting for its highest proportion of heart defects?

Explanation: Complete AVSD is the most common heart defect in Down syndrome, accounting for 45% of cardiac defects in this population, reflecting the shared underlying mechanism of disrupted endocardial cushion development.

3. In a complete AVSD, how many distinct atrioventricular valves are present, and what is the functional consequence?

Explanation: In complete AVSD, there is one common AV valve rather than separate mitral and tricuspid valves; this common valve's leaflets may be malformed or fail to close tightly, contributing to valvular regurgitation.

8. Fetal Circulation & Transition to Postnatal Life

Fetal Circulation

Oxygenated blood from the placenta enters the fetus via the umbilical vein and reaches the right atrium (via the IVC/ductus venosus pathway). From there:
  • Most blood flows through the foramen ovale into the left atrium — a natural shunt that bypasses the fluid-filled fetal lungs
  • The remainder enters the right ventricle; because fetal pulmonary vascular resistance is high (lungs aren't yet breathing air), most of this blood is shunted from the pulmonary artery into the aorta via the ductus arteriosus, again bypassing the lungs
  • The lower body is supplied partly via blood passing through the patent ductus arteriosus
  • Blood in the left ventricle is pumped out the aorta to supply the brain and upper body

The Transition at Birth

1. Umbilical cord is clamped — placental blood/nutrient supply stops
2. First breath → lungs expand → alveolar fluid clears
3. Systemic blood pressure increases; pulmonary vascular resistance drops sharply
4. Reduced need for the ductus arteriosus to shunt blood → ductus arteriosus closes (becomes the ligamentum arteriosum)
5. Increased pulmonary blood return raises left atrial pressure, which now exceeds right atrial pressure → this pressure shift pushes the septum primum flap closed against the septum secundum → foramen ovale closes (becomes the fossa ovalis)
Why This Matters Clinically Some congenital cyanotic lesions (Section 9) are only survivable in the newborn period because the ductus arteriosus is still open. Recognizing this transition explains why prostaglandin E1 (PGE1) is used to keep the ductus artificially open in these babies until surgical correction is possible.
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. In fetal circulation, why does most blood entering the right atrium get shunted through the foramen ovale into the left atrium rather than passing through the right ventricle and pulmonary artery?

Explanation: Since fetal lungs are fluid-filled and not yet functioning for gas exchange, shunting blood from the right atrium directly to the left atrium via the foramen ovale bypasses the unnecessary pulmonary circulation, directing oxygenated placental blood toward the systemic circulation and brain.

2. What is the key hemodynamic change at birth that triggers closure of the foramen ovale?

Explanation: As pulmonary blood flow increases after birth, left atrial pressure rises and now exceeds right atrial pressure; this pressure gradient pushes the septum primum flap against the septum secundum, functionally closing the foramen ovale.

3. Why is prostaglandin E1 (PGE1) administered to neonates with certain cyanotic congenital heart defects?

Explanation: PGE1 is used to keep the ductus arteriosus patent in neonates with ductal-dependent cyanotic lesions, since closure of this shunt (which normally occurs after birth) would be fatal before surgical correction can be performed.

9. Cyanotic Heart Disease Overview: The "5 T's"

Classic Case

A 6-week-old female infant presents with respiratory distress: wheezing, increased work of breathing, pale/mottled with cyanosis around the lips. Vitals: T 35.2°C, HR 169, RR 50, O2 sat on room air 72%, BP 38/23. This presentation should immediately prompt consideration of cyanotic congenital heart lesions.

The "5 T's" of Cyanotic Heart Disease

LesionCore Defect
Transposition of the great arteries (TGA)Aorta arises from the RV, pulmonary artery from the LV — complete separation of systemic and pulmonary circuits
Truncus arteriosusA single truncal vessel fails to divide into the aorta and pulmonary trunk
Tricuspid atresiaFailure of tricuspid valve development
Total anomalous pulmonary venous return (TAPVR)Pulmonary veins fail to connect normally to the left atrium
Tetralogy of FallotSee Sections 10–12
Other (less common) causes: single ventricle, hypoplastic left heart syndrome, double outlet right ventricle, single atrium, pulmonic stenosis or atresia.

Transposition of the Great Arteries (TGA)

  • Complete separation of pulmonary and systemic circulations — incompatible with life without a shunt allowing blood mixing
  • Often detected prenatally or shortly after birth
  • A shunt (patent ductus arteriosus, ASD, or VSD) is needed to allow mixing; the ductus must be kept open (PGE1) pending prompt surgical correction

Truncus Arteriosus

  • The primary truncal vessel fails to divide into separate pulmonary and aortic trunks
  • Detected prenatally or immediately after birth
  • Shunts (e.g., ductus arteriosus) are required for any chance of survival pending prompt surgical correction

Tricuspid Atresia

Failure of tricuspid valve development. Requires maintenance of a patent ductus arteriosus (treat with prostaglandin) to supply blood flow to the lungs, pending surgical management.
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Which mnemonic groups the five classic causes of cyanotic congenital heart disease, and what are they?

Explanation: The '5 T's' -- Transposition of the great arteries, Truncus arteriosus, Tricuspid atresia, Total anomalous pulmonary venous return, and Tetralogy of Fallot -- are the classic causes of cyanotic congenital heart disease most likely to be tested.

2. In transposition of the great arteries, why is a shunt (such as a PDA, ASD, or VSD) necessary for the infant's survival?

Explanation: In TGA, the aorta arises from the right ventricle and the pulmonary artery from the left ventricle, creating two parallel, separate circulations. Without a shunt allowing blood to mix between these circuits, the infant cannot survive, since oxygenated blood would never reach the systemic circulation.

3. An infant with tricuspid atresia requires which medication to maintain ductal patency prior to surgical correction?

Explanation: Prostaglandin E1 is used to maintain patency of the ductus arteriosus in tricuspid atresia (and other ductal-dependent cyanotic lesions), ensuring adequate pulmonary blood flow until surgical correction can be performed.

10. Tetralogy of Fallot: Components & Pathophysiology

Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) is the most common cyanotic heart defect, accounting for 5–7% of congenital heart defects.

The Four Components — Mnemonic: "I have to PROVE..."

LetterComponent
PPulmonary stenosis
RRight ventricular hypertrophy
OOverriding aorta
VVSD
Memory Aid "I have to PROVE that the patient has tetralogy" — P-R-O-V spells out the four defects in order.

Severity Determinant

Severity is determined by the degree of right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) obstruction (i.e., how severe the pulmonary stenosis is):
Degree of ObstructionShunt DirectionClinical Result
Lesser obstructionLeft-to-rightAllows for oxygenation — less severe cyanosis
Greater obstructionRight-to-leftCyanosis

Associations

  • Trisomy 21 (Down syndrome)
  • DiGeorge syndrome
  • Velocardiofacial syndrome
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Using the mnemonic 'I have to PROVE...' for the four components of Tetralogy of Fallot, what does the letter 'O' represent?

Explanation: In the PROVE mnemonic for Tetralogy of Fallot, 'O' stands for overriding aorta -- the aorta sits above the VSD, receiving blood from both ventricles -- alongside Pulmonary stenosis, Right ventricular hypertrophy, and VSD.

2. What determines the clinical severity of cyanosis in a patient with Tetralogy of Fallot?

Explanation: The severity of Tetralogy of Fallot, and specifically the degree of cyanosis, is determined by the severity of right ventricular outflow tract obstruction: lesser obstruction allows a left-to-right shunt with adequate oxygenation, while greater obstruction forces a right-to-left shunt causing cyanosis.

3. Tetralogy of Fallot is associated with which of the following syndromes?

Explanation: Tetralogy of Fallot is associated with trisomy 21 (Down syndrome), DiGeorge syndrome, and velocardiofacial syndrome, reflecting shared underlying developmental pathways affecting cardiac outflow tract formation.

11. Tetralogy of Fallot: Tet Spells & Clinical Presentation

Presentation by Age

AgePresentation
NeonateCritically ill, cyanotic; decreased paO2/SpO2; requires PGE1 to keep the PDA patent
Up to 1st year of lifeDyspnea on exertion; cyanosis; exercise-induced "squatting"; "Tet" spells

Tet Spells — Mechanism

Hypercyanotic episodes precipitated by a sudden increase in right-to-left shunting. Elicited by feeding, crying, or exertion; may also be spontaneous.
  • Classical description: the patient becomes cyanotic, then instinctively squats to relieve the cyanosis/hypoxia
Why Squatting Works Squatting increases systemic vascular resistance and aortic pressure. This decreases right-to-left shunting across the VSD (there's now more resistance pushing blood the "wrong way" out to the body, so more blood is forced through the stenotic pulmonary outflow toward the lungs instead) — allowing more blood to reach the lungs for oxygenation and raising arterial O2 saturation.

Cardiac Exam

FindingDetail
Right ventricular outflow tract murmurCrescendo-decrescendo harsh systolic ejection murmur
LocationLoudest at the left mid-to-upper sternal border; radiates posteriorly
Heart soundsNormal S1; single S2 (due to diminished P2, since the pulmonic valve component is weak/soft from stenosis)
CyanosisVisible in the lips and nail beds
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A toddler with known Tetralogy of Fallot suddenly becomes cyanotic during feeding and instinctively squats. What physiologic effect does squatting have that improves the child's oxygenation?

Explanation: Squatting increases systemic vascular resistance and aortic pressure, which decreases right-to-left shunting across the VSD, forcing more blood through the pulmonary outflow tract to be oxygenated in the lungs, thereby raising arterial oxygen saturation.

2. On cardiac auscultation, a patient with Tetralogy of Fallot has a single S2 sound. What is the explanation for this finding?

Explanation: The single S2 in Tetralogy of Fallot results from a diminished P2 (pulmonic valve closure sound) due to the underlying pulmonary stenosis, making the aortic component (A2) the dominant or only audible component of S2.

3. A neonate with Tetralogy of Fallot is critically ill and cyanotic shortly after birth. What medication is required to stabilize this patient prior to surgical intervention?

Explanation: PGE1 is required in critically ill cyanotic neonates with Tetralogy of Fallot to maintain patency of the ductus arteriosus, ensuring adequate pulmonary blood flow until surgical correction can be performed.

📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A postoperative patient with repaired Tetralogy of Fallot has a QRS duration of 190 msec on EKG. What is the clinical significance of this finding?

Explanation: A QRS duration greater than 180 msec in patients with Tetralogy of Fallot (particularly post-repair) is associated with the greatest risk of sudden death, making this an important EKG parameter to monitor.

2. What classic chest X-ray finding, resembling a type of wooden footwear, is associated with Tetralogy of Fallot?

Explanation: The boot-shaped heart, or Coeur en Sabot (named for a wooden shoe called a sabot), is the classic chest X-ray finding in Tetralogy of Fallot, reflecting a prominent right ventricle and concave right ventricular outflow tract.

3. During the definitive (complete) surgical repair of Tetralogy of Fallot, which structure is closed with a patch?

Explanation: During complete repair of Tetralogy of Fallot, the VSD is closed with a patch, the right ventricular outflow tract is widened by removing thickened muscle, and the obstructive pulmonary valve is repaired or removed as needed.

Patent Ductus Arteriosus (PDA) — Baseline Overview

  • Failure of the ductus arteriosus (the fetal vessel connecting the pulmonary artery to the aorta) to close after birth
  • Produces a persistent left-to-right shunt (aorta → pulmonary artery, since aortic pressure normally exceeds pulmonary pressure postnatally)
  • Classic exam finding: continuous "machinery" murmur
  • Small PDAs may be asymptomatic; larger ones can cause left heart volume overload, failure to thrive, and (if uncorrected) pulmonary hypertension with eventual Eisenmenger physiology
  • Management: pharmacologic closure (e.g., indomethacin/NSAIDs) in premature infants, or surgical/catheter-based closure; conversely, PGE1 is used to keep a PDA open in ductal-dependent cyanotic lesions (see Section 9)

Coarctation of the Aorta — Baseline Overview

  • Narrowing of the aorta, classically near the ligamentum arteriosum (the site of the closed ductus arteriosus)
  • Classic exam findings: upper extremity hypertension with diminished/delayed femoral pulses (brachial-femoral pulse delay), lower extremity blood pressure lower than upper extremity
  • Associated with bicuspid aortic valve and Turner syndrome
  • Chest X-ray finding in older children/adults: "rib notching" from collateral vessel formation
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. What is the classic auscultatory finding associated with a patent ductus arteriosus?

Explanation: PDA classically produces a continuous 'machinery' murmur, reflecting the persistent left-to-right shunt from the aorta to the pulmonary artery throughout both systole and diastole.

2. A child is found to have hypertension in the upper extremities with diminished and delayed femoral pulses compared to the brachial pulses. What congenital lesion does this suggest?

Explanation: Upper extremity hypertension with diminished/delayed femoral pulses (brachial-femoral pulse delay) is the classic presentation of coarctation of the aorta, a narrowing typically located near the ligamentum arteriosum.

3. Coarctation of the aorta is classically associated with which chromosomal condition and which valve abnormality?

Explanation: Coarctation of the aorta is classically associated with Turner syndrome and with a bicuspid aortic valve, an important pairing of findings to recognize together on exams.
