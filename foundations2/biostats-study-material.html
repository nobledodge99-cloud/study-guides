1. Population, Sample & Parameter Estimates

A population is "the whole" — the complete group you actually care about. A sample is a part of that population. A parameter is a numerical characteristic of the population itself, while a value calculated from a sample is a parameter estimate — an approximation of the true parameter.

We very rarely have population-wide data, so we rely on samples and use them to calculate parameter estimates that approximate the true (usually unknown) population parameters.

Subpopulations may exist within a population. A representative sample reflects the subpopulation proportions observed in the general population — if it doesn't, conclusions drawn from it may not generalize back to the population.

Population vs. Sample
PopulationSample
"The whole"A part of "the whole"
Subpopulations may existA representative sample reflects those subpopulation proportions

2. Incidence & Prevalence

Epidemiological Concepts for Populations
IncidencePrevalence
Measures new casesMeasures all existing cases
Reflects the risk of developing diseaseReflects the burden of disease
Includes only people who become ill during a specified periodIncludes everyone currently living with the disease
Worked example (from lecture) Suppose a town with 1,000 people.

At the beginning of the year, 50 people already have diabetes:
Prevalence = 50 / 1,000 = 5%

During the year, 20 additional people are newly diagnosed. Only the 950 people who were initially disease-free are at risk (you cannot get diabetes two times):
Incidence = 20 / 950 = 2.1%

At the beginning of the next year, there are 70 people with diabetes (50 original + 20 new):
Prevalence = 70 / 1,000 = 7%
Notice the denominator difference: prevalence is measured against the total population, while incidence is measured only against the population still at risk (i.e., excluding existing cases who can't become "new" cases again).

3. Probability & Distributions

Probability is the likelihood that an event will occur — in other words, how likely some event is to happen based on previous experience. Probability incorporates error.

A population's parameters have variability, and that variability follows a probability distribution. The Normal distribution is the most popular, but others exist depending on the type of data:

  • Binomial — e.g., a coin toss
  • Multinomial — e.g., a dice toss
  • Poisson — e.g., counts
Important points about probability, per lecture: we can modify the conditions that change the likelihood of an event, but once those conditions are set, the event itself is out of our control. There is no good luck or bad luck, karma does not exist, and wishing hard does not change the likelihood of an event. Humans also tend to be poor at weighing the conditions that determine an event's likelihood based on experience alone.

4. Error, Power & Margin of Error

Error, in statistics, is the difference between the value we estimated and the true value. We very rarely know the true value. Sample estimates carry error — specifically sampling error — and the margin of error shrinks gradually as sample size increases (the estimate becomes closer to the true population value).

Sampling error and measurement error are related but distinct: sampling error occurs because a sample is missing some part of the population, while measurement error occurs because our measuring methods themselves have error.

Types of Error (Hypothesis Testing)
H0 TrueH0 False
Reject H0Type I Error (false positive) — probability = αCorrect decision (true positive)
Fail to reject H0Correct decision (true negative)Type II Error (false negative) — probability = β
Consequences of each error type: what is worse, a false positive or a false negative? In the real world, it all depends on what is at stake and what your liability is — there is no universally "worse" error, only context-dependent tradeoffs.

Estimating the margin of error for a future study is called a power calculation. Note the lecture's specific labeling convention: power (the probability of avoiding a Type II error / correctly detecting a true effect) is denoted "β" in this framework, tied directly to the margin of error shrinking as sample size grows.

To perform a power calculation, you need to know: significance level (α), sample size, effect size, and the distribution being used.

5. Sensitivity & Specificity

Sensitivity: how good is the test at finding people who have the disease? Specificity: how good is the test at ruling out people who do not have the disease?

Sensitivity = 90 / (90 + 10) = 90 / 100 = 90% — the test correctly identifies 90% of diseased patients.
Specificity = 80 / (80 + 20) = 80 / 100 = 80% — the test correctly identifies 80% of non-diseased patients.

6. Bias, Confounding, Randomization & Blinding

Reliability and validity relate to measurement error: reliability is how stable or consistent a measurement is, while validity is how well a measurement reflects what it is purported to measure.

Bias is a disproportionate weight in favor of or against an idea. There are many types of bias:

  • Bad sampling can lead to bias
  • Estimation methods can introduce mathematical bias
  • Conflict of interest can lead to corruption — and therefore must always be disclosed

Sampling randomization is the process of selecting a sample through a random process (e.g., a computer program, or pieces of paper in a hat), and it helps prevent bias. Note: haphazardness is not the same as randomization — picking "whoever's around" is not a random process.

Confounding happens when there is an additional variable that is related to the variable we're interested in, which also independently contributes to the outcome. Confounders are often unconsidered, and in practice it can be very difficult to separate a true effect from a confounder's effect.

Concealment allocation and blinding means hiding the method of sorting participants or treatments to prevent bias from affecting the outcome:

  • Single-blind
  • Double-blind
  • Triple-blind

Blinding is not always possible (e.g., a surgical vs. non-surgical intervention is hard to blind to the surgeon or often the patient).

9. Hypothesis Testing, P-Values & Confidence Intervals

A p-value is the probability of occurrence of a given event based on the assumed distribution — in other words, the random chance of a specific event outcome happening. We use p-values to test hypotheses, and the significance level is called "α."

Types of Hypothesis
Null Hypothesis (H0)Alternate Hypothesis (Ha)
There is no differenceThere is a difference
There is no associationThere is an association
There is no relationshipThere is a relationship

In the simplest scenario, statistical hypothesis testing asks: do two samples belong to the same population (H0), or not (Ha)? In other words — do we have one population represented by two samples, or two populations each represented by one sample?

One-tailed significance example (from lecture) Ha = observed value > true value under H0.
If the observed p-value is above the established significance threshold, it is declared statistically significant.

A confidence interval is an estimated range where we can expect our parameter estimate to land; it is bound to the significance level chosen for the analysis.

10. Contingency Tables: Risk, Odds Ratio & Relative Risk

A regression uses numerical data and an equation that tests whether the slope (β) differs from zero:

Y = α + βX + e   (Y = dependent variable, X = independent variable, α = intercept, β = slope, e = error)
Contingency table (from lecture)
Lung DiseaseNo Lung DiseaseTotal
Smokers4060100
Non-Smokers1090100
Total50150200
Risk (relative risk approach):
Risk among smokers = 40 / 100 = 0.40
Risk among non-smokers = 10 / 100 = 0.10
Relative risk = 0.40 / 0.10 = 4.0

Odds (odds ratio approach):
Odds among smokers = 40 / 60 = 0.667
Odds among non-smokers = 10 / 90 = 0.111
Odds ratio = 0.667 / 0.111 = 6.0
Same underlying 2×2 table, two different questions: relative risk compares the risk (probability) of disease between groups, while the odds ratio compares the odds of disease between groups. Because odds and risk diverge more as the outcome becomes more common, the OR (6.0) here is noticeably larger than the RR (4.0) — lung disease in this table is not rare.

11. Chi-Square, Causality & Significance

Chi-square (χ²) compares the proportion of observed vs. expected counts:

  • Small χ² — observed counts are close to expected counts; data are consistent with chance.
  • Large χ² — observed counts differ greatly from expected counts; evidence of an association.
Causality: when a relationship, association, or correlation is detected, we need to pay attention to define the cause. Statistical significance does not imply cause. A confounding variable is an unconsidered variable that affects the variables we are considering.
Multiple testing burden (from lecture) This is the cumulative probability risk of making an erroneous estimate. Traditionally, significance is declared if p ≤ 0.05. So, if 20 independent tests are performed at the 0.05 level, then at least 1 is erroneous by definition.
Statistical significance vs. clinical (biological) significance: statistics evaluates the numbers, but the relevance of those numbers must be put in context. In other words — does that statistical significance actually matter?

As the lecture closes: these concepts are all inter-related, and while the material may look complicated, understanding the basic concepts helps since they repeat across other concepts. The difficulty of biostatistics is not in the calculations — it is in understanding and accounting for context. In medicine, black-and-white answers are rare; most decisions lie in the gray area.
