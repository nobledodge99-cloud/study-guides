1. Introduction & the ABCS Framework

The same four-part framework used for the upper respiratory system applies here to the lower respiratory system (trachea, bronchi, lungs):

  • A — Autonomics: sympathetic and parasympathetic innervation to the lung and its action on the system, plus Chapman's points
  • B — Biomechanics: myofascial and skeletal biomechanical structures, and how restriction can affect the system
  • C — Circulatory: related arteries, veins, and lymphatics, and how ideal circulation is vital for system function
  • S — Screening: additional areas to screen for the lower respiratory system
Required reading: the MCOM OCS III & IV Manual, Chapter 5.

Section 1

Q1. The ABCS framework applied to the lower respiratory system covers which four categories?

The ABCS framework stands for Autonomics (innervation + Chapman's points), Biomechanics (myofascial/skeletal structures), Circulatory (arteries/veins/lymphatics), and Screening (additional areas to examine) — the same structure used for the upper respiratory system, now applied to the lower respiratory system.

2. A — Autonomics

Sympathetic & Parasympathetic Effects

Section 2.1

Q2. Which autonomic division is normally dominant in the healthy lung, and what is a key functional consequence of this?

The lung is normally parasympathetic dominant, producing a slight increase in bronchial smooth muscle tone along with a clear, thin, saliva-like mucus blanket that keeps the bronchial passages moist and clean.

Section 2.1

Q3. Which finding is associated with sympathetic (rather than parasympathetic) stimulation of the lung?

Sympathetic stimulation of the lung causes local hypoperfusion and epithelial hyperplasia, along with thickened secretions (increased goblet cells), slight bronchial tube dilation, and vasoconstriction of the lung parenchyma — the opposite pattern from parasympathetic stimulation.

The Pneumonia Cascade — Autonomics Gone Wrong

This step-by-step cascade illustrates how an initially parasympathetic-dominant, healthy lung spirals into the shallow, rapid breathing seen in a sick patient:

  1. An irritant is introduced (viral infection, bacterial infection, or smoking)
  2. Local irritation of the bronchial epithelium triggers an inflammatory response that spreads into lung tissue
  3. Alveolar surfactant is reduced; exudate forms; congestion and lung tissue edema ensue → tissue congestion symptoms begin
  4. These symptoms activate the cough reflex, stimulating visceral afferent nerve endings in the area of tissue injury
  5. This is a sympathetic response — facilitated segments/Chapman's reflexes may appear, and the normally parasympathetic-dominant lung is now in a hypersympathetic state
  6. This causes vasoconstriction and local hypoperfusion of the already-injured area (which needs oxygen the most), plus slight bronchial tube dilation
  7. Increased goblet cells make the once-thin mucus layer thick and sticky — now the lung is congested, with edematous interstitial tissue and thick, abundant mucus in the bronchial tubes
  8. Distal air sacs that should be filled with air start filling with fluid/edema instead
  9. A parasympathetic response re-emerges: the body can't distinguish air-filled from fluid-filled alveoli, so the vagus nerve signals the respiratory center to limit diaphragm excursion
  10. Decreased diaphragmatic excursion, combined with the need for more oxygen, increases the abdominal diaphragmatic rate — producing shallow, rapid breathing
Clinical decision point: once the patient is stable, decide which part of this cycle to target — the parasympathetic side, or the sympathetic side? The hypersympathetic state is what really starts this downward spiral, so that's the priority target for treatment.

Section 2.2

Q4. In the pneumonia cascade, what triggers the shift from the lung's normal parasympathetic dominance into a hypersympathetic state?

Once tissue congestion symptoms activate the cough reflex, visceral afferent nerve endings in the area of tissue injury are stimulated — this is a sympathetic response, shifting the normally parasympathetic-dominant lung into a hypersympathetic state.

Section 2.2

Q5. Why does a sick patient with advanced lung congestion develop shallow, rapid breathing?

As distal air sacs fill with fluid/edema, the body (via the vagus nerve) can't distinguish these from normal air-filled alveoli, so it limits diaphragm excursion. Combined with the physiologic drive for more oxygen (increasing diaphragmatic rate), this produces shallow, rapid breathing.

Section 2.2

Q6. According to this lecture's clinical reasoning, which part of the autonomic cascade should be the priority target for treatment once a pneumonia patient is stabilized?

The lecture's view is that the hypersympathetic state is what really starts the downward spiral, making it the priority target for treatment — hence the emphasis on treating sympathetic segments (like T2-T6 and the ribs) in the case discussed later.

Chapman's Points

Anterior and posterior Chapman's points exist for the bronchus, upper lung, and lower lung — all can be treated with firm circular pressure.

Chapman's Points for the Lower Respiratory System
StructureAnteriorPosterior
Bronchus2nd ICS, close to the sternal borderBetween the spinous process and transverse process of T2
Upper lung3rd ICS, close to the sternal borderBetween the transverse processes of T3–4, midway between spinous and transverse process
Lower lung4th ICS, close to the sternumMidway between the spinous and transverse processes of T4–5
Diagnostic use: Chapman's points aren't just for treatment — they can help localize a problem. If a patient has congestion in both the upper and lower lung and you find a Chapman's point at the 3rd ICS near the sternal border, that points to an upper lung problem; if it's at the 4th ICS, that points to a lower lung problem.

Section 2.3

Q7. A patient has diffuse congestion on auscultation, and the physician is trying to determine whether the primary process is upper or lower lobe. A tender Chapman's point is found at the 3rd intercostal space near the sternal border. What does this suggest?

The anterior Chapman's point for the upper lung is at the 3rd ICS close to the sternal border; finding tenderness there — as opposed to the 4th ICS point for the lower lung — helps localize the problem to the upper lung.

Section 2.3

Q8. Where is the posterior Chapman's point for the bronchus located?

The posterior Chapman's point for the bronchus is located between the spinous process and transverse process of T2.

3. B — Biomechanics

Lung & Thoracic Cage Anatomy

The lungs attach to the heart and trachea via the roots of the lung and the pulmonary ligament. The right lung is heavier and larger, but has decreased vertical extent (because the right hemidiaphragm sits higher, due to the solid mass of the liver beneath it).

  • Left lung: superior and inferior lobes, separated by the oblique fissure
  • Right lung: superior, middle, and inferior lobes — the horizontal fissure separates the superior from the middle lobe; the oblique fissure separates the superior and middle lobes from the inferior lobe

The Thoracic Cage

Composed of the 12 thoracic vertebrae (with their discs), 12 pairs of ribs and costal cartilages, and the sternum. It protects the heart, lungs, great vessels, liver, and spleen.

InferiorThe diaphragmatic surface

The right lung is heavier and larger overall, but its vertical extent is decreased because the right dome of the diaphragm sits higher, due to the solid mass of the liver positioned beneath it.

Q10. Which structure separates the superior and middle lobes of the right lung?

The horizontal fissure separates the superior lobe from the middle lobe of the right lung; the oblique fissure then separates the superior and middle lobes from the inferior lobe.

Section 3.1

The hilum (medial mediastinal border) is where vessels and nerves enter and leave the lung, surrounded by pleura; its inferior extension becomes the pulmonary ligament.

Section 3.2

Q12. A patient has restricted motion at C1-C2. Which autonomic function to the lung is most directly relevant to this segment?

C1-2 is the relevant segment for the vagus nerve, which provides parasympathetic innervation to the lung — a key consideration when addressing the cervical spine in a lower respiratory case.

Section 3.2

Q13. Why is restriction of the lumbar spine relevant to a patient with a lower respiratory complaint?

The diaphragm's crura attach from L1-L3 on the left and L1-L4 on the right, and the sympathetic chain passes through L1-L2 — making lumbar spine restriction relevant to diaphragmatic excursion in a lower respiratory case.

Section 3.2

Q14. A patient has significant hypertonicity of the thoracic paraspinal muscles. What is the downstream functional consequence described in this lecture?

Hypertonicity of the thoracic paraspinal muscles decreases excursion of the thoracic cage, leading to less productive respiration, which in turn leads to fatigue and moves the patient further from homeostasis/baseline.

Muscles & Fascia

The diaphragm is the main muscle of respiration, innervated by the phrenic nerve, rising and falling during respiration. It must always be evaluated in a respiratory case, even if it isn't the first area treated.

Fascia

The 4th layer of fascia (visceral fascia) is derived from splanchnic tissue. This fascia around the lung provides a conduit for neurovascular bundles entering the lung, as well as a drainage route out of the lung.

Section 3.3

Q15. A child is having a severe asthma attack and is visibly using their sternocleidomastoid and scalene muscles to breathe. What does this indicate?

The SCM and scalenes are accessory muscles of inspiration, not used in normal quiet breathing — their visible use indicates recruitment during a pathological state (like an asthma attack) requiring increased respiratory effort.

Section 3.3

Q16. What is the origin and function of the visceral (4th layer) fascia surrounding the lung?

The 4th layer (visceral) fascia around the lung is derived from splanchnic tissue and provides both a conduit for neurovascular bundles entering the lung and a drainage route out of it.

4. C — Circulation

Arteries & Veins

Arterial Supply
VesselKey Points
Pulmonary arteriesBranch from the pulmonary trunk; deliver less-oxygenated blood to the lungs for gas exchange; divide into lobar arteries for the individual lobes
Bronchial arteriesCarry oxygenated blood to the lung parenchyma and visceral pleura; 2 left bronchial arteries arise from the thoracic aorta; 1 right bronchial artery arises from the thoracic aorta or the proximal posterior intercostal artery
Venous Drainage
VesselKey Points
Pulmonary veinsDrain the distal lung parenchyma and visceral pleura into the left atrium. Superior right PV drains the right superior + middle lobes; superior left PV drains the left superior lobe; right and left inferior PVs drain their respective inferior lobes

Section 4.1

Q17. Which vessels carry oxygenated blood to the lung parenchyma itself, as opposed to blood destined for gas exchange?

The bronchial arteries carry oxygenated blood to the lung parenchyma and visceral pleura (nourishing the lung tissue itself), while the pulmonary arteries carry less-oxygenated blood to the alveoli specifically for gas exchange.

Section 4.1

Q18. Where does the right bronchial vein typically empty?

The right bronchial vein empties into the azygos vein, while the left bronchial vein empties into the accessory hemiazygos vein or the left superior intercostal vein.

Lymphatics

Pulmonary lymphatics are divided into two parts:

  • Superficial plexus: drains the lung tissue and visceral pleura, into the bronchopulmonary lymph nodes
  • Deep plexus: drains the pulmonary lymph nodes and bronchopulmonary lymph nodes; final stop is the thoracic duct or the right lymphatic duct
Why this matters: both medical and osteopathic research on chronic lymphatic congestion has noted that when chronic congestion occurs, concurrent poor cellular oxygenation is associated with increased infection, increased mortality, increased healing time, and increased fibrosis/scarring. This is why treating the thoracic inlet — opening it to allow proper lymphatic drainage — is such an important osteopathic priority in lower respiratory disease.

Section 4.2

Q19. Lymphatic fluid draining from the deep plexus of the lung ultimately empties into which structure(s)?

The deep plexus drains the pulmonary and bronchopulmonary lymph nodes, with its final stop being the thoracic duct or the right lymphatic duct.

Section 4.2

Q20. According to research on chronic lymphatic congestion, which of the following outcomes is associated with poor cellular oxygenation from that congestion?

Chronic lymphatic congestion, with its associated poor cellular oxygenation, is associated with increased infection, increased mortality, increased healing time, and increased fibrosis and scarring — the rationale for prioritizing thoracic inlet treatment.

5. S — Screening

Screening is "the art of medicine" — everything taught in lab put to use.

Observation

  • Accessory muscle use; rapid or shallow breathing; blue lips (especially important in a baby)
  • Gait: are they splinting/holding the ribcage while walking (common with a rib fracture, since deep breaths hurt)? Are they swinging their arms normally?
  • Posture, landmarks, symmetry

Structural Exam

  • Check the bony regions discussed: thoracic area, ribs, cervical spine — does the finding lead toward a visceral or muscular complaint?
  • Chapman's screen — anterior points in the 2nd/3rd/4th ICS, or posterior points along the transverse and spinous processes
  • Evaluate the diaphragm: is it moving into inhalation and exhalation evenly? Are lumbar restrictions or accessory muscle use suggesting it isn't moving the way it should?
Pearl: the screening exam doesn't end with the osteopathic exam — it ends with a complete exam.

Additional Testing

  • Chest x-ray — a great first step; if inconclusive but suspicion remains high, follow with a CT scan
  • Pulse ox — how are they oxygenating?
  • Blood work — CBC or chem panel if an infectious process is suspected
  • Spirometry — assess how they breathe
  • Arterial blood gas — if the patient is very sick
  • Bronchoscopy — direct visualization of the lung, if needed

History Questions

Is the patient a smoker? Do they live with a smoker? Are they allergic to anything in the air? What's their diet like? These details help build a better differential.

Section 5

Q21. A patient walks into the exam room holding their arm across their ribcage, taking shallow breaths, and moving carefully. What should this observation raise suspicion for?

Splinting the ribcage while walking, with shallow, careful breathing, is a classic sign of a rib fracture — patients avoid deep breaths and support the area because it hurts to breathe deeply.

Section 5

Q22. A chest x-ray is inconclusive in a patient with ongoing suspicion for a lower respiratory process. What is the appropriate next imaging step?

If the chest x-ray is inconclusive but clinical suspicion remains, a CT scan is the appropriate next step for further evaluation.

6. Case: History, Exam & Diagnosis

History & Physical Reasoning

HPI 32-year-old male with cough and shortness of breath for 10 days. Chest pain for the last week — sharp and burning, left-sided, front and back; worse with coughing and lying down; better with rest and NSAIDs. Cough is productive, foul-tasting, and constant; can't lie down without increasing coughing. SOB occurs with increased activity or walking far. Feels tired and achy all over; temperature up to 100.9°F at home. No travel history.

Reasoning through the HPI:

  • Chest pain is always a "stop and describe" moment — you need to rule out a cardiac cause quickly
  • Sharp pain suggests a possible fractured rib; burning pain suggests something neurologic (e.g., shingles/zoster)
  • Given the productive, foul-tasting cough plus fever, a bacterial process (bronchitis or pneumonia) rises on the differential; cardiac causes remain on the list but lower in priority
PMH / Surgical / Meds / Social / Family History Left knee partial ACL tear 3 months ago (playing soccer); history of GERD; tonsillectomy (no complications); takes NSAIDs; completed a course of oral steroids 2 weeks ago (for the knee); NKDA; denies tobacco/illicit drug use, rarely drinks alcohol; single, works as a financial planner (sits/drives a lot, but active — soccer or weightlifting 4–5x/week). Mother alive and well with type 2 diabetes; father alive and well, prostate cancer in remission; no siblings.

Reasoning: GERD can itself cause a cough. Recent oral steroids can weaken the immune system — a plausible reason this otherwise young, active, healthy patient is now so sick. Mother's diabetes raises a (admittedly speculative) question about early undiagnosed diabetes/neuropathy in this patient. Father's prostate cancer is noted as potentially relevant later.

Section 6.1

Q23. This patient describes his chest pain as "sharp and burning." What two potential causes does this specific description raise, according to the case reasoning?

"Sharp" pain raises the possibility of a fractured rib, while "burning" pain raises the possibility of a neurologic cause such as shingles (zoster) — both worth considering alongside the more likely respiratory/infectious process.

Section 6.1

Q24. Why is this patient's recent completion of an oral steroid course clinically significant to his current presentation?

Oral steroids can weaken the immune system — a plausible explanation for why this otherwise young, active, healthy patient became significantly ill, potentially leaving him in a compromised state that allowed an infection to take hold.
Objective / PE Vitals: only BP is normal. Temp elevated; pulse ~100 (elevated for a young healthy male, whose HR should be in the 60s–80s); RR 20 (elevated); pulse ox 90% on room air (technically within a broad "normal" range, but low for a young, theoretically healthy person). General: mild respiratory distress; cough deep and productive (suggests something bacterial). HEENT: cobblestoning of the pharynx (consistent with his GERD); shoddy anterior cervical lymph nodes (suggests the body is actively fighting something, likely bacterial). Chest and heart: normal. Lungs: findings over the right lower lung/lower lobe bronchi. Abdomen: normal. Skin: no peripheral edema or rash.

Reasoning: the absence of a rash doesn't fully rule out shingles/zoster, since the rash can appear after the pain starts — but it does mean that if it is zoster, it's being caught early (better prognosis), and it makes zoster less likely overall.

Section 6.1

Q25. This patient's pulse oximetry reads 90% on room air, which is described as "technically" within a broad normal range. Why is this still a concerning finding?

While 90% may fall within a broad acceptable range for some populations, it is notably low for a young, otherwise healthy patient, whose oxygen saturation would typically be expected to be higher — making this a meaningful red flag in this specific patient.

Section 6.1

Q26. This patient has no rash on skin exam. Does this fully rule out shingles (zoster) as a cause of his chest pain?

The rash of shingles/zoster can appear after the onset of pain, so the absence of a rash doesn't completely rule it out — though it does make it less likely at this point, and if zoster is later confirmed, it would mean it was caught early (a better prognosis).

Osteopathic Structural Exam

OSE Findings AGR (upper rib cage) left greater than right; hypertonic thoracic paraspinal muscles and iliocostalis, left greater than right; atlas (AA) rotated left; C4, T1, T4, and T6 are all type II somatic dysfunctions; inhaled ribs 2–5 on the left, with rib 5 as the key rib — this rib is also structurally prominent anteriorly on the left, and less prominent posteriorly; tender points in the bilateral pectoralis major and the right anterior scalene; diaphragm inhaled on the right.

Reasoning through each finding:

  • AA (atlas) rotated left → the vagus nerve is nearby, and the vagus provides parasympathetic input to the lung
  • C4 → part of C3-5, which "keeps the diaphragm alive" via the phrenic nerve
  • T1 → a border of the thoracic inlet; since this patient has a productive cough, fluid drainage is a real concern, making T1 important
  • T4 and T6 → sympathetics to the lung
  • Inhaled ribs 2–5 → also relevant to sympathetics, since the sympathetic chain ganglia sit right underneath the rib angles
  • Prominent left 5th rib anteriorly, less prominent posteriorly → a new concept: ribs can have a structural component, where part of the rib is stuck in a particular position. With an inhalation dysfunction, the key rib gets stuck more anteriorly (as it would normally slide with a deep inhalation, but now stays stuck there); with an exhalation dysfunction, the key rib gets stuck more posteriorly. Some schools call these "structural ribs."
  • Tender points, bilateral pec major and (right) anterior scalene → these are accessory muscles of inspiration
  • Diaphragm inhaled on the right → the main muscle of respiration is stuck

Section 6.2

Q27. This patient has a structural finding at the left 5th rib — prominent anteriorly, less prominent posteriorly — corresponding to an inhalation dysfunction. According to the new concept introduced in this case, why does an inhaled rib get stuck anteriorly rather than posteriorly?

On a normal deep inhalation, ribs move to slide more anteriorly. With an inhalation dysfunction, the key rib gets stuck in that anteriorly-displaced position rather than returning to neutral — the opposite pattern (posterior prominence) is seen with an exhalation dysfunction.

Section 6.2

Q28. Why is T1 specifically highlighted as an important finding in a patient with a productive cough?

T1 is one of the borders of the thoracic inlet, which is key to venous and lymphatic drainage — since this patient has a productive cough (a fluid-related issue), ensuring the thoracic inlet can properly drain that fluid is an important consideration.

Differential, Testing & Diagnosis

Differential Diagnosis Pneumonia; bronchitis; GERD; somatic dysfunction (cervical spine, thoracic spine, diaphragm, rib cage); rib fracture; cardiomyopathy with CHF; asthma; pulmonary embolism; sinusitis with post-nasal drip; zoster (shingles); and MI (not very likely, but still on the list).
Testing Chest x-ray shows a right lower lobe infiltrate. Sputum culture is somewhat controversial — reasonable if you're uncertain of the diagnosis, but this presentation is fairly consistent with community-acquired (likely pneumococcal) pneumonia given his demographics and exposure (meeting clients, driving). EKG is probably not helpful here, since nothing points toward a cardiac cause. If the patient were sicker or hospital admission were being considered: CBC with differential (track WBC trend, screen for bleeding), CMP (assess metabolic/acid-base status), and blood cultures (aerobic and anaerobic, in case of an unexpected organism).
Diagnosis Pneumonia — pneumococcal versus aspiration, most likely pneumococcal (he doesn't appear to have been inebriated or lost consciousness, which would raise aspiration risk). Plus somatic dysfunction of the cervical spine, thoracic spine, diaphragm, and rib cage.
Medical Plan Augmentin 875/125 mg, one tablet PO twice daily for 10 days; offer a cough suppressant if desired; OMT today in the office; follow up in 10–14 days to check pulse ox and consider further treatment — but explicitly tell the patient to come back sooner (or call) if anything changes, both for his benefit and as good medicolegal practice.

Section 6.3

Q29. Why does the physician favor a pneumococcal etiology over an aspiration etiology for this patient's pneumonia?

Aspiration pneumonia classically follows an event of impaired consciousness or protective reflexes (e.g., intoxication, loss of consciousness). Since this patient doesn't appear to have had such an event, a community-acquired pneumococcal pneumonia is favored.

Section 6.3

Q30. Why is the patient explicitly told to return sooner than his scheduled 10-14 day follow-up if something changes?

Telling a patient to return sooner (or call) if something changes serves two purposes: it ensures the patient gets timely care if his condition worsens, and it documents that this instruction was given — protecting the physician medicolegally if the patient's condition changes before the scheduled follow-up.

7. Case: OMT Treatment Sequence

The "Procedure Rights"

OMT is a procedure, no different from a joint injection or any other clinical procedure — it deserves the same deliberate thought process. Before starting, line up your "rights":

  • Do I have the right patient with the right problem?
  • Am I the right practitioner doing the right procedure?

If all of these line up, a good outcome is much more likely.

Section 7.1

Q31. According to this lecture, how should OMT be conceptualized as a clinical intervention?

OMT should be thought of as a procedure — no different from a shoulder or knee injection — deserving the same deliberate thought process: lining up the right patient, right problem, right practitioner, and right procedure before proceeding.

The 8-Step Treatment Order

Given all his findings, here's the treatment sequence used in this case, along with the reasoning for each step and technique choice. Early in the visit, techniques requiring patient effort (like muscle energy) are avoided, since this patient is sick and fatigued — techniques like Still technique or HVLA are preferred instead, since they require little to no effort from the patient.

Section 7.2

Q32. Why does this physician avoid using muscle energy technique early in the treatment sequence for this particular patient?

Muscle energy technique requires active effort from the patient. Since this patient is sick and likely fatigued, techniques requiring little to no patient effort (like Still technique or HVLA) are preferred early in the treatment, with muscle energy introduced later once the patient can better tolerate it.

Section 7.2

Q33. Why is the thoracic inlet (T1) treated first in this sequence, before any other finding?

Since pneumonia has a fluid component, opening the thoracic inlet first ensures there's an available drainage pathway before further treatment potentially mobilizes additional fluid — a central theme repeated throughout both the upper and lower respiratory lectures.

Section 7.2

Q34. Why are T4 and T6 treated relatively early in this sequence (step 3), before the atlas (AA) or C4?

T4 and T6 represent the sympathetic innervation to the lung. Since pneumonia reflects a hyperparasympathetic-dominant state overall, addressing the sympathetic segments helps work toward rebalancing the autonomic tone — consistent with the "treat the sympathetics" priority established in Section 2.

Section 7.2

Q35. Which technique is highlighted as especially versatile in this case — usable on muscles, bones, articulations, and fascia — and applied to the hypertonic thoracic paraspinal muscles?

Facilitated positional release (FPR) is highlighted as especially versatile, since it can be used on muscles, bones, articulations, and fascia — making it a good choice for the hypertonic thoracic paraspinal muscles and iliocostalis in this case.

Section 7.2

Q36. The tender points in the bilateral pectoralis major and right anterior scalene are treated last (step 8) in this sequence, with counterstrain if still needed. What does "if still needed" imply about this step?

Treating tender points last, "if still needed," reflects that addressing the underlying somatic dysfunctions earlier in the sequence may resolve some of the secondary tenderness in these accessory muscles — counterstrain is reserved for whatever tenderness persists by that point.
