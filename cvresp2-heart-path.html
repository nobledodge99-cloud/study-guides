Part 1 — Hypertensive Heart Disease & Pericardial Disease

1. Hypertensive Heart Disease: Epidemiology & Diagnosis

Epidemiology

  • ~25% of the US population has clinical hypertension (some estimates run higher, 30–50%)
  • Hypertensive heart disease (HHD) is the second most common cause of cardiac death, directly causing ~7,000 deaths/year
  • Compensated HHD may be asymptomatic, discovered incidentally on routine EKG or echocardiogram
  • Atrial fibrillation or CHF leading to cardiac dilation may be the first clinical sign of HHD
  • HHD is a major risk factor accelerating coronary artery disease (CAD)
  • Most untreated hypertensive patients (30–50%) die of HHD; the remainder die of stroke, renovascular disease, or vascular complications (aneurysms)

Diagnostic Criteria for HHD

The diagnosis of HHD requires both of the following:
  1. Left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) in the absence of any other cardiovascular pathology that could induce it (e.g., aortic stenosis, coarctation of the aorta) — this necessarily excludes hypertrophic cardiomyopathy as a cause
  2. A clinical history of hypertension, defined as blood pressure ≥140/90 mmHg
Board Tip HHD is a diagnosis of exclusion for the cause of LVH — you must rule out other structural causes (AS, coarctation, HCM) before attributing hypertrophy to chronic hypertension.
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Which of the following combinations is required to establish a diagnosis of hypertensive heart disease?

Explanation: HHD requires both LVH that cannot be explained by another cardiovascular cause (aortic stenosis, coarctation, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy) AND a clinical history of hypertension (BP >=140/90 mmHg). Neither finding alone is sufficient.

2. A patient is found to have left ventricular hypertrophy on echocardiogram. Which additional finding would exclude a diagnosis of hypertensive heart disease as the cause?

Explanation: Because HHD is a diagnosis of exclusion, finding another explanation for LVH (aortic stenosis, coarctation of the aorta, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy) rules out HHD as the underlying cause, even if the patient also has hypertension.

3. Which statement about the clinical presentation of compensated hypertensive heart disease is most accurate?

Explanation: Compensated HHD is often clinically silent and discovered incidentally on routine cardiac testing; atrial fibrillation or CHF with cardiac dilation may be the first overt clinical sign that prompts further workup.

2. HHD: Morphology & Clinical Outcomes

Gross Pathology

FindingCriteria
Left ventricular hypertrophyWall thickness ≥2 cm
Heart weight≥500 g
Left atrial enlargementOccurs secondarily, due to the stiffness of the hypertrophied LV impairing diastolic filling
Guiding Question Answered Chronic pressure overload from hypertension causes concentric LV hypertrophy (wall thickening without early chamber enlargement) — this is why ventricular dilation is not a feature of early/compensated left-sided HHD. The stiff, hypertrophied, poorly compliant LV then raises left atrial pressure, secondarily enlarging the left atrium as it works harder against impaired diastolic filling.

Microscopic Findings

  • Early: myocyte enlargement (hypertrophy)
  • Late: increased interstitial fibrosis and irregular enlargement of myocytes

Clinical Outcomes

  • Outcome severity ranges from a normal lifespan to severe ischemic heart disease (IHD) complicated by CAD, renal injury, and/or stroke
  • Risk of sudden cardiac death (SCD) is increased
  • Effective blood pressure control is the key to preventing or regressing the hypertrophy — lifelong medication adherence is essential
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. What is the minimum left ventricular wall thickness used as a gross pathologic criterion for LVH in hypertensive heart disease?

Explanation: A left ventricular wall thickness of 2 cm or more is the gross pathologic threshold used to define LVH in the context of HHD.

2. Why does left atrial enlargement occur as a secondary consequence of hypertensive left ventricular hypertrophy?

Explanation: The hypertrophied LV becomes stiff and poorly compliant, impairing diastolic filling. This raises pressure in the left atrium, which enlarges secondarily as it works against this impaired filling — not from direct atrial hypertrophy.

3. Why is ventricular dilation not typically seen in early, compensated left-sided hypertensive heart disease?

Explanation: Chronic hypertension imposes a pressure overload on the left ventricle, which responds with concentric hypertrophy (wall thickening into a smaller chamber) rather than dilation. Dilation is a feature of decompensation/later-stage disease, not the early compensated phase.

3. Cor Pulmonale (Right-Sided HHD)

Cor pulmonale = isolated pulmonary hypertensive heart disease, stemming from right ventricular pressure overload. Chronic cor pulmonale is characterized by right ventricular hypertrophy, dilation, and potentially right-sided heart failure.
Important Distinction Right-sided HHD is usually associated with left-sided heart disease (pulmonary hypertension most commonly occurs as a complication of left heart disease). Isolated cor pulmonale specifically means the right-sided pathology stems from a primary pulmonary problem, not from left heart disease.

Acute vs. Chronic Cor Pulmonale

Guiding Question: How Lung Disease Leads to Heart Disease

Chronic lung pathology (parenchymal destruction, vascular obliteration, or chest-wall restriction) increases pulmonary vascular resistance → the right ventricle faces chronically elevated afterload → RV pressure overload → RV hypertrophy → eventual RV dilation and failure (cor pulmonale). This is a purely mechanical/pressure-based pathway distinct from left-sided HHD.

Summary

  • HHD affects either or both ventricles
  • Cor pulmonale is due specifically to right-sided disease
  • Hypertension and HHD (in both forms) are major causes of morbidity and mortality; diagnosis and treatment remain the mainstays of management
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A patient with severe COPD develops right ventricular hypertrophy and dilation with no evidence of left-sided heart disease. What is this condition called?

Explanation: Cor pulmonale is isolated pulmonary hypertensive heart disease resulting from right ventricular pressure overload, classically caused by chronic lung disease such as COPD/emphysema, occurring independent of left-sided heart disease.

2. A patient develops sudden right heart strain shortly after a large pulmonary embolism. This is best classified as:

Explanation: Acute cor pulmonale classically follows a massive pulmonary embolism, causing sudden right ventricular pressure overload, in contrast to chronic cor pulmonale which develops gradually from chronic lung or pulmonary vascular disease.

3. Which statement correctly describes the relationship between pulmonary hypertension and left-sided heart disease?

Explanation: While isolated (primary) pulmonary hypertension causing cor pulmonale does occur, it should be remembered that pulmonary hypertension most commonly arises as a complication of left-sided heart disease, which backs up pressure into the pulmonary circulation.

4. Pericardial Effusion & Cardiac Tamponade

Normal Pericardium

The pericardial sac normally contains <50 mL of thin, clear, straw-colored fluid.

Types of Abnormal Pericardial Fluid

TermFluid TypeCommon Causes
Pericardial effusionSerous fluidCHF, pericardial tumor infiltration, hypoalbuminemia
HydropericardiumTransudateSevere CHF (transudation), uremia, advanced hypoproteinemia
HemopericardiumBloodExternal trauma (e.g., gunshot wound), myocardial rupture post-MI, retrograde aortic dissection, malignant tumors, bleeding diatheses/clotting defects
Purulent pericarditisPusInfectious (bacterial/fungal)

Chronic vs. Acute Accumulation — Why Volume Alone Doesn't Predict Severity

Slow/chronic accumulation: the pericardium has time to stretch and dilate, so effusions <500 mL may cause only a globular enlargement of the heart shadow on chest imaging, without hemodynamic compromise.
Rapid accumulation: as little as 150–400 mL (occasionally up to 1 L if slow) can cause cardiac tamponade — the thin-walled atria, vena cavae, and ventricles are compressed before the pericardium has time to stretch.
Board Tip It's the rate of accumulation, not just the volume, that determines whether an effusion causes tamponade. A slowly accumulating liter of fluid may be tolerated; 150–200 mL accumulating rapidly (e.g., from a ruptured MI or aortic dissection) can be fatal.

Cardiac Tamponade

  • Rapid and/or massive fluid accumulation compresses the heart, impairing venous return from the caval veins → poor diastolic filling → reduced cardiac output
  • Cardiac tamponade is usually due to blood — trauma, MI with myocardial rupture, or a ruptured dissecting aneurysm extending into the pericardial space

Classic Clinical Findings

FindingDetail
Beck's triadHypotension, jugular venous distension, muffled heart sounds
Pulsus paradoxusDecrease in systolic BP >10 mmHg with inspiration — an important exam finding suggesting tamponade
Mechanism of pulsus paradoxus: fluid compressing the heart impairs filling; during inspiration, increased venous return to the right heart causes the interventricular septum to bow toward (compress) the left ventricle, further decreasing LV preload and stroke volume — producing an exaggerated inspiratory drop in systolic BP.
Diagnostic Challenge No finding here is perfectly specific — a high index of clinical suspicion is essential to make the diagnosis.
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A patient develops sudden hemodynamic collapse after a motor vehicle accident, with only 200 mL of blood found in the pericardial space at autopsy. How is it possible that such a small volume caused death?

Explanation: It is the rate of accumulation, not just the total volume, that determines the hemodynamic impact. A rapidly accumulating 150-400 mL can cause fatal cardiac tamponade because the pericardium has no time to stretch, whereas a slowly accumulating liter or more may be tolerated.

2. A patient with suspected cardiac tamponade is found to have a drop in systolic blood pressure of 15 mmHg during inspiration. What is this finding called, and what is its mechanism?

Explanation: Pulsus paradoxus is defined as a drop in systolic BP >10 mmHg with inspiration. In tamponade, increased right heart venous return during inspiration causes the interventricular septum to bow toward the left ventricle, further reducing LV filling, preload, and stroke volume.

3. Which combination of findings constitutes Beck's triad, classically associated with cardiac tamponade?

Explanation: Beck's triad consists of hypotension, jugular venous distension, and muffled heart sounds, and is a classic (though not universally sensitive) finding in cardiac tamponade.

5. Pericarditis: Classification & Etiology

Definition

Pericarditis = inflammation of the (visceral) pericardium.

Three Ways to Classify Pericarditis

Classification AxisCategories
CourseAcute vs. chronic
OriginPrimary (in the pericardium) vs. secondary (result of some other process)
Character of exudateSerous, serofibrinous, fibrinous, purulent/suppurative, hemorrhagic

Etiology — By Category

CategoryExamples
InfectiousViruses (Coxsackie B, echovirus, influenza, mumps, EBV); bacteria (S. aureus, streptococci, H. influenzae); TB; fungi (especially in immunocompromised patients)
Immune-relatedRheumatic fever, SLE, scleroderma, post-cardiotomy syndrome, post-MI (Dressler syndrome), drug hypersensitivity
MiscellaneousUremia, myocardial infarction, neoplasia, trauma, radiation, surgery
Board Tip Acute pericarditis is idiopathic in 90% of cases. Uremia and MI are especially important causes to recognize clinically since they require careful, specific follow-up. Post-MI pericarditis with an immune-mediated, delayed presentation is Dressler syndrome.

Mesothelial Incidental Cardiac Excrescence (MICE)

  • A hypersensitivity-type pericarditis finding: accumulations of fibrinous material infiltrating the pericardium, sometimes forming villus-like structures that can impede cardiac function
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. What percentage of acute pericarditis cases are idiopathic?

Explanation: Acute pericarditis is idiopathic in approximately 90% of cases, though viral, bacterial, uremic, post-MI, and autoimmune causes should always be considered.

2. A patient develops pericarditis several weeks after a myocardial infarction, thought to be immune-mediated. What is this condition called?

Explanation: Dressler syndrome is a delayed, immune-mediated pericarditis occurring weeks after myocardial infarction, distinct from the early post-infarct pericarditis that can occur from direct injury.

3. Pericarditis can be classified along which three independent axes?

Explanation: Pericarditis is classified by its course (acute vs. chronic), whether it is primary to the pericardium or secondary to another process, and the character of the inflammatory exudate (serous, serofibrinous, fibrinous, purulent/suppurative, or hemorrhagic).

📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. An autopsy specimen shows fibrin strands on the pericardial surfaces described grossly as 'bread and butter' pericarditis. Which type of acute pericarditis does this represent, and what are its typical causes?

Explanation: 'Bread and butter' pericarditis is the classic gross description of serofibrinous pericarditis, with fibrin strands in straw-colored serous fluid, typically caused by viral infection or uremia.

2. A pericardial biopsy shows caseating granulomas with extensive fibrosis and a thick, cheese-like exudate. What is the most likely underlying cause, and what serious long-term complication can result?

Explanation: Caseating granulomas with a thick, cheesy exudate are characteristic of tuberculous pericarditis, which carries a significant risk of progressing to chronic constrictive pericarditis due to extensive fibrosis.

3. Hemorrhagic pericarditis found on autopsy with tumor cells suspended in the pericardial fluid is most frequently associated with metastasis from which primary malignancy?

Explanation: Hemorrhagic pericarditis is often due to tumor invasion, most frequently extending from carcinomas of the lung, with tumor cells often identifiable suspended in the bloody pericardial fluid.

7. Chronic Pericarditis & Evolution/Sequelae

Chronic (Constrictive) Pericarditis

  • Acute pericarditis may become chronic, with adhesions forming between the visceral and parietal layers
  • Constrictive pericarditis: marked fibrosis causing restriction of cardiac motion; often related to previous infection, radiation therapy, or heart surgery
Guiding Question: Constrictive Pericarditis vs. Restrictive Cardiomyopathy These two conditions present very similarly (both impair diastolic filling), and distinguishing them is a classic board topic covered in more depth in Part III (Cardiomyopathies) of this series. Key discriminators to know once you reach that material: constrictive pericarditis has a rigid, thickened/calcified pericardium (often visible on imaging) with preserved myocardium, while restrictive cardiomyopathy involves the myocardium itself (infiltrative disease) with a normal pericardium.

Severe Chronic Constrictive Pericarditis (Classically Post-TB)

FindingDescription
Pericardial sac obliterationCan develop significant caval constriction and severe systemic venous congestion
Pick's diseaseHepatomegaly and ascites resulting from this severe caval constriction/congestion
Concretio cordisFocal or diffuse calcification of the constricted pericardium

Evolution & Sequelae of Pericardial Inflammation

1. Total resolution — typical after mild serous or fibrinous pericarditis
2. Organization with focal fibrous strands (adhesions) — produces focal opacity and fibrosis
3. Fibrous obliteration of the pericardial cavity — most commonly seen after major cardiac surgery
4. Suppurative and tuberculous pericarditis can also produce adhesions to the pleura and/or mediastinum
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A patient with a history of tuberculous pericarditis develops hepatomegaly and ascites due to severe caval constriction from pericardial scarring. What is this specific presentation called?

Explanation: Pick's disease refers to the hepatomegaly and ascites that result from severe caval constriction and systemic venous congestion caused by chronic constrictive pericarditis, classically following tuberculous infection.

2. Imaging of a patient with long-standing constrictive pericarditis reveals focal calcification of the pericardium. What is this finding called?

Explanation: Concretio cordis refers to focal or diffuse calcification of the chronically constricted, fibrotic pericardium.

3. Which sequela of pericardial inflammation is most commonly associated with major cardiac surgery rather than infection?

Explanation: Fibrous obliteration of the pericardial cavity is most commonly seen following major cardiac surgery, as opposed to adhesions to the pleura/mediastinum, which are more typical after suppurative or tuberculous pericarditis.

Part 2 — Valvular Heart Disease & Tumors

1. Cardiac Valve Anatomy & Valvular Dysfunction Overview

Anatomy

  • Cardiac valves are leaflets of fibrous connective tissue anchored to the heart by the fibrous skeleton (a central connective tissue structure that all four valves attach to, roughly in the same plane)
  • Valves usually have three leaflets/cusps — except the mitral valve, which has only two

Two Fundamental Types of Valvular Dysfunction

LesionHemodynamic ConsequenceVentricular Response
Stenosis (narrowed opening)Pressure overloadConcentric hypertrophy
Regurgitation/Insufficiency (incompetence, backflow)Volume overloadEccentric hypertrophy/dilatation
Both stenosis and regurgitation can ultimately culminate in heart failure.

Functional vs. Primary Regurgitation

Key Distinction Functional regurgitation is valve incompetence stemming from an abnormality of a support structure (e.g., papillary muscle, annulus, ventricular geometry) — not a primary valve leaflet defect. Functional mitral regurgitation is particularly common and clinically important in ischemic heart disease and dilated cardiomyopathy.
  • Can be acute (e.g., infective endocarditis) or chronic (e.g., rheumatic heart disease)
  • Chronic valvular disease is often well tolerated (time to compensate); acute valvular disease is poorly tolerated

Major Etiologies of Acquired Heart Valve Disease

Valve/LesionCommon Causes
Aortic stenosisRheumatic heart disease; senile calcific aortic stenosis; calcification of a congenitally deformed (bicuspid) valve
Mitral regurgitation (leaflet abnormality)Post-inflammatory scarring (RHD), infective endocarditis, mitral valve prolapse, drugs
Mitral regurgitation (tensor apparatus abnormality)Papillary muscle rupture or fibrosis/dysfunction, chordae tendineae rupture
Aortic regurgitation (aortic dilation)Degenerative aortic dilation, syphilitic aortitis, ankylosing spondylitis, rheumatoid arthritis, Marfan syndrome
Mitral regurgitation (LV/annulus abnormality)LV enlargement, myocarditis, dilated cardiomyopathy, mitral annular calcification
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Which cardiac valve is unique in having only two leaflets rather than the typical three?

Explanation: The mitral valve is the exception among cardiac valves, having only two leaflets (bicuspid), while the aortic, pulmonary, and tricuspid valves each normally have three.

2. A patient has functional mitral regurgitation in the setting of dilated cardiomyopathy, with structurally normal valve leaflets. What is the underlying mechanism?

Explanation: Functional regurgitation arises from an abnormality in a valve's support structures (annulus, papillary muscles, ventricular geometry) rather than a primary defect in the leaflets themselves, and is particularly common in ischemic heart disease and dilated cardiomyopathy.

3. Why is chronic valvular disease generally better tolerated than acute valvular disease?

Explanation: Chronic valvular lesions allow the heart time to develop compensatory changes (concentric or eccentric hypertrophy) that maintain cardiac output, whereas acute lesions (e.g., acute infective endocarditis with valve destruction) do not allow this adaptation, making them poorly tolerated.

2. Aortic Stenosis

Calcific (Senile/Degenerative) Aortic Stenosis

  • Most common of all valvular lesions — most aortic stenosis is not rheumatic in origin, but calcific/degenerative
  • Patients are typically in the 7th–9th decade, usually without a history of RHD ("wear and tear")
  • Mitral valve is usually uninvolved
  • May result in calcium emboli

Clinical Physiology of Aortic Stenosis

AS → concentric LV hypertrophy and eventual dilatation → pressure overload with narrow pulse pressure → systolic crescendo-decrescendo murmur → reduced peripheral/coronary perfusion. Increased diastolic pressure from concentric LVH → pulmonary edema and dyspnea.
Board Tip — Prognosis Without Treatment Poor prognosis once symptomatic: sudden death ~3 years after syncope onset, ~5 years after angina onset. Treatment is surgical valve replacement.

📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A 78-year-old with no history of rheumatic fever is found to have severe aortic stenosis with a structurally normal, uninvolved mitral valve. What is the most likely etiology?

Explanation: Calcific/senile aortic stenosis is the most common valvular lesion overall, typically affecting patients in the 7th-9th decade without a history of RHD, and classically spares the mitral valve, unlike rheumatic AS which usually co-occurs with mitral involvement.

2. What proportion of aortic stenosis cases in adults is attributable to an underlying bicuspid aortic valve?

Explanation: A bicuspid aortic valve, though present in only 1-2% of the population, is responsible for approximately 50% of aortic stenosis cases in adults, reflecting the valve's predisposition to premature calcification and degeneration.

3. Which statement about discrete subaortic stenosis is correct?

Explanation: Discrete subaortic stenosis presents as a gray-white fibroelastic endocardial ridge just below the aortic valve and accounts for 8-10% of all congenital outflow obstructions, distinct from muscular subaortic stenosis (due to HCM) and supravalvular stenosis (an arterial dysplasia).

3. Aortic Insufficiency & Mitral Stenosis

Aortic Insufficiency (Regurgitation)

  • Causes LV hypertrophy and dilatation — this is a volume overload lesion
  • Wide pulse pressure ("bounding pulse") with failure to maintain normal diastolic pressure
  • Early blowing diastolic murmur
  • May cause coronary insufficiency and angina; sudden death due to impaired coronary filling (coronary perfusion occurs during diastole, which is compromised)

Mitral Stenosis

Impaired LV filling → left atrial hypertrophy and dilatation → pulmonary congestion → RV hypertrophy/dilatation → increased venous pressure and decreased peripheral perfusion.
  • No LV hypertrophy in pure mitral stenosis (the LV is actually somewhat "protected" from the elevated pressure, which stays upstream in the LA)
  • Rigid, stenotic valves are often also insufficient (mixed disease)
  • Enlarged, poorly contracting atria (especially the atrial appendage) may harbor mural thrombi → risk of embolization if they break off
  • Auscultation: opening snap followed by a diastolic rumble

Causes of Mitral Stenosis

Rheumatic Mitral Stenosis — Clinical Pattern

  • Predominantly affects women, in the 4th–5th decades
  • In patients from endemic regions, presentation is earlier — likely due to recurrent rheumatic fever attacks
  • Rarely, calcific degeneration alone can cause mitral stenosis in the elderly
  • Atrial fibrillation is a common sequela and carries a much higher thromboembolism risk than non-valvular AFib
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A patient with severe aortic regurgitation develops angina and is at risk for sudden death. What is the mechanism behind the impaired coronary perfusion in this condition?

Explanation: Coronary artery perfusion occurs predominantly during diastole. In aortic regurgitation, the wide pulse pressure and failure to maintain normal diastolic pressure impair this diastolic coronary filling, predisposing to ischemia and sudden death.

2. Why is left ventricular hypertrophy typically absent in pure mitral stenosis?

Explanation: In pure mitral stenosis, the obstruction is proximal to the left ventricle, so the elevated pressure backs up into the left atrium (causing LA hypertrophy/dilation) rather than imposing a direct pressure or volume load on the LV itself, which is why LVH is characteristically absent.

3. Atrial fibrillation occurring as a complication of mitral stenosis carries which risk compared to non-valvular atrial fibrillation?

Explanation: Atrial fibrillation in the setting of mitral stenosis carries a substantially higher risk of thromboembolism compared to non-valvular atrial fibrillation, related to the enlarged, poorly contracting left atrium and its propensity to form mural thrombi, especially in the atrial appendage.

4. Mitral Insufficiency & Mitral Valve Prolapse

Mitral Insufficiency (Regurgitation) — Pathophysiology

  • Generally progressive; increases LV preload; afterload is unchanged or reduced
  • Chronic severe MR: symptoms or LV dysfunction develop within 6–10 years
  • Eccentric hypertrophy accommodates the increased LV filling volume and maintains forward stroke volume — until increased left atrial pressure eventually causes dyspnea, pulmonary hypertension, and (with progressive LA dilation) atrial fibrillation

The "floppy valve" — the most common cause of mitral regurgitation and one of the most common cardiac disorders overall (3–5% prevalence). Usually discovered incidentally between ages 20–40 when a mid-systolic click is heard.

6. Rheumatic Heart Disease: Pathogenesis & Immunology

Rheumatic Fever (RF) Basics

RHD involves pancarditis — inflammation of all layers of the heart. Acute rheumatic fever follows a Group A β-hemolytic streptococcal pharyngeal infection by 10 days to 6 weeks (roughly ~2 weeks on average). A major problem in the developing world.
  • Autoimmune disease affecting heart, joints, skin, and brain via molecular mimicry — bacterial M protein cross-reacts with host tissue
  • CD4+ T cell infiltration occurs in valvular tissue
  • By the time RHD/RF develops clinically, throat cultures for strep are usually negative — the acute infection has already resolved

Explanation: Rheumatic fever develops 10 days to 6 weeks after the inciting streptococcal pharyngitis, by which point the acute infection has typically resolved, so throat cultures are usually negative at the time RF/RHD becomes clinically apparent.

1. Cardiomyopathy: Definition & Classification

Cardiomyopathy = heart muscle disease of a non-inflammatory nature — myocardial disease causing clinical dysfunction without hypertensive, congenital, valvular, atherosclerotic, or pericardial derangements. In other words, these other causes must first be excluded/absent for a primary cardiomyopathy diagnosis.

Why the EF Differs DCM impairs the heart's ability to contract, so EF (a measure of systolic emptying) drops. 📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Which classic genetic mutation is most commonly identified in dilated cardiomyopathy?

Explanation: Titin mutations are the most common identified genetic cause of dilated cardiomyopathy, distinct from the beta-myosin heavy chain mutations classically associated with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and transthyretin mutations associated with restrictive (amyloid) cardiomyopathy.

2. A patient with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy has a measured LVEF of 70%, yet still has significant heart failure symptoms. How is this apparent paradox explained?

Explanation: HCM impairs ventricular relaxation and filling (diastolic dysfunction) rather than contraction, so the fraction of blood ejected from whatever volume fills the ventricle remains normal or even supranormal, even though overall cardiac output and symptoms reflect the underlying filling problem.

3. By definition, a diagnosis of primary cardiomyopathy requires the exclusion of which of the following as the cause of myocardial dysfunction?

Explanation: Cardiomyopathy is defined as myocardial disease causing dysfunction in the absence of hypertensive, congenital, valvular, atherosclerotic, or pericardial disease -- these must be excluded before attributing dysfunction to a primary cardiomyopathy.

1. CHF: Definition, Epidemiology & Pathophysiologic Consequences

Definition

CHF = inability of the heart to pump blood to meet tissue demands, or the ability to do so only with elevated filling pressure. Excludes other processes that indirectly affect the heart's pumping ability (e.g., blood loss). Usually low-output failure; high-output failure is less common, seen in states of high tissue demand.
  • May involve the left side, right side, or both; can result from chronic heart disease
  • Left-sided failure is more common

Epidemiology

Affects >5 million people/year in the US (~2% of the population); causes >1 million hospitalizations/year; primary or contributing cause of death in an estimated 300,000 people/year. The leading discharge diagnosis in patients >65; annual cost ~$18 billion.

Explanation: Compensated heart failure reflects adaptive changes that successfully meet metabolic demand, while decompensated heart failure occurs when the harmful effects of those same compensatory changes (e.g., excessive hypertrophy, fibrosis) begin to compromise the heart, causing cardiac output to drop.

Fetal Gene Re-Expression

Hemodynamic overload-induced hypertrophy causes re-expression of dormant fetal genes. Atrial natriuretic factor (ANF) is expressed in the fetal ventricle and atrium, but after birth its production is normally restricted to the atrium only. In a hypertrophic ventricle, however, ANF and brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) are abundantly re-expressed.
  • ANF/BNP reduce hemodynamic overload via effects on salt and water metabolism
  • These peptides can be measured to help differentiate CHF from lung disease
  • Natriuretic peptides downregulate the RAAS, decrease sympathetic activity in heart/kidney, increase renal blood flow, and increase renal sodium excretion (direct effect on the collecting duct)

The Multinational BNP Study — Test Characteristics

At a cutoff of 100 pg/mL, the point-of-care BNP test for CHF has: Sensitivity 90%, Specificity 76%, PPV 79%, NPV 89%.
  • Patients with a history of ventricular dysfunction had higher BNP levels; levels also correlated with more severe failure
Clinical Use Reserve the BNP test to differentiate cardiac vs. pulmonary causes of dyspnea when the diagnosis is unclear. Do not routinely measure BNP in patients with typical, clear signs and symptoms of heart failure.

Explanation: The subendocardial zone is the least perfused region of the myocardium because it lies farthest along the pressure gradient from the epicardial coronary arteries, making it the most vulnerable to any reduction in coronary flow, even before a complete occlusion occurs.

2. A patient presents with an EKG showing ST-segment elevation. Which type of myocardial infarction does this most likely represent, and what is the corresponding extent of myocardial involvement?

Explanation: ST-segment elevation (STEMI) reflects a transmural, full-thickness myocardial infarction, since the entire wall thickness must be involved to produce this characteristic EKG finding, in contrast to NSTEMI, which typically reflects a subendocardial (partial-thickness) infarct.

3. A subendocardial infarct is defined as involving up to what fraction of the inner myocardial wall thickness before it is considered to have evolved into a transmural infarct?

Explanation: A subendocardial infarct involves up to one-third of the inner (subendocardial) wall thickness; beyond this, it can evolve into a full transmural (full-thickness) infarction.

7. Transmural MI: Causes, Vessel Anatomy & Outcomes

Causes of Transmural Infarction

Almost always caused by a combination of: marked stenosis (>90% occlusion of at least one coronary; most patients have 2–3 narrowed arteries with >75% stenosis) plus thrombosis over plaque fissure/rupture/hemorrhage — found in 90–95% of AMIs.
  • Platelet activation and aggregation release thromboxane and other vasoactive products → vasospasm

Other (Less Common) Precipitants of Transmural Infarction

  • Hypotensive crisis (spinal anesthesia, shock)
  • Marked tachycardia
  • Coronary emboli (including as a complication of transluminal coronary angioplasty)
  • Thrombosis from arteritis, aortic dissection, trauma

Coronary Artery Territory Mapping

RCA (right coronary artery)30–40%Posterior/"inferior"/diaphragmatic wall, including posterior 1/3 of the septum (most common pattern of right-dominant circulation)
LCx (left circumflex)15–20%Posterolateral wall
Board Tip Posterior/posterolateral infarcts (RCA or LCx territory) are notoriously difficult to detect on standard EKG compared to anterior (LAD) infarcts.
  • Intermittent ischemia with several "ages" of infarct (some old, some new) may occur in the same patient

Extension & Special Patterns

  • Transmural infarct primarily involves the left ventricle
  • A posterior septal infarct may extend into the posterior right ventricle
  • Atrial infarction occurs in 1–2% of AMIs, typically with a large posterior LV infarct

📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Occlusion of which coronary artery is responsible for the largest proportion (40-50%) of transmural myocardial infarctions?

Explanation: The LAD is responsible for approximately 40-50% of transmural MIs, supplying the anterior wall and anterior two-thirds of the septum, making it the single most commonly implicated vessel.

2. Why are posterior and posterolateral myocardial infarctions (from RCA or LCx occlusion) often more difficult to detect on a standard EKG compared to anterior infarcts?

Explanation: Posterior and posterolateral infarcts, resulting from RCA or LCx occlusion respectively, are notoriously more difficult to detect on standard EKG because these territories are less directly visualized by the standard 12-lead views compared to the anterior wall territory supplied by the LAD.

3. What is the approximate critical time window after coronary occlusion before a zone of irreversible necrosis begins to develop, emphasizing the urgency of early treatment?

Explanation: The zone of necrosis begins to develop at approximately 2 hours after coronary occlusion, which is why immediate treatment (e.g., aspirin) and rapid transport to the hospital within this critical window are emphasized to salvage myocardium.

This timeline is heavily tested — memorize the sequence and rough time windows.

The Danger Window The period of hyperemic border with central soft, necrotic tissue (roughly days 3–10) is when the infarct is mechanically weakest and most prone to rupture — this is the most dangerous time for the patient, clinically speaking, even though the "worst" symptoms often occurred days earlier.

Other classic microscopic clues along the way: wavy myofibers (an early sign of ischemic stress, sometimes visible before frank necrosis), interstitial edema, and progressive neutrophil → macrophage/lymphocyte transition as healing proceeds.
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A myocardial infarction specimen examined grossly shows a hyperemic border surrounding central yellow-brown, soft tissue. Approximately how many days post-infarction does this correspond to, and why is this a clinically dangerous period?

Explanation: The hyperemic border with central soft, yellow-brown necrotic tissue corresponds to approximately days 3-7 post-infarction, a period during which the infarcted tissue is mechanically weakest and most prone to rupture, making this the most clinically dangerous window.

2. On microscopic examination, coagulative necrosis with loss of nuclei and cross-striations, along with a brisk neutrophilic infiltrate, is most characteristic of which time window post-infarction?

Explanation: Coagulative necrosis with loss of nuclei and cross-striations, accompanied by a brisk neutrophil infiltrate, is characteristic of the 1-3 day window following myocardial infarction.

3. The appearance of granulation tissue with plump fibroblasts, new collagen, and blood vessels at the infarct margin is most characteristic of which time period?

Explanation: Granulation tissue formation, with plump fibroblasts, new collagen deposition, and new blood vessel formation, is characteristic of the 7-10 day window, as the healing process transitions from acute inflammation toward scar formation.

9. MI Biomarkers

The most sensitive and specific biomarkers of myocardial damage are the cardiac-specific proteins — particularly cardiac troponin T (cTnT) and cardiac troponin I (cTnI).
BiomarkerTime to ElevationPeakReturn to Normal
CK-MB3–12 hours (classically ~4–6 hr)~24 hours48–72 hours
Troponin I (cTnI)3–12 hours (classically ~2–4 hr)~24 hours5–10 days
Troponin T (cTnT)3–12 hours~24 hours5–14 days (longer-acting than troponin I)
Board Tip All three biomarkers rise within a similar early window (3–12 hours) and peak around 24 hours — the key differentiator tested is how long each stays elevated: CK-MB clears fastest (2–3 days), troponin I intermediate (5–10 days), and troponin T longest (5–14 days). This matters clinically for detecting a re-infarction — a re-rise in CK-MB (which clears quickly) can signal a new event more reliably than troponin, which may still be elevated from the original infarct for over a week.

Troponins are released from damaged/necrotic heart muscle cells at the time of injury.

📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Which cardiac biomarker returns to normal fastest after a myocardial infarction, making it clinically useful for detecting a subsequent re-infarction?

Explanation: CK-MB returns to normal fastest, within 48-72 hours, compared to troponin I (5-10 days) and troponin T (5-14 days). This faster clearance makes CK-MB clinically useful for detecting a new (re-infarction) event in a patient whose troponin might still be elevated from an initial infarct.

2. A patient's troponin I level remains elevated 6 days after an initial myocardial infarction. Is this expected, and why?

Explanation: Troponin I remains elevated for approximately 5-10 days following myocardial infarction before returning to baseline, so a level still elevated at day 6 is expected and does not necessarily indicate a new event.

3. What are the two most sensitive and specific biomarkers of myocardial damage?

Explanation: Cardiac troponin T (cTnT) and cardiac troponin I (cTnI), the cardiac-specific proteins, are the most sensitive and specific biomarkers of myocardial damage, superior to CK-MB in specificity.

Complications Specific to Transmural MI

Myocardial rupture occurs between days 2–10 post-infarct — this window aligns with the mechanically weak, softened necrotic tissue phase described in the evolution timeline.
Rupture SiteFrequencyConsequence
Free wall90%Massive cardiac tamponade → usually death (hemopericardium)
Interventricular septum10%Left-to-right shunt (acquired VSD)
  • Ventricular aneurysm may form with large infarcts (a healed, thinned, bulging wall segment)
  • Myocardial rupture accounts for ~10% of deaths after AMI in hospitalized patients
  • More common in elderly patients having their first infarct — most are women
  • Rarely, a ruptured ventricle becomes walled off and the patient survives with a false (pseudo) aneurysm

Reperfusion Injury

Reperfusion of previously ischemic myocardium can itself cause injury — leading to hypercontraction and necrosis. Most prominent after thrombolytic therapy or following prolonged cardiopulmonary bypass.
  • Mechanisms: hemorrhage compressing microvasculature, excess calcium influx, explosive cellular edema, neutrophil-derived free radicals, complement membrane attack complex (C5–C9)
  • Microscopically: fragmented myocytes, considerable edema, and further necrosis/destruction
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Rupture of the free wall of the left ventricle after myocardial infarction most often leads to which fatal complication?

Explanation: Free wall rupture (accounting for 90% of MI ruptures) most often leads to hemopericardium and death from cardiac tamponade, as blood rapidly fills the pericardial space and compresses the heart.

2. Myocardial rupture after an acute MI is most classically seen in which patient population, and during what time window?

Explanation: Myocardial rupture classically occurs in elderly patients sustaining their first infarct (most of whom are women), during the days 2-10 window when the infarcted tissue is mechanically weakest.

3. Reperfusion injury following thrombolytic therapy is characterized by which of the following mechanisms?

Explanation: Reperfusion injury results from several mechanisms including excess calcium influx causing explosive cellular edema, free radical generation from neutrophils, and complement activation forming the membrane attack complex (C5-9), all of which can cause further myocardial injury despite restored blood flow.

11. Angina Pectoris, Chronic Ischemic Heart Disease & Sudden Cardiac Death

Three Patterns of Angina Pectoris

PatternTrigger/ReliefMechanism
Stable anginaProvoked by exertion or tachycardia; relieved by restFixed coronary stenosis
Prinzmetal's (variant) anginaOccurs at rest; relieved by vasodilatorsCoronary vasospasm
Unstable (crescendo) anginaIntense, prolonged, sometimes worsening painFindings are often subclinical — patient may feel fine intermittently and delay seeking care, which is dangerous

Chronic Ischemic Heart Disease

A diagnosis of exclusion — essentially a "specific cardiomyopathy" caused by significant coronary artery stenosis. Insidious and progressive, due to prior MI or chronic low-grade ischemia, with onset/development of EKG changes and CHF over time. Seen in older patients with a history of angina pectoris or prior MI; can be accelerated by subsequent infarcts.

Sudden Cardiac Death (SCD)

Defined as death within 24 hours of symptom onset.
  • The most common cause of SCD is chronic IHD, often with no significant acute myocardial damage identified at autopsy
  • Chronic IHD in this context is due to marked coronary atherosclerosis (>90% occlusion) and plaque rupture
  • Classic scenario: a patient starts a heavy exercise regimen after years of inactivity and drops dead

SCD in Younger Patients — Different Causes

In younger patients, consider: hereditary/congenital coronary artery abnormalities, mitral valve prolapse, myocarditis, sarcoidosis, dilated or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, pulmonary hypertension, and myocardial hypertrophy in athletes.
  • The lethal terminal arrhythmia in SCD is asystole or ventricular fibrillation
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A patient experiences chest pain at rest that resolves with administration of a vasodilator, without any relationship to exertion. Which pattern of angina does this represent, and what is the underlying mechanism?

Explanation: Prinzmetal's (variant) angina occurs at rest and is relieved by vasodilators, reflecting an underlying mechanism of coronary artery vasospasm rather than fixed stenosis.

2. A young athlete dies suddenly during a marathon. Autopsy is unremarkable for coronary atherosclerosis. Which of the following is a recognized cause of sudden cardiac death specifically in younger patients?

Explanation: In younger patients, sudden cardiac death is more likely due to causes such as mitral valve prolapse, congenital coronary abnormalities, myocarditis, sarcoidosis, cardiomyopathies, or athletic myocardial hypertrophy, rather than the chronic atherosclerotic IHD that predominates in older patients.

3. What is the most common overall cause of sudden cardiac death, and what finding is often notably absent at autopsy in these cases?

Explanation: Chronic ischemic heart disease (from marked coronary atherosclerosis with plaque rupture) is the most common cause of sudden cardiac death, and notably, significant acute myocardial damage is often absent at autopsy despite the severe underlying chronic disease.

Connecting the Parts
• A patient with Part 2's mitral stenosis develops Part 1's pulmonary congestion, which can progress to Part 4's right-sided heart failure and eventually resemble features of Part 1's cor pulmonale physiology (though the underlying cause here is left-sided, not primary lung disease).

• Part 2's rheumatic heart disease damages a valve; if that patient later develops a deformed valve colonized by bacteria, they've moved into Part 2's infective endocarditis territory — and if vegetations embolize or destroy the valve badly enough, acute regurgitation can precipitate Part 4's acute decompensated CHF.

• Part 3's dilated cardiomyopathy and Part 4's ischemic heart disease can look clinically similar (both cause systolic CHF) — the key distinguishing test is coronary angiography; chronic IHD is a "diagnosis of exclusion" specifically because it must be distinguished from primary cardiomyopathy.

• Part 3's myocarditis and Part 4's myocardial infarction both show neutrophilic infiltration early on, but only myocarditis represents true inflammation — ischemic damage must always be excluded before diagnosing myocarditis.
