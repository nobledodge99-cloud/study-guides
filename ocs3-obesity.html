00. Overview & Learning Objectives

  • Recognize the overweight/obesity crisis and how it may affect future patients and practice
  • Define how the overweight/obesity crisis has progressed and where it may eventually lead
  • Recognize how physical activity and exercise relate to an improved lifestyle, particularly the role they play in weight management
  • Understand and describe the physician's role in assisting patients with this problem
Framing: The overweight/obesity crisis is described as having become an epidemic much like COVID did — one that will affect nearly every physician's practice, regardless of specialty.
Practice Question

01. Body Mass Index & Classification

Body Mass Index (BMI) is calculated from a person's height and weight and used to estimate body fat percentage and categorize patients into weight groups.

Formula: BMI = (weight in pounds ÷ height in inches²) × 703

Watch out: The standard BMI classification chart applies to adults 20 years of age and older. Pediatric patients require a separate, age/sex-specific growth chart — always confirm you are using the correct chart for the patient's age group.
ClassificationBMI (kg/m²)
Underweight< 18.5
Normal weight18.5 – 24.9
Overweight25 – 29.9
Obese30 – 39.9
Severe / morbid obesity≥ 40

02. Epidemiology: Scope of the Crisis

Applying the BMI classification to the U.S. adult population (20 years and older) reveals the scale of the problem:

Group% of U.S. Adults
Overweight or greater (BMI ≥ 25)76%
Obese (BMI 30 – 39.9)43%
Severely / morbidly obese (BMI ≥ 40)9%

CDC State-by-State Trends (2018 vs. 2022)

  • In 2018, Colorado was the only state with 20–24.9% of its population overweight/obese — the lowest category on the map.
  • By 2022, Colorado had shifted into the 25–29.9% category, alongside California, Vermont, and Rhode Island.
  • Every other state was at 30% or greater by 2022, with some states reaching 45–50%.
  • In just four years, the national trend shifted markedly upward — the epidemic is progressing rapidly.
Projection: At current trends, the population reaching an overweight/obese classification is projected to hit approximately 50% by 2030.
Practice Questions

03. Contributing Factors

  • Occupational shift: A marked decrease in high-activity occupations with a corresponding increase in low-activity, desk-based occupations. Medicine itself is an example — physicians once spent most of the day moving between patients; now, for every hour spent with a patient, an estimated two hours are spent on the computer.
  • Transportation & the built environment: Increased reliance on automobiles as suburbs have expanded; people drive short distances (even a few blocks to the grocery store) rather than walk or bike, and spend time sitting in traffic.
  • Sedentary childhood: School-aged children spend more time in sedentary activities — TV, computers, cell phones/social media — rather than playing outside. Documented by the World Health Organization.
  • Dietary shift: A move away from home-cooked, from-scratch meals toward fast food and processed/frozen meals, which are higher in calories, carbohydrates, and sugar. Dual-income households make convenience foods more appealing, since they take less time to prepare.
Practice Questions

04. Health Risks & Consequences

  • Cancer: Increased incidence of certain cancers, including endometrial cancer and breast cancer in women
  • Stroke: Increased incidence
  • Metabolic disease: Type 2 diabetes has become epidemic and is strongly tied to obesity
  • Cardiovascular disease: Increased hypertension, abnormal lipid levels, and resulting increase in myocardial infarction — both are reducible with weight loss
  • Chronic low-grade inflammation: Excess weight drives a chronic inflammatory state that contributes to the conditions above

Reproductive Issues in Overweight/Obese Women

Reproductive-aged women who are overweight or obese face a distinct set of elevated risks:

  • Irregular ovulation
  • Polycystic ovarian disease (PCOS)
  • Infertility
  • Higher incidence of miscarriage
  • Higher incidence of stillbirth and birth defects
  • Gestational hypertension
  • Gestational diabetes
  • Pre-eclampsia
  • Higher incidence of cesarean section, with higher intraoperative complications, excessive blood loss, post-operative infection, and wound breakdown
Practice Questions

05. Economic & Healthcare Burden

  • The World Health Organization estimates that 31% of adults and 80% of adolescents do not meet recommended daily physical activity levels.
  • This inactivity is projected to cost global public healthcare systems approximately $300 billion between 2020 and 2030.
  • In the U.S. alone, an overweight individual spends approximately $1,800/year more on healthcare, while an obese individual spends approximately $3,200/year more.
Take-home: With 43% of the U.S. population currently overweight/obese and projections nearing 50% by 2030, this represents a major, growing stress on the healthcare system — one best addressed proactively in the doctor's office.
Practice Questions

06. Treatment Framework: Energy Balance & Modalities

Weight management comes down to two levers:

  • Energy intake — addressed through dietary modification
  • Energy output — addressed through physical activity/exercise, i.e., becoming less sedentary

There are four treatment modalities, addressed in a specific order of preference:

  1. Exercise
  2. Diet
  3. Medications
  4. Surgery
Osteopathic principle: As osteopathic physicians, the goal is to treat the whole patient and, when possible, without medication. Exercise and diet are addressed first; medications and surgery are reserved for when those measures are insufficient or for specific populations (see Section 13).
Practice Questions

07. Exercise Prescription: METs & Intensity

Exercises are classified by their Metabolic Equivalent of Task (MET). One MET represents the energy expenditure of sitting quietly, equal to 3.5 mL O₂/kg/min, or approximately 1 kcal/kg/hour.

IntensityMET RangeExamples
Light< 3Sitting, desk work, walking slowly
Moderate3 – 6Vacuuming, yoga, doubles tennis, moderate weightlifting
Vigorous6 – 12Aerobic dancing, cycling 10–12 mph on the level, swimming, jogging 6–8 mph
Very vigorous> 12Mountain biking (~18 METs)
Distinction: Sitting on the couch watching TV is still "physical activity" (MET of 1), but it is not exercise. When counseling for weight management, the goal is at least moderate-to-vigorous intensity activity.

08. How Exercise Influences Weight

Clinical PatternThink AboutClinical Decision
Sedentary patient with increasing weightLow energy expenditureIncrease physical activity
Patient seeking weight lossEnergy intake + expenditureCombine activity with dietary modification
Weight loss followed by regainLong-term maintenanceSustain a higher weekly activity dose
Low fitness or limited enduranceAerobic conditioningBuild aerobic activity gradually
Low strength or lean massResistance exerciseAdd strength training

Two Mechanisms

  • Prevent weight gain: Multiple studies show an inverse relationship between exercise and BMI/total adiposity — the more (and more vigorously) one exercises, the lower the BMI and adiposity.
  • Assist weight loss: Exercise alone is generally insufficient — weight loss requires sufficient physical activity coupled with a reduction in energy intake (diet).
Don't forget the psychological component: Many patients with obesity carry psychological patterns from upbringing (e.g., being told to "clean your plate"). Addressing diet and exercise alone, without addressing these behavioral patterns, is often insufficient — this conversation belongs in the physician's office.
Practice Questions

09. Aerobic vs. Resistance Exercise

TypePrimary EffectsEffect on Weight
Aerobic exerciseIncreases oxygen utilization; increases cardiopulmonary fitnessMore effective for weight loss than resistance exercise
Resistance (anaerobic) exerciseIncreases basal metabolic rate (BMR), strength, and lean body massQuestionable/variable — may even increase weight, since lean muscle weighs more than adipose tissue
Why resistance exercise may not show scale change: An individual lifting heavy weight and building lean muscle mass may see their weight increase rather than decrease — the benefit of resistance training on weight comes indirectly, through a higher basal metabolic rate that burns more calories over time.

The American College of Cardiology recommends aerobic exercise as the primary modality, plus weight training approximately 3 days per week — this does not need to involve heavy loads; light resistance training is effective for this purpose. Both types of exercise provide complementary benefits and are important.

Practice Questions

10. How Much Exercise: Dosing Guidelines

  • General target: 1 hour/day, or 150–300 minutes/week of moderate-to-vigorous exercise
  • Vigorous-only alternative: 75–150 minutes/week if exercise is limited to vigorous-intensity activities
  • Long-term weight loss maintenance: A minimum of 200–300 minutes/week of moderate-to-vigorous exercise
  • Bouts: Exercise does not need to be one continuous session — short bouts throughout the day are effective, but each bout should be greater than 10 minutes to count
Counseling tip: When a patient says "I don't have time," reframe the prescription around achievable bouts (>10 minutes each) spread across the day rather than a single continuous session.
Practice Questions

11. Individual Variability in Response

Two patients with the same body build, given the identical exercise prescription, can have very different outcomes — one loses more weight than the other. Twin studies assigned to identical exercise prescriptions have demonstrated this same variability between twins.

  • Biologic and genetic factors contribute, though the mechanisms are not fully understood
  • Resting basal metabolic rate (BMR) is individual — a person with a higher baseline BMR will burn more calories for the same added exercise than someone starting with a lower BMR
Practice Questions

12. Other Benefits of Exercise

Exercise provides benefits well beyond the number on the scale:

  • Decreased anxiety/depression — related to endorphin release; regular exercisers also often report reduced tiredness after a workout
  • Improved fitness and body image
  • Improved cardiovascular risk factors — decreased lipids, decreased hypertension, possible decreased risk of myocardial infarction
  • Improved metabolic state — a patient with type 2 diabetes who returns to a normal BMI may be able to come off medication entirely, with improved blood sugar control
  • Possible decreased mortality — more research is needed, but fit, active individuals appear to live longer and have a lower incidence of conditions like Alzheimer's disease, affecting both mental and physical state
Board tip: Health benefits from exercise occur even when weight change on the scale is modest or absent — don't treat exercise as useful only when it produces visible weight loss.
Practice Questions

13. Medications & Bariatric Surgery

Medications

  • Eligible population: BMI > 30, with or without comorbidities
  • Medication may produce weight loss, but maintaining a healthy weight long-term still requires psychological and physical behavior change — patients must also change dietary habits and exercise routines
  • A pill alone does not cure the underlying problem; patients are often drawn to medication (e.g., seeing ads for drugs like Ozempic) as an "easy" fix, but medication must be paired with lifestyle change

Bariatric Surgery

  • Eligible population: BMI > 40 (severe/morbid obesity), especially with comorbidities
  • Patients often lose significant weight in the first year, but commonly regain weight within 1–2 years if diet and exercise habits are not also changed — surgery alone does not change the underlying psychological outlook or lifestyle
  • Post-operative patients can and should exercise, but should typically start more gradually with lower-MET activities and build up over time
Practice Questions

14. The Physician's Role

  • Provide a good example — patients (like children) respond better to lifestyle advice when their physician models it. "Physician, heal thyself" is directly applicable to the overweight/obesity epidemic.
  • Communicate with compassion — this is a sensitive topic, and patients can become upset; it must be approached the same way any other serious medical epidemic (e.g., COVID, tuberculosis, diabetes) would be discussed, but with tact and empathy.
  • Use good clinical judgment in deciding who is appropriate for medication vs. surgery vs. lifestyle-only management.
  • Know available resources for patient counseling and referral:
    • American College of Sports Medicine
    • The Obesity Medicine Association
    • The Obesity Society
    • American Society for Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery (ASMBS)

Practice Questions

16. Common Mistakes & Board Tips

Mistake: Treating exercise as useful only when it produces visible weight loss.
Mistake: Assuming resistance exercise has little value because scale weight may not change.
Mistake: Ignoring the patient's baseline activity level when recommending intensity.
Mistake: Focusing only on weight while overlooking cardiovascular, metabolic, fitness, mood, and body-image benefits.
Mistake: Treating physical activity as separate from the broader weight-management plan (diet, medication, surgery, and behavioral/psychological factors all interact).
