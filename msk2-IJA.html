Juvenile Idiopathic Arthritis

MSK II — Clinical Lecture · Dr. Scott Stephens, DO

Learning Objectives

  • Learn the common forms of Juvenile Idiopathic Arthritis (JIA)
  • Understand the presenting features and diagnosis of JIA
  • Be familiar with the treatment of JIA
  • Recognize the long-term morbidity associated with JIA

JIA Basics

JIA was formerly called juvenile rheumatoid arthritis — a name that stuck for over 100 years before being changed because JIA is a distinct disease process from adult rheumatoid arthritis.

  • Autoimmune disease of unknown origin — some infections/stressors are thought to trigger flares, but no single cause has been identified
  • Most subtypes lack a strong genetic signal; a few have a minor HLA association
  • It is a clinical diagnosis — no single confirmatory test or imaging finding. Diagnosis requires working through other possibilities first (infection, malignancy, trauma) before landing on JIA
There is no single lab test or imaging study that rules JIA in. It is a diagnosis of exclusion built on pattern recognition — joint findings, timing, and supporting labs.

Classification Rules

  • 7 recognized types of JIA (some texts lump or split differently, but 7 is the standard teaching set)
  • Unequal distribution among types and between sexes — except systemic JIA, which is the one type with a roughly equal male:female ratio
  • Symptoms must be present for at least 6 weeks before a JIA diagnosis can be made
  • Final subtype should not be locked in until the 6-month point, since early symptoms can evolve into a more severe or milder pattern
  • Onset must occur before age 16 — symptoms starting after 16 are not classified as JIA

The 7 Types of JIA

1. Systemic JIA

  • <10% of cases — peak onset any time throughout childhood
  • Only subtype with a 1:1 male:female ratio
  • Whole-body disease: joint involvement plus splenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, high fevers
  • ~50% develop a salmon-pink rash spanning the whole body — classic testable buzzword
  • Low risk of uveitis
Systemic JIA behaves a lot like lupus clinically — fevers, rash, multi-organ involvement — and the two are often distinguished only by serologic testing.

2. Oligoarthritis

  • ~50% of all JIA cases — the most common subtype
  • Peak onset 2–4 years; female:male ratio 4:1
  • Defining feature: swelling in fewer than 6 joints, often without pain
  • Joints may be swollen and warm — this painless-swelling pattern helps differentiate it from septic arthritis, which is painful
  • Highest uveitis risk of any subtype — 30% overall, over 50% if ANA-positive and female
Uveitis in oligoarticular JIA is frequently painless. Kids can be silently losing vision, which is why they need repeat ophthalmology follow-up regardless of how the joints are doing — a single "all clear" exam is not enough.

3 & 4. Polyarticular JIA (RF-negative and RF-positive)

Both are defined by swelling in more than 6 joints, including large joints (hips, shoulders) and axial joints (spine).

RF-negative (~25% of cases): two peak ages — 2–4 years and 9–11 years; female:male ratio ~3:1.

RF-positive (~5% of cases): peak onset 12–16 years; much higher female:male ratio (~9:1); tends to be more aggressive with more joint destruction than RF-negative disease; low uveitis risk.

Rheumatoid-factor status is the key differentiator between the two polyarticular types — and RF-positive disease is the one that most closely resembles adult RA in its aggressive joint destruction.

5. Psoriatic JIA

  • <10% of cases — two peak windows, 2–4 years and 9–11 years; female:male ratio ~2:1
  • ~50% develop a scaly rash (vs. the salmon-pink rash of systemic JIA)
  • Uveitis risk ~10–20%

6. Enthesitis-related JIA

  • ~10% of cases — peak onset after age 11
  • Only subtype with a male predominance (male:female higher than other types)
  • Defining feature: inflammation at tendon insertion sites (entheses), in addition to joint swelling — this is what separates it from the other subtypes
  • Uveitis risk ~10–20%

7. Undifferentiated JIA

A catch-all category — disease that doesn't fit the criteria of any other type, or that is too early in its course to classify.

Case Walkthrough

The Case

A 4-year-old female presents with a 6-week history of a swollen right knee. Seen previously by a partner and an urgent care; X-ray was negative. Started on cephalosporin (cephalexin) for presumed soft-tissue infection — no improvement.

On further history: mother has also noticed bilateral ankle swelling that comes and goes, warm to the touch. The child has little pain and no fevers.

Labs: ANA positive, rheumatoid factor negative, CRP elevated, WBC normal.

Number of joints: mother describes up to 3 joints → fewer than 6, consistent with oligoarthritis.

Duration: right at 6 weeks — the minimum needed to make an initial JIA diagnosis.

Pain vs. swelling: minimal pain despite visible swelling — classic for JIA (kids more often report morning stiffness that improves with activity, rather than pain).

Labs: ANA-positive is common in oligoarticular JIA (and flags higher uveitis risk); a normal WBC with elevated CRP helps distinguish this from a septic joint or acute osteomyelitis.

Diagnosis: Oligoarticular JIA — and given ANA-positive + female, this child needs close, recurring ophthalmology follow-up for silent uveitis.

Treatment

  • NSAIDs — first line; often sufficient alone for milder disease
  • Steroids — used short-term, including intra-articular injections; first-line in systemic JIA to control the acute presentation
  • Methotrexate — for more chronic or aggressive disease
  • Biologics — IL-1 inhibitors (e.g., anakinra) and IL-6 inhibitors (e.g., tocilizumab); IL-1 inhibition is the most commonly used biologic class and is started earlier than steroids/methotrexate in more aggressive forms
With early recognition and modern biologic therapy, many children achieve complete remission with no further disease activity.

Complications of Untreated / Undertreated JIA

  • Uveitis — the most common complication overall, often painless
  • Leg-length discrepancy
  • Chronic short stature
  • Joint destruction
  • In severe/aggressive cases: osteopenia and osteoporosis, TMJ involvement (can cause a "lock jaw" presentation), and a lupus-like systemic illness with multi-organ involvement

Key Points

  • JIA is an autoimmune process diagnosed clinically, with supporting labs — not a single confirmatory test
  • Of the 7 types, oligoarthritis, polyarticular, and systemic make up the vast majority of cases
  • Uveitis is the most common complication seen across JIA subtypes
  • Treatment to prevent long-term joint damage: NSAIDs → steroids/IL-inhibitor biologics → methotrexate, tailored by subtype and severity

Clinical Pearls (Attending's Perspective)

Dr. Stephens notes JIA is uncommon in practice (only a handful of cases over 15 years), so kids often cycle through multiple diagnoses, antibiotics, joint taps, and MRIs before JIA is finally considered — partly because of the mandatory 6-week symptom window.

Keep JIA in the back of your mind whenever a child presents with joint swelling, no history of trauma, and little-to-no pain. That triad should trigger the question: "could this be JIA?"

Pediatric rheumatology is a rare subspecialty concentrated at large academic centers — patients are typically co-managed between the specialist and primary care rather than treated exclusively by the rheumatologist.

Practice Questions

1. A 3-year-old girl has painless swelling of her left knee for 7 weeks. Labs show a positive ANA and a negative RF. Which subtype is most likely, and what is the key associated risk to counsel the family on?

  • Oligoarthritis — high risk of (often painless) uveitis requiring recurring eye exams
  • Systemic JIA — risk of macrophage activation syndrome
  • Polyarticular RF-positive — risk of aggressive joint destruction
  • Enthesitis-related JIA — risk of TMJ lock-jaw

2. Which JIA subtype is the only one with an equal male-to-female ratio, and what rash is classically associated with it?

  • Psoriatic JIA — scaly rash
  • Oligoarthritis — no characteristic rash
  • Systemic JIA — salmon-pink rash
  • Enthesitis-related JIA — no characteristic rash

3. A 14-year-old girl presents with swelling in more than 6 joints, including the hips and spine, with rapid progressive joint damage. Rheumatoid factor is positive. What is true about this presentation?

  • This subtype carries a high risk of uveitis
  • This is RF-positive polyarticular JIA — the most aggressive subtype, but with low uveitis risk
  • This is oligoarthritis with secondary joint involvement
  • First-line treatment should be NSAID monotherapy

4. What is the minimum symptom duration required before a JIA diagnosis can be made, and when should the final subtype be assigned?

  • 2 weeks to diagnose; type at 3 months
  • 6 weeks to diagnose; final typing not until 6 months
  • 3 months to diagnose; type at 1 year
  • No minimum duration; diagnosis can be made immediately with a positive ANA

5. Which subtype is distinguished by inflammation at tendon insertion sites in addition to joint swelling?

  • Psoriatic JIA
  • Systemic JIA
  • Polyarticular RF-positive JIA
  • Enthesitis-related JIA
