02 · Lentigo

Lentigo

A demarcated hyperpigmented patch — "sunspot" whose cause is actually unknown
  • Description: demarcated, hyperpigmented patch, roughly 5–10 mm
  • Etiology: occurs on sun-exposed skin, but the actual cause is unknown per Robbins (despite the common nickname "sunspot")
  • Mechanism: increase in melanocytes and pigmentation along the basement membrane — i.e., a lentiginous pattern (single layer of melanocytes at the dermal-epidermal junction)
Important distinguishing pointUnlike a freckle, a lentigo shows no change in coloration with sun exposure. This is the classic freckle-vs-lentigo distractor on exams: freckles darken/fade with the seasons; lentigos do not change.

03 · Melanocytic Nevi

Nevus — General Concept

"Nevus" is a broad term — Stedman's lists more than 60 forms

Per Stedman's Medical Dictionary, nevus can mean:

  • A birthmark: a circumscribed malformation of the skin, especially if colored by hyperpigmentation or increased vascularity — it may be epidermal, adnexal, melanocytic, vascular, or mesodermal (not all birthmarks are pigmented — e.g., a capillary nevus is vascular, not a pigment cell lesion)
  • A benign, localized overgrowth of melanin-forming cells of the skin, present at birth or appearing later in life

When people say "nevus" clinically, they usually mean a melanocytic nevus, and this lecture focuses on that group and its variants (junctional, compound, intradermal, blue, Spitz).

Melanocytic Nevi — Forms & Mutation

Junctional → compound → intradermal is the typical maturation sequence
  • Basic description: skin lesion with proliferation of benign melanocytes; the cells are called nevus cells or "nevoid"
  • Junctional nevus: nests of nevus cells at the dermal-epidermal junction only
  • Compound nevus: junctional nests plus nests/cords of cells in the dermis
  • Intradermal nevus: nests/cords of cells only in the dermis (no junctional component)
Maturation sequenceNevi are thought to begin junctional, progress to compound, and finally become intradermal as they age — the cells "mature" and migrate deeper into the dermis over time.
Molecular driverGain-of-function mutation of BRAF (or RAS) drives melanocyte proliferation, which is then followed by senescence (growth arrest) — this is the same initiating mutation implicated in melanoma, but in a nevus it is followed by senescence rather than continued malignant progression.

Pathology of Melanocytic Nevi

Gross appearance is almost the opposite of what you'd intuitively guess
Gross & microscopic findings by type

Counterintuitive point (per lecture)The junctional nevus — the one closest to the skin surface — is actually flat, while compound/intradermal nevi (which grow down into the dermis) are the ones that are raised. Dr. Kemp specifically flags this as unexpected.

04 · Nevus vs. Melanoma

Distinguishing a Benign Nevus from Melanoma

The key concept: nevus cells "mature" and lose their potency as they go deeper — melanoma cells don't

Dr. Kemp emphasized this distinction with a personal anecdote about a family member misdiagnosed with melanoma in situ — reinforcing that pigmented lesions can be genuinely difficult to distinguish, even for experts, and that a second opinion is often reasonable.

  • Superficial cells in a nevus are larger, produce melanin, and grow in nests
  • Deeper cells get progressively smaller, generally produce little/no pigment, and grow in cords or as single cells
  • Deepest cells take on a spindled/fusiform, nerve-like appearance — described as becoming "neuralized"
  • This maturation pattern (large/pigmented → small/non-pigmented → spindled/"neuralized") reflects cellular senescence and is NOT seen in melanoma
Melanoma red flagIn melanoma, malignant cells characteristically migrate upward through the epidermis toward the stratum corneum (upward, or "pagetoid," spread) — this is not seen in nevi, where cells are confined and show maturation with depth.
Quick contrastNevus: cells shrink, lose pigment, and become spindled ("neuralized") with increasing depth → senescence.
Melanoma: cells stay large, keep producing pigment, do not become spindled, and can migrate upward into the epidermis.

05 · Blue Nevus & Spitz Nevus

Blue Nevus

Dark blue/gray-black — can be confused with melanoma on gross exam
  • Description: dark blue to gray-black in color
  • Microscopic: abundant melanin pigment; spindle-shaped dermal melanocytes, often with an ill-defined, infiltrative-appearing pattern in the dermis
  • Often associated with fibrosis/scarring around the lesion
Lecture noteDr. Kemp: on microscopy, the melanin pigment itself is easy to see, but the cells containing it can be poorly defined — the pigment can be more obvious than the cell borders.

Spitz Nevus

A disease process of the young — important because it can mimic melanoma
  • Demographics: usually occurs in children
  • Other names: spindle and epithelioid cell nevus; Stedman's calls it a "benign juvenile melanoma" (an unusual pairing of "benign" and "melanoma" in the same name)
  • Location: frequently face and extremities
  • Clinical course: may appear and grow rapidly, then stabilize — the rapid growth phase alone can be alarming and mimic a true neoplasm if the lesion is caught before it stabilizes
  • Gross: light brown to red (the red color can cause confusion with a hemangioma)
  • Microscopic: large cells, can be fusiform (elongated); cell size stays relatively uniform throughout the lesion (a feature that can also be seen in melanoma)
Important pointSpitz nevus is a classic melanoma mimicker. In practice, these are often sent for a second expert opinion because missing an actual melanoma would be a serious error — Dr. Kemp notes this from personal pathology experience.

06 · Dysplastic Nevi

Dysplastic Nevi — Concept & Risk

A risk marker, not truly a premalignant lesion in its own right
  • Most melanomas arise de novo (on their own), not from a preexisting dysplastic nevus
  • Dysplastic nevi are instead a marker of increased risk for melanoma developing elsewhere
  • Familial dysplastic nevus syndrome (a.k.a. melanoma-nevus syndrome, B-K mole syndrome): individuals can have numerous dysplastic nevi and carry a ~100% lifetime risk of developing melanoma
  • Sporadic dysplastic nevi: increased melanoma risk once a person has 10 or more dysplastic nevi
Molecular associationsCDKN2A (familial dysplastic nevus syndrome) — encodes p16 (negative regulator of CDK4, i.e., helps maintain Rb) and p14 (helps stabilize p53 by inhibiting MDM2, which normally degrades p53). Possible additional role for TERT (catalytic subunit of telomerase) — relevant because telomerase activity is part of what makes cancer cells effectively immortal.

Pathology of Dysplastic Nevi

Gross and microscopic features overlap with — and can be confused with — melanoma
  • Gross: macules to patches to slightly raised plaques, typically >5 mm; variable pigmentation and irregular border (both concerning features shared with melanoma)
  • Microscopic: often compound nevi; characteristic bridging (fusion/coalescence) between nests of cells at the dermal-epidermal junction
  • Lentiginous hyperplasia: a "shoulder" of junctional melanocytes extending beyond the main nested component at the periphery of the lesion
  • Melanin incontinence: free melanin pigment released into the dermis (not contained within cells) with sparse accompanying lymphocytes
Key histologic clueBridging/fusion/coalescence between nests is the classic distinguishing microscopic feature of a dysplastic nevus — ordinary junctional nevi show discrete, separate nests that do not connect to one another.
Real-world diagnostic caveatDr. Kemp notes that gross/microscopic categories are not always absolute — he has personally mistaken an intradermal nevus for a seborrheic keratosis (and vice versa) at times, underscoring that these diagnoses can have genuine interpretive overlap in practice.

Melanoma is aggressive, but usually visible — early recognition matters enormously

Lecture emphasis"E" (evolution) was added after Dr. Kemp's own medical training (which originally taught only A–D) — itching or bleeding in a mole is a genuine red flag; delayed biopsy of a bleeding mole was cited in lecture as leading to a case of invasive/metastatic melanoma. Excisional biopsy is the preferred diagnostic approach when melanoma is suspected.

Melanoma — General Features & Locations

  • Etiology: UV radiation exposure — but not the only factor
  • Primary location: sun-exposed skin (~93% of melanomas arise in skin)
  • Other locations (non-sun-exposed): oral and anogenital mucosa (oropharynx, GI tract, genitourinary tract), esophagus, meninges/spinal cord, and the uvea (eye) — reflecting that melanocytes are present at all of these sites

08 · Melanoma — Molecular Pathogenesis

Categories of Melanoma-Associated Mutations

Robbins groups melanoma mutations into three functional categories
Mutation categories
CategoryGene(s)Effect
Disrupt cell cycle controlCDKN2A (encodes p16 & p14)p16 normally inhibits CDK4 (maintains Rb); p14 normally promotes p53 (by inhibiting MDM2, which degrades p53). Loss removes these brakes on the cell cycle.
Activate pro-growth signalingBRAF (activates RAS pathway; rare in non-sun-exposed sites), KIT (more common in non-sun-exposed sites), PTEN, NF1Drives proliferation
Activate telomeraseTERTConfers replicative immortality — a hallmark that made melanoma's stepwise mutation sequence unusually well characterized among neoplasms, per lecture
Additional risk-associated molecular findingsMutations in MITF, CDKN2A, CDK4; the red-hair phenotype (melanocortin-1 receptor variants); RB1; BRCA-associated protein 1; and various microRNAs have also been implicated in melanoma risk.

Stepwise Pathogenesis (Sun-Exposed Melanoma)

Robbins describes a specific mutation sequence tied to specific steps of neoplastic progression — unusual detail for a single neoplasm
  1. UV-induced DNA damage is the initial insult
  2. Genetic background: CDKN2A (9p21) — p16 (maintains Rb) and p14 (prevents p53 degradation)
  3. Initiating event: activating mutation of BRAF (or RAS)
  4. Loss of telomerase regulation → cellular atypia
  5. Loss of p16 → transition to the vertical growth phase (the dangerous, invasive growth pattern)
  6. Loss of p53 and/or PTEN → metastasis
Memory anchorBRAF initiates → p16 loss drives vertical growth → p53/PTEN loss drives metastasis. Each mutation maps to a specific, testable step in progression.

09 · Melanoma — Pathology & Prognosis

Gross & Microscopic Pathology of Melanoma

  • Gross: variation in color throughout the lesion; irregular border; two growth phases — radial (grows along the skin surface) and vertical (grows down into the dermis — the dangerous phase)
  • Microscopic: cells larger than melanocytic nevus cells; large, sometimes pleomorphic nuclei, often with prominent nucleoli; critically, there is no maturation of cells with depth (unlike a nevus) — cells stay large and pigment-producing even deep in the dermis, and do not become spindled
  • Immunohistochemistry: HMB-45 and S-100 are characteristic positive stains (HMB-45 highlighted in lecture as the single most useful IHC stain to know for melanoma)
Prognosis — Breslow thicknessBreslow thickness (measured from the top of the granular cell layer to the deepest point of invasion, in millimeters) is the single most important prognostic factor for melanoma — it is objective and reproducible, unlike the older, more subjective Clark's level system.

Favorable vs. Unfavorable Microscopic Factors

Prognostic factors
Positive (favorable)Negative (unfavorable)
Less tumor depthDeeper invasion
No or few mitoses>1 mitotic figure per mm²
Lymphocytic infiltrateNo/minimal lymphocytic infiltrate
Absence of regressionEvidence of tumor regression
Lack of ulcerationUlceration
Counterintuitive point (flagged in lecture)Tumor regression is a BAD (unfavorable) prognostic sign — despite sounding reassuring. A regressed primary can leave the metastatic disease as the only remaining evidence of tumor, since the immune system may eliminate the primary while metastases persist elsewhere. Lymphocytic infiltrate, in contrast, is favorable — it suggests active immune engagement against the tumor (and explains why immunosuppressed patients are at higher melanoma risk).

Melanoma Compared With Other Neoplasms

Melanoma breaks several "usual" tumor-grading rules
  • Horizontal (surface) size has no prognostic significance — unlike most solid tumors, a 1 cm vs. 10 cm melanoma (as measured across the skin surface) does not by itself change prognosis; only depth of invasion matters
  • Morphologic variants (e.g., spindle cell) carry no prognostic significance — contrast with tumors like meningioma, where certain morphologic variants (e.g., clear cell, chordoid, atypical) do carry prognostic weight
  • No histologic grade is assigned to melanoma — there is no "well/moderately/poorly differentiated melanoma" the way there is for squamous cell carcinoma or many gliomas; it is simply called malignant melanoma
Diagnostic humility — lecture anecdoteDr. Kemp recounts a case where a diagnosis of melanoma was overturned to benign nevus after expert review, and a separate case where a lesion originally called benign nevus was later found to represent metastatic melanoma. Pigmented lesion diagnosis can be genuinely difficult even for trained pathologists, and a second opinion is a reasonable step when the diagnosis is uncertain or consequential.

13 · Melanoma In Situ & the Paget's Mimic

Features of the In-Situ Forms

Three of the four invasive types have a matching in-situ precursor — nodular does not
  • Superficial spreading, acral lentiginous, and lentigo maligna all have an in-situ counterpart (superficial spreading in situ, acral lentiginous in situ, lentigo maligna in situ). Nodular melanoma does not have an in-situ form — by definition it is vertical/invasive from the start.
  • In-situ lesions are limited to the epidermis (i.e., confined to the dermal-epidermal junction) — they look like melanoma cytologically but have not invaded.
  • Cytologic atypia is present: nuclear enlargement, hyperchromasia, prominent nucleoli.
  • +/- Transepidermal migration — neoplastic cells migrating up through the epidermis. This is still consistent with an in-situ lesion (not invasion into the dermis) but is a feature strongly associated with melanoma.
"This is breast — why is this here?"Dr. Kemp shows a slide of Paget's disease of the breast (large neoplastic cells migrating through the epidermis) specifically because melanoma's transepidermal migration produces a similar picture. Whenever there is a differential for Paget's disease of the skin, melanoma must be considered — immunohistochemistry is used to distinguish the two.

Melanoma — Poor Differentiation & the Unknown-Primary Problem

  • Melanoma can become poorly differentiated to the point that pigment production is lost entirely and the cell of origin can't be identified on routine stains.
  • This makes melanoma a classic culprit when a pathologist is handed a metastatic nodule (e.g., in lung or liver) with an unknown primary.
  • HMB-45 immunohistochemical stain is commonly used to confirm melanocytic origin in this setting.
  • The original cutaneous melanoma can sometimes regress or otherwise disappear, so a patient can present with metastatic disease and no identifiable skin primary at all.

14 · Metastasis, Clark's & Breslow Staging

Metastatic Dissemination

  • Lymph nodes: regional lymph nodes are the classic first stop; knowing regional lymphatic drainage patterns matters (analogous to knowing that upper-outer-quadrant breast cancer drains to axillary nodes, while medial breast cancer does not — sampling the wrong node basin will miss disease).
  • Sentinel node biopsy: a radioactive tracer is injected to identify the first node(s) draining the tumor; that node is sampled and examined with multiple sections and immunohistochemical stains (e.g., HMB-45) looking for occult neoplastic cells.
  • Distant sites: liver, lungs, brain, spinal cord, GI tract, skin, and — notably — the heart (melanoma is well known for cardiac metastases, an unusual site for most other cancers).
High-yield framingMelanoma should always be on the differential when working up a poorly differentiated metastatic malignancy of unknown primary.

15 · Congenital Melanocytic Nevi

Congenital Melanocytic Nevi

A birthmark whose melanoma risk tracks almost entirely with size
  • Description: a macule or patch, present at birth; classically described in a "bathing suit" distribution (trunk/buttocks are commonly shown).
  • Potential etiology: disrupted migration of melanocytes from the neural crest — since neural crest cells give rise to melanocytes, a large aberrant patch of them in one location suggests abnormal migration during development.
  • Important point — melanoma risk: overall about 0.5–0.7% of congenital melanocytic nevi develop melanoma. Risk scales with size:
    • >7 cm in infancy → 4–6% risk
    • Giant congenital melanocytic nevi (>14 cm in infancy) carry the highest risk
  • Differential/management: the main concern is development of melanoma within the lesion — managed by watching for change (a stable lesion is reassuring; sudden change warrants removal if it hasn't already been excised).
Intuition checkBigger lesion → more melanocytes → more "chances" for one of them to acquire a transforming mutation. This is why size, not location or any other feature, is the dominant predictor of future melanoma risk in a congenital nevus.

16 · Dermal Melanosis & Hemangioma

Dermal Melanosis ("Mongolian Spot")

A benign birthmark whose main danger is being mistaken for abuse
  • Description: flat, brown/blue-gray lesion; melanocytes are located deep in the dermis (rather than at the dermal-epidermal junction, as in most pigmented lesions).
  • Location: classically centered over the lower back / sacrum / buttocks, but can be more extensive — case example given of patches extending up the back to the shoulder, and can occur off the midline as well.
  • Epidemiology: common in Black, Native American, Asian, and Hispanic populations. Most fade with age.
  • Important point: this lesion can be misdiagnosed as bruising from child abuse — Dr. Kemp specifically notes this has happened repeatedly with pediatricians, family physicians, and ER physicians. Recognizing dermal melanosis is important precisely to avoid this misinterpretation.

Hemangioma (Birthmark)

  • Description: benign proliferation of blood vessels; most common type in children is the capillary hemangioma (small-vessel proliferation).
  • Contrast with a cavernous hemangioma — usually found in adults, often in the liver, with large vascular spaces; cavernous hemangiomas generally do not regress.
  • Important point: capillary hemangiomas typically regress over time (or mostly regress) — important to counsel families before pursuing removal of a large lesion, since excision of a large birthmark can leave a scar.
  • Differential diagnosis: like dermal melanosis, a prominent hemangioma can be mistaken for abuse.
Common thread across this sectionCongenital melanocytic nevus, dermal melanosis, and hemangioma are grouped together in lecture as "other birthmarks" — normal, usually benign findings whose main clinical pitfalls are (1) melanoma risk tracking with nevus size, and (2) both dermal melanosis and hemangiomas being mistaken for non-accidental trauma.

18 · Seborrheic Keratosis & Leser-Trélat

Seborrheic Keratosis

"Stuck-on," warty, and extremely common — the classic mimicker of pigmented neoplasms
  • Description: raised, brown, warty lesion with a characteristic "stuck-on" appearance — as if a wrinkled, raised structure were glued onto the skin.
  • Microscopic: sheets/proliferation of basaloid cells (cells resembling the basal layer — bland, uniform in size and shape, not producing keratin the way flattened squamous cells do), hyperkeratosis, and keratin-filled horn cysts (fully enclosed by basaloid cells) as well as pseudo-horn cysts (keratin that has ruptured/erupted to the surface, contributing to the wrinkled look). Basaloid cells may also be pigmented.
  • Location: most commonly the trunk, in middle-aged and older individuals.
  • Potential etiology: genetic — associated with mutations in fibroblast growth factor receptor 3 (FGFR3).
  • Clinical pearl: if you see hair growing out of a lesion, that favors a benign process — neoplasms tend to obliterate adnexal structures, while benign lesions tend to grow around them.
  • Differential diagnosis: pigmented neoplasm — seborrheic keratoses and intradermal nevi can genuinely be mistaken for one another, in both directions, even by experienced pathologists.
Leser-Trélat signThe abrupt (rapid) development of multiple seborrheic keratoses at once. Classically associated with an underlying malignancy — usually gastric adenocarcinoma. (Lecture note: this association is well described in the literature, though rarely observed directly in practice — the confounder is that clinicians usually don't know the actual timeframe over which a patient's seborrheic keratoses arose.)

19 · Acanthosis Nigricans

Acanthosis Nigricans

Velvety, hyperpigmented flexural skin — usually metabolic, occasionally paraneoplastic
  • Description: velvet-like thickened and hyperpigmented skin of flexural areas — axillae most commonly, but also neck, groin, and anogenital regions.
  • Association with benign conditions: can be an autosomal dominant condition, or — far more commonly encountered clinically — associated with obesity and/or diabetes mellitus.
  • Association with cancer: associated with gastrointestinal adenocarcinomas (paralleling the gastric adenocarcinoma association of the Leser-Trélat sign).
  • Molecular: FGFR3 activating mutations — the same gene implicated in seborrheic keratosis.
Practical framing from lectureJust as with Leser-Trélat and seborrheic keratosis, Dr. Kemp notes that in his own experience he has seen acanthosis nigricans repeatedly in patients with obesity/diabetes, but essentially never traced one directly to a GI malignancy — the malignant association is real and worth knowing for boards, but the everyday clinical association is metabolic.

20 · Acrochordon, Epidermal Inclusion Cyst & Cherry Angioma

Acrochordon (Fibroepithelial Polyp)

The common "skin tag" — epithelium wrapped around a fibrovascular core
  • Description: skin-colored, pedunculated lesion — epithelium overlying a core of fibrous tissue, with blood vessels within the core; hyperplasia of both dermis and epidermis.
  • Location: neck, groin, axillae.
  • Potential etiology: a response to irritation of the skin — chronic rubbing/friction leads to reactive hyperplastic thickening.
  • Important point: associated with metabolic syndrome (obesity, dyslipidemia, hypertension, insulin resistance) — acrochordons are commonly seen clustered together with acanthosis nigricans in the axilla, reflecting their shared metabolic association.
  • Differential diagnosis: neurofibroma, seborrheic keratosis, pedunculated nevus.
  • No meaningful malignant potential — the main issues are irritation and cosmetic concern; can be removed by excision or freezing.

Epidermal Inclusion Cyst

  • Description: a nodule that forms when epidermal cells are displaced into the dermis, forming a space lined by epidermis that continues to produce and accumulate keratin inside itself; may have a visible central punctum connecting to the surface.
  • Location: head, neck, trunk.
  • Important point: can become inflamed and/or rupture. Multiple epidermal inclusion cysts are associated with Gardner syndrome — an autosomal dominant colon cancer syndrome.
  • Practical clue: a movable subcutaneous nodule is most often either an epidermal inclusion cyst or a lipoma — sectioning distinguishes them at gross exam: a lipoma sections as simple fat, while an epidermal inclusion cyst releases cheesy yellow keratin debris.

Cherry Angioma

  • Description: small (0.1–0.5 cm), dome-shaped, red papules composed of blood vessels (dilated, slit-like vascular spaces filled with red blood cells, without true vessel proliferation); will bleed if injured.
  • Location: trunk and upper extremities.
  • Important point: increase in number with age — despite the popular association with alcohol use ("spider angiomas" are the ones tied to alcohol/liver disease), cherry angiomas themselves are not known to be specifically alcohol-related.
  • Differential diagnosis: hemangioma, other vascular tumors.

21 · Adnexal Tumors & Cylindroma

Sweat Gland (Adnexal) Tumor Differentiation — Don't Memorize the Whole Tree

24 · Actinic Keratosis

Actinic Keratosis

The precursor lesion — atypia confined to the lower epidermis
  • Description: tan-brown, rough/"sandpaper-like" to the touch; microscopically, cytologic atypia (hyperchromasia, increased mitotic figures, pleomorphism) confined to the lower third of the epidermis, with overlying parakeratosis (nucleated stratum corneum — contributes to the rough texture) and maturation of the more superficial layers (cells flatten and become more eosinophilic as they mature toward the surface)
  • Location: sun-exposed skin — face, arms, dorsum of the hands
  • Etiology: UV damage to DNA, specifically damage to TP53 (p53)
  • Important point: precursor lesion for squamous cell carcinoma
  • Differential diagnosis: squamous cell carcinoma — grossly, an early SCC and an actinic keratosis can look very similar
The single highest-yield distinction in this sectionIf atypia is confined to the lower third of the epidermis (with maturation above it), it's actinic keratosis. If atypia is full thickness — no maturation at all, all the way to the surface — it becomes squamous cell carcinoma in situ (which in skin is also called Bowen's disease). Dr. Kemp draws the direct analogy to CIN (cervix) or VIN (vulva) grading — the same "how far up does the atypia go" logic applies here.
Clinical pearl — "leukoplakia" logic applies to skin tooBecause AK and early SCC can look identical grossly, Dr. Kemp compares this to the concept of leukoplakia in the mouth: a white/rough patch is a descriptive term covering a wide range of possible diagnoses, and you always have to biopsy to know which one it is.

25 · Keratoacanthoma

Keratoacanthoma

A rapidly growing, cratered nodule — benign, or a well-differentiated SCC? The debate matters clinically
  • Description: rapidly growing papule/nodule with a central keratin-filled crater ("keratin plug")
  • Location: sun-exposed areas — face, upper and lower extremities (same distribution as actinic keratosis)
  • Etiology: sun exposure, cigarette smoking, HPV, genetic factors, trauma, chemical carcinogens — essentially the same risk-factor list as SCC
  • Important point: genuinely debated whether these are a completely benign lesion or a well-differentiated squamous cell carcinoma (or something in between) — because of this uncertainty, a punch biopsy (which samples the whole lesion) is preferred over a shave biopsy
  • Differential diagnosis: squamous cell carcinoma
Dr. Kemp's framingHe's explicit that this classification is unsettled and varies by source — "are they completely benign, which is what we were always taught... or are they a well-differentiated squamous cell carcinoma, or somewhere in between." The practical takeaway he wants you to retain is the biopsy-technique point (punch > shave) more than a firm answer to the benign-vs-malignant debate.

26 · Squamous Cell Carcinoma

Squamous Cell Carcinoma

Red, scaling plaques → nodular/ulcerated invasive disease; keratin pearls and intercellular bridges on histology
  • Description (gross): non-invasive — red, scaling plaques; invasive — nodular, may have hyperkeratotic scale, may ulcerate
  • Description (microscopic): variable differentiation — well-differentiated tumors show intercellular bridges and keratin pearls; poorly differentiated tumors may require immunohistochemistry to confirm the cell of origin. Non-invasive disease = full-thickness atypia (carcinoma in situ)
  • Location: sun-exposed regions
  • Etiology: UV light damage to DNA (TP53, activating RAS mutations, loss-of-function Notch mutations); also arises in chronic non-healing ulcers (a "Marjolin ulcer"); chemical exposure; xeroderma pigmentosum (defect in nucleotide excision repair); immunosuppression
  • Important point: less than 1% metastasize, but locally aggressive — early identification and removal generally cures the patient; increased incidence in immunosuppressed patients and associated with HPV infection
  • Differential diagnosis: actinic keratosis
Marjolin ulcer — the mechanism to understand, not just memorizeA chronic non-healing ulcer (from any cause — decubitus/pressure ulcer, burn scar, chronic venous stasis ulcer) creates ongoing cellular turnover and division at the wound edge; every round of division is another opportunity for mutation, which over time can give rise to SCC arising within the ulcer. Dr. Kemp illustrates this with a case from residency: a patient with a chronic sacral decubitus ulcer (from long-term immobility after a gunshot wound) developed an overlying SCC — cause of death was eventually attributed back through that whole chain.
Point to ponder (from lecture)Why does SCC of the skin have a much better prognosis than SCC of the lung? Dr. Kemp's answer: skin lesions are visible and grow relatively slowly, so patients and clinicians get the chance to identify and remove them while still small (even a lesion up to ~3 cm is still fairly easy to notice on skin). A lung SCC of the same size can develop silently, since there's no way to see or feel it internally, so it's often found later and at a more advanced stage — hence the worse prognosis for the same histologic tumor type.

27 · Basal Cell Carcinoma

Basal Cell Carcinoma

The most common — and least aggressive — skin cancer; arises from the basal layer, drives Hedgehog signaling
  • Description (gross): pearly papule with telangiectasias (small surface vessels); can enlarge and ulcerate, producing a "rodent ulcer" appearance
  • Description (microscopic): nests of basaloid cells (high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio, arising from the basal/germinative layer — no keratinization, unlike SCC) with characteristic peripheral palisading — nuclei line up around the periphery of each nest like a picket fence
  • Location: sun-exposed skin
  • Etiology: UV-induced DNA damage causing loss-of-function mutation of PTCH1, which normally negatively regulates the Hedgehog signaling pathway — loss of PTCH1 function activates Hedgehog signaling; immunosuppression also plays a role (same theme as SCC — the immune system helps eliminate neoplastic cells)
  • Important points: Gorlin syndrome (autosomal dominant, inherited PTCH1 defect) is associated with multiple BCCs — also called nevoid basal cell carcinoma syndrome or basal cell nevus syndrome (same entity, three names); BCC very rarely metastasizes — case-report-level rarity
De-emphasized content (per lecture)Dr. Kemp shows several BCC histologic variants (adenoid — glandular-appearing nests; morpheaform — worse prognosis) but explicitly notes "Robbins doesn't talk about" these and "would not be tested by that" — he includes them only to introduce the idea that BCC has multiple subtypes for your future clinical awareness, not as board-testable material.
Kemp's own analogy — skin vs. thyroidHe draws a parallel between skin and thyroid pathology: each organ contains both one of the most indolent tumor types in the body (BCC in skin; papillary thyroid carcinoma in the thyroid) and one of the most aggressive (melanoma in skin; anaplastic thyroid carcinoma, by implication, in the thyroid) — both extremes tied to UV/sun exposure in the skin's case.

28 · Merkel Cell Carcinoma

Merkel Cell Carcinoma

Not in Robbins, but Dr. Kemp flags it as worth knowing — an aggressive small round blue cell tumor of the skin
  • Description: presents as a papule or nodule, typically in the head and neck (about 50% of cases); lymph node involvement in over 50% of cases; histology shows cords and bands of uniform small cells with scant cytoplasm and numerous mitotic figures — a "small round blue cell" tumor pattern, resembling other high-grade neuroendocrine carcinomas (e.g., small cell carcinoma of the lung)
  • Demographics: older individuals
  • Important point: a really aggressive neoplasm; requires immunohistochemical/neuroendocrine staining to confirm diagnosis
Emphasis flag — pay attention hereDr. Kemp explicitly says: "This one's not in Robbins, but I think it's a good one to know." Content he calls out this way (present in lecture/slides but absent from the primary textbook) is a signal he considers it fair game for his own exam even though it won't be reinforced by your reading — treat it as high-yield rather than skippable.
Practice Question
An elderly patient has a rapidly growing nodule on the neck. Biopsy reveals sheets and cords of uniform small cells with scant cytoplasm, numerous mitotic figures, and a "small round blue cell" appearance resembling small cell carcinoma of the lung. What is the most likely diagnosis?

29 · Dermatofibroma & Dermatofibrosarcoma Protuberans (DFSP)

Dermatofibroma (Benign Fibrous Histiocytoma)

A benign fibroblast proliferation with a distinctive physical exam finding — the dimple sign
  • Description: firm, raised papule or nodule composed of a benign proliferation of fibroblasts; histology shows a circumscribed proliferation of spindle cells, with fibroblasts surrounding collagen bundles
  • Demographics/location: adults; lower extremities, especially young/middle-aged females
  • Important point: dimple sign — lateral compression (squeezing the lesion from the sides) causes it to dimple inward at the center, due to scarring/fibrosis in the dermis associated with the lesion
  • Differential diagnosis: neurofibroma
What NOT to expect on the exam (per lecture)Dr. Kemp is explicit that he will not show an image and ask you to histologically distinguish dermatofibroma from neurofibroma ("I'm not going to show you an image and say is this a dermatofibroma or a neurofibroma"). What he DOES want you to know cold is the clinical associations of neurofibromatosis (café au lait spots, Lisch nodules, neurofibromas — see Pt. 1) — that's the testable half of this differential, not the histologic pattern-matching.

Dermatofibrosarcoma Protuberans (DFSP)

A slow-growing but locally recurrent dermal sarcoma driven by a COL1A1–PDGFB translocation
  • Description: slow-growing tumor, presenting as a nodule or plaque
  • Location: trunk of young adults
  • Molecular: translocation between COL1A1 (collagen 1A1) and PDGFB (platelet-derived growth factor-β)
  • Microscopic: storiform ("pinwheel") arrangement of fibroblasts; infiltrates subcutaneous fat, producing a honeycomb pattern
  • Important point/complication: local recurrence after excision is common — requires a wide local excision
Cross-reference Dr. Kemp draws himselfHe explicitly calls back to breast pathology when discussing the need for wide local excision, comparing DFSP to phyllodes tumor of the breast: both are low-grade-but-locally-aggressive lesions that infiltrate surrounding tissue and require wide excision (not simple excision) to prevent recurrence, even though most cases behave in a low-grade fashion overall.
Practice Question
A young woman has a firm nodule on her lower leg that dimples inward when compressed from the sides. What is the most likely diagnosis, and what is the significance of the dimple sign?
Practice Question
A young adult presents with a slowly enlarging plaque on the trunk. Biopsy reveals a storiform (pinwheel) proliferation of spindle cells infiltrating the subcutaneous fat in a honeycomb pattern. Molecular studies would most likely reveal a translocation involving which two genes?

30 · Mycosis Fungoides & Sézary Syndrome

Mycosis Fungoides (Cutaneous T-cell Lymphoma)

A clonal CD4+ T-cell lymphoma of the skin — usually indolent, unless it goes leukemic (Sézary syndrome)
  • Description: cutaneous T-cell lymphoma — clonal populations of CD4+ helper T cells attracted to cutaneous antigens; starts as an erythrodermic rash, then progresses through plaque (flat, slightly raised) to tumor (nodular) stages; the lymphocytes have a distinctive cerebriform nuclear appearance (convoluted/folded, like gyri and sulci — "little mini-brains"), and infiltrate both epidermis and dermis
  • Important point: as a standalone diagnosis, mycosis fungoides behaves as a relatively low-grade malignancy — patients can live with it for a long time
Anecdote with a real teaching pointDr. Kemp recounts a residency case where a dermatology resident and a dermatopathologist went back and forth on the differential for a case that "looks like mycosis fungoides" but could technically represent several histologic mimics — until the attending simply said "fine, it's mycosis fungoides, next case," deferring to the strong clinical impression. The point: in dermatopathology, microscopic findings are frequently non-specific and can fit more than one diagnosis — clinical correlation is often what actually establishes the diagnosis, not the histology in isolation.

Sézary Syndrome

The leukemic phase — same cerebriform cells, now circulating, and a worse prognosis
  • Description: the malignant (cerebriform) lymphocytes of mycosis fungoides spill out into the peripheral blood
  • Features: erythroderma, pruritus, lymphadenopathy, alopecia
  • Important point: a more significant/worse-prognosis disease than mycosis fungoides confined to the skin — likely related to blood/systemic involvement
Practice Question

Practice Question

31 · Mastocytosis

Mastocytosis

Increased mast cells in skin ± internal organs — histamine/heparin release drives the clinical picture; KIT is the molecular marker
  • Description: increased numbers of mast cells in the skin, and possibly internal organs
  • Forms:
    • Cutaneous — often children — called urticaria pigmentosa
    • Systemic — often adults — involves internal organs
  • Effects: due to release of histamine and heparin
    • Darier sign — local dermal edema (a wheal/flare) when a lesion is rubbed/stroked — cutaneous, hallmark exam finding
    • Systemic: bone pain (from mast cell infiltration of bone) → can progress to osteoporosis; rhinorrhea; pruritus and flushing
  • Molecular marker: KIT
Physical-exam pattern worth grouping togetherDr. Kemp points out that mastocytosis (Darier sign) belongs to a small group of skin diseases where physically manipulating/traumatizing the lesion produces a diagnostic change — he draws the parallel to psoriasis (Auspitz sign — pinpoint bleeding when scale is scraped off) and epidermolysis bullosa (blistering with minor trauma, Nikolsky-type phenomenon). If a question stem describes a skin finding that changes with rubbing/scratching/trauma, think through this whole family of diagnoses.
Practice Question

32 · Ichthyosis

Ichthyosis

"Fish scale" skin from defective shedding of the stratum corneum
  • Description: excessive keratin build-up that grossly resembles fish scales (source of the name)
  • Forms: hereditary (e.g., ichthyosis vulgaris — autosomal dominant; X-linked ichthyosis) or acquired
  • Pathogenesis: defective desquamation — normally, the epidermis constantly sheds enormous numbers of dead keratinocytes; in ichthyosis, the mutations impair the ability of maturing keratinocytes to slough off, so they simply accumulate, producing thick, dry layers that create the scaly appearance
De-emphasized content (per lecture)Dr. Kemp deliberately left the specific gene/mutation-level detail for the various hereditary ichthyoses out of the lecture, saying it's "kind of, uh, really too detailed" for this course level — know the general mechanism (defective desquamation) and the two named hereditary forms, but don't expect to be tested on molecular specifics for this topic the way you would for, e.g., BCC or DFSP.
Practice Question
A patient has diffuse, dry, scaly skin resembling fish scales, most prominent on the extremities. Biopsy shows marked hyperkeratosis. What is the underlying pathogenic defect?

33 · Urticaria & Acute Eczematous Dermatitis

Acute Inflammatory Dermatoses — General Features

Days-to-weeks duration; inflammation + edema ± epidermal injury — but typically mononuclear, not neutrophilic
  • Description: acute skin lesions lasting days to weeks; feature inflammation, edema, and sometimes epidermal injury
  • Important point: unlike most acute inflammation (which you'd expect to be neutrophil-predominant), acute inflammatory dermatoses are usually mononuclear-cell/lymphocyte predominant
  • Forms to know: urticaria, acute eczematous dermatitis, erythema multiforme
Kemp's teaching device — predict before you're toldHe deliberately pauses before giving the description and asks students to predict the morphology purely from the name/category ("acute" + "inflammatory" + "dermatosis") — reinforcing that a lot of dermpath, like other systems, is built on general principles you already know (inflammation = edema, cellular infiltrate, possible tissue injury) rather than pure memorization. He draws the same-logic parallel to viral myocarditis: "acute" doesn't always mean neutrophilic — myocarditis is acute but lymphocyte-predominant, just like these dermatoses.

Urticaria

Wheals from mast cell degranulation — usually a clinical, not a biopsy, diagnosis
  • Description: erythematous, edematous, pruritic plaques called wheals, due to mast cell degranulation; individual lesions usually fade within a day, though episodes/flares can recur over days to months if exposure continues
  • Pathogenesis: most cases are a type I hypersensitivity reaction (IgE-mediated mast cell degranulation)
  • Etiologies: viruses, pollens, foods, drugs, insect venom
  • Other mechanisms (non-IgE): direct mast cell degranulation (e.g., radiographic contrast media); direct increase in vascular permeability (e.g., aspirin)
  • Microscopic (when biopsied): sparse perivascular inflammatory infiltrate; visible spaces between collagen bundles (dermal edema — the fluid itself isn't visualized, but the spaces it creates are)
Practical pointUrticaria is usually a clinical diagnosis based on history of exposure — biopsy is uncommon, since the findings (sparse perivascular infiltrate, dermal edema) are non-specific. It's mentioned here mainly because these are the two exam vignettes Dr. Kemp actually wrote for this topic.

Acute Eczematous Dermatitis

"Eczema" literally means "to boil over" — a family of dermatitides named by their trigger, not their appearance
  • Description: erythematous papules that can develop vesicles and ooze; pruritic; lesions can coalesce into plaques
  • Forms/etiology (named by cause, not appearance):
    • Allergic contact dermatitis — an allergen
    • Atopic dermatitis — defect in keratinocyte barrier function
    • Drug-related eczematous dermatitis — drugs
    • Photo-eczematous dermatitis — UV or visible light
    • Primary irritant dermatitis — a substance that directly injures the epidermis
  • Important point: can be triggered by external OR internal delivery of the antigen
Kemp's own exampleHe describes developing recurrent red, itchy spots in his axilla that he initially treated (unsuccessfully) as a fungal infection, before realizing it was a fragrance/chemical component of his deodorant — an allergic contact dermatitis, which resolved once he switched to an unscented product.

Allergic Contact Dermatitis — Detail

  • Pathogenesis: type IV hypersensitivity reaction
  • Morphology: erythematous, papulovesicular, oozing and crusted lesions; acanthosis and hyperkeratosis produce plaques over time; spongiosis (intercellular epidermal edema) is the key microscopic finding

34 · Erythema Multiforme, Stevens-Johnson Syndrome & Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis

Erythema Multiforme

CD8+ T-cell-mediated keratinocyte apoptosis — a clinical/histologic spectrum from limited disease to a burn-like emergency
  • Pathogenesis: epithelial injury due to CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes directed against the skin
  • Triggers: infections (herpes simplex, mycoplasma, fungi); drugs (sulfonamides, penicillin, salicylates, anti-malarials); cancer; collagen vascular disease
  • Morphology (gross): can have macules, papules, vesicles, and bullae — "multiforme" — often forms a targetoid lesion
  • Morphology (microscopic): lymphocytic infiltrate at the dermal-epidermal junction, associated with keratinocyte apoptosis, which can become confluent
Clinical takeawayAny patient presenting with a new rash should be asked (1) about any recent new exposures (drugs especially) and (2) about underlying chronic disease processes, particularly autoimmune conditions — both are relevant to the erythema multiforme/SJS/TEN differential.

37 · Chronic Inflammatory Dermatoses & Psoriasis

Chronic Inflammatory Dermatoses — General Features

Months-to-years duration; classically produce a rough skin surface
  • Description: skin lesions lasting months to years; usually produce a rough skin surface, largely due to parakeratosis and/or hyperkeratosis
  • Forms to know: psoriasis, seborrheic dermatitis, lichen planus, lichen simplex chronicus

Psoriasis

Salmon-colored plaque with adherent silvery scale — an autoimmune keratinocyte-proliferation disease
  • Demographics: common, ~1–2% of the population
  • Risk factor: HLA-Cω*0602
  • Pathogenesis: autoimmune; CD4+ TH17 and TH1 cells plus CD8+ T cells in the epidermis secrete cytokines/growth factors that drive marked keratinocyte proliferation (pronounced acanthosis/"serpiginous"-type downward rete-ridge growth)
  • Koebner phenomenon: a new psoriatic lesion can form at a site of trauma in someone who already has psoriasis
  • Gross morphology: salmon-colored plaque with adherent white-silver scale; can cause yellow-brown nail changes
  • Microscopic: acanthosis with downward elongation of rete ridges ("serpiginous"/ downgrowth pattern); loss of stratum granulosum; prominent parakeratosis; thinning of epidermis over dermal papillae → removal of the scale/plaque exposes the vascular dermal papillae and causes pinpoint bleeding = Auspitz sign; collections of neutrophils in the stratum corneum = Munro's microabscesses; mild lymphocytic infiltrate
  • Location: classically extensor surfaces (elbows, knees), scalp, lumbosacral/gluteal cleft; can involve genitalia and nails
Personal/family framingKemp mentions a family member with psoriasis (severe scalp involvement) and that it's commonly seen at autopsy — reinforcing how common this condition is. He also flags that the thick retained parakeratotic/hyperkeratotic scale, which normally would slough off, creates a real risk for secondary infection.
Mechanism chainThin epidermis over dermal papillae (loss of granular layer + parakeratosis) → papillae (which contain capillaries) become exposed with any plaque removal → pinpoint bleeding = Auspitz sign.

38 · Seborrheic Dermatitis, Lichen Planus & Lichen Simplex Chronicus

Seborrheic Dermatitis

"Dandruff" — very common, pathogenesis uncertain
  • Demographics: affects up to 5% of the population (i.e., common ordinary dandruff)
  • Location: scalp, forehead, external auditory canal, retroauricular area — anywhere with a prominence of sebaceous glands
  • Pathogenesis: uncertain
Case story — why "just dandruff" isn't always trivialKemp describes an infant with severe seborrheic dermatitis whose parents tried home remedies; it progressed to scalp hair loss and spread to the trunk with complications. A second child born into the same family had a similar presentation, suggesting a familial component even though seborrheic dermatitis is usually thought of as sporadic and benign.

Lichen Planus

The 4 P's — Purple, Pruritic, Polygonal Papules/Plaques
  • Description: papules and plaques that are purple, pruritic, and polygonal
  • Pathogenesis: CD8+ T cells act against antigens in the basal cell layer/dermal-epidermal junction
  • Gross morphology: white dots/lines on the plaques = Wickham striae; melanin loss into the dermis
  • Microscopic: prominent interface dermatitis (inflammation right at the dermal-epidermal junction); atrophy/necrosis of individual basal keratinocytes = Civatte bodies (Kemp likens this to the scattered single-cell necrosis of Councilman bodies in hepatitis, rather than the confluent injury of erythema multiforme); chronic features: epidermal hyperplasia, hyperkeratosis
  • Location: wrists, elbows; can involve genitalia and oral mucosa
Kemp's teaching noteHe explicitly compares lichen planus's individual-cell basal necrosis (Civatte bodies) to Councilman bodies of viral hepatitis rather than to the more confluent epidermal injury of erythema multiforme — a useful way to calibrate "how much" necrosis to expect.

Lichen Simplex Chronicus

Rough skin from constant scratching/rubbing — a response to chronic mechanical trauma, not a primary disease
  • Description: rough skin from constant irritation/scratching (e.g., in someone with chronic pruritus); can form nodules = prurigo nodularis
  • Microscopic: acanthosis, hyperkeratosis, hypergranulosis; dermal inflammatory infiltrate (expected with any chronic inflammatory process)
  • Important point: changes resemble those normally seen on the palms/soles — sites of routine repetitive trauma — reinforcing that this is simply the skin's thickening response to chronic mechanical irritation
Clinical correlation reminderKemp repeats a theme from earlier lectures: dermpath findings are frequently nonspecific and need clinical correlation. Many chronic-process features (acanthosis, hyperkeratosis, dermal infiltrate) overlap across multiple chronic dermatoses.

Pemphigus — General Features

IgG against desmosomal proteins → keratinocytes fall apart → flaccid bullae
  • Pathogenesis: IgG antibodies against desmosomal proteins (desmoglein 1 and 3), which join keratinocytes together — disrupting these causes keratinocyte separation (acantholysis)
  • Demographics: older adults; women > men; medications can induce pemphigus foliaceus
  • Gross: flaccid bullae (contrast with the tense bullae of bullous pemphigoid) — because the outer "roof" is made of cells that are themselves falling apart, they rupture easily
  • Microscopic: suprabasal acantholytic blister (separation among keratinocytes, not at the dermal-epidermal junction)
  • Immunofluorescence: linear deposits along the keratinocytes themselves (versus along the dermal-epidermal junction in bullous pemphigoid)

40 · Bullous Pemphigoid & Dermatitis Herpetiformis

Bullous Pemphigoid

IgG against hemidesmosomes → tense, subepidermal bullae that heal without scarring
  • Pathogenesis: IgG antibodies against hemidesmosomal proteins (type XVII collagen) at the dermal-epidermal junction; fixes complement → tissue injury via neutrophils/eosinophils
  • Gross: tense bullae (the epidermis lifts off the dermis largely intact — contrast with pemphigus, where the epidermis itself falls apart)
  • Microscopic: subepidermal bulla (separation between epidermis and dermis); no acantholysis
  • Immunofluorescence: linear deposits along the dermal-epidermal junction (not along individual keratinocytes)
  • Important point: heals with no scarring — despite affecting the dermal-epidermal junction (which one might intuitively expect to scar more than pemphigus vulgaris, which doesn't scar)
  • Gestational pemphigoid: a bullous pemphigoid variant that can occur in the 2nd or 3rd trimester of pregnancy and typically resolves after childbirth
Kemp flags this as counterintuitiveHe notes it seems paradoxical that bullous pemphigoid (affecting the dermal-epidermal junction) heals without scarring, while pemphigus vulgaris (affecting only the epidermis) does not — he speculates this may relate to the flaccid vs. tense nature/cleavage plane of each blister, but flags it as something Robbins doesn't fully explain mechanistically.

Dermatitis Herpetiformis

Small, intensely pruritic vesicles strongly linked to celiac disease — granular IgA at dermal papillae tips
  • Pathogenesis: associated with gluten sensitivity; IgA antibodies cross-react with epidermal transglutaminase/endomysium
  • Demographics: males, 3rd–4th decade; ~80% have celiac disease
  • Gross: markedly pruritic vesicles and papules (small — "herpetiform")
  • Microscopic: neutrophils at the tips of the dermal papillae, forming microabscesses/vacuolization that becomes a blister
  • Immunofluorescence: granular IgA deposits at the dermal-epidermal junction/papillary tips (contrast with the linear patterns of pemphigus/pemphigoid)
  • Location: bilateral, symmetrical — extensor surfaces (elbows, knees), upper back, buttocks
History-driven vignette patternKemp explicitly notes that if a board question gives a history of celiac disease alongside vesicular skin lesions, that history is doing the diagnostic work for dermatitis herpetiformis — a strong testable association to keep in mind.

41 · Epidermolysis Bullosa & Porphyria

Epidermolysis Bullosa

Inherited structural-protein defect → blisters at sites of trauma, present from birth
  • Description: inherited condition due to a defect in a structural protein, causing instability of the skin — trauma triggers blister formation at the site of injury
  • Important point: present at birth / manifests soon after — an inherited condition, not acquired
  • Forms (know that different forms affect different structural layers — Kemp is explicit that you will not be asked to identify a specific subtype by name, only to understand the overall pathogenesis and clinical presentation):
    • Simplex type: affects the basal cell layer; keratin 14 (or 5)
    • Junctional type: affects the lamina lucida; laminin
    • Dystrophic type: mutations of type VII collagen (COL7A1)
    • Mixed type
Kemp's explicit exam guidance"I promise you there's no test question that's going to ask you to identify one of those [EB] forms" — know the overall disease concept (inherited defect → trauma-induced blistering, presents in infancy/childhood) rather than memorizing which protein maps to which subtype.

Porphyria

Sun-exacerbated vesicles/scarring from a heme-synthesis defect — minimal detail given in Robbins
  • Features: urticaria and vesicles that lead to scarring; exacerbated by sunlight exposure
  • Microscopic: subepidermal vesicles; thickened dermal vessel walls due to deposited serum proteins
  • Forms: congenital erythropoietic porphyria, erythrohepatic protoporphyria, acute intermittent porphyria, porphyria cutanea tarda, mixed porphyria
Kemp's candid note on depthHe states Robbins gives very little detail on porphyria beyond these basic features, and mentions (with appropriate hedging — "don't quote me on that") that acute intermittent porphyria is classically considered in the differential diagnosis of abdominal pain, a fact from outside this specific lecture's source material.

43 · Acne Vulgaris & Rosacea

Disorders of Epidermal Appendages — Overview

Acne vulgaris and rosacea — both extremely common, both incompletely understood

Acne Vulgaris

Open vs. closed comedones — the closed ones are the ones that rupture and inflame
  • Demographics: most common in middle-to-late teenage years, though can occur at any age
  • Risk factors:
    • Drugs: corticosteroids, testosterone, contraceptives, iodides
    • Conditions promoting occlusion of sebaceous glands: cosmetics, heavy clothing
  • Forms: inflammatory and non-inflammatory
    • Open comedones: small papule with a central black keratin plug (from melanin) — exposed to the outside
    • Closed comedones: follicular papule with no central plug — because they're occluded, they become the source for follicle rupture and inflammation
  • Pathogenesis: not completely understood, likely multifactorial — possible mechanisms include keratinization blocking sebum outflow, sebaceous gland hypertrophy, lipase-synthesizing bacteria colonizing the hair follicle, and secondary follicular inflammation
Kemp's asideHe notes that despite how common and clinically important acne is, its pathogenesis still isn't fully worked out — an example, in his view, of a very common/benign-seeming condition that just hasn't had the same depth of mechanistic study as rarer diseases.

Rosacea

"Adult acne" with a distinct progression — flushing → telangiectasia → papules/pustules → rhinophyma
  • Demographics: middle age and older; ~3% of the population
  • Stages (in order):
    1. Flushing
    2. Persistent erythema and telangiectasia (dilated blood vessels — the same finding seen on the surface of basal cell carcinoma)
    3. Pustules and papules
    4. Rhinophyma — permanent thickening of the nasal skin (chronic end-stage change)
  • Pathogenesis: high cutaneous concentrations of cathelicidin (an antimicrobial peptide), processed by proteases such as kallikrein 5; toll-like receptors (TLR2) upregulate kallikrein 5
Cross-referenceKemp explicitly links the telangiectasias of rosacea back to the same finding used to identify basal cell carcinoma on gross exam — a good visual/conceptual anchor across two different lecture topics.

44 · Panniculitis

Panniculitis — Overview

Inflammation of subcutaneous fat — erythema nodosum and erythema induratum are the two to know cold
  • Forms to know: erythema nodosum, erythema induratum
  • Rare forms (name recognition only): Weber-Christian disease (relapsing febrile nodular panniculitis), factitial panniculitis, T-cell lymphoma, lupus erythematosus
Kemp's "Point to Ponder" — factitial panniculitisHe poses this as a reflection question before answering it himself: what's a likely common cause of factitial panniculitis, given how often it's seen at autopsy? His own working answer is injection-site trauma — e.g., repeated insulin or heparin injections in diabetic patients causing local bruising/inflammation at the injection site, since the inflammatory response is directly caused by the injection itself ("factitial" = self-induced/iatrogenic).

Erythema Nodosum

Tender plaques/nodules, often "better palpated than seen" — think delayed hypersensitivity
  • Description: tender, erythematous plaques and nodules — often better felt on exam than visually striking
  • Associations: β-hemolytic streptococcal infection, tuberculosis, drugs (sulfonamides), sarcoidosis, inflammatory bowel disease (most often lower extremities), neoplasia
  • Pathogenesis: likely a delayed (type IV) hypersensitivity reaction
  • Morphology (time-dependent):
    • Early: edema, fibrin, neutrophils (acute-process pattern)
    • Later: lymphocytes, macrophages, multinucleated giant cells, septal fibrosis (chronic-process pattern)
Kemp's "lymphocytes ≠ automatically chronic" caveatHe flags that seeing lymphocytes doesn't always mean a chronic process — lymphocytic myocarditis, for example, is an acute disease that's still lymphocyte-predominant. But for erythema nodosum specifically, the early/late progression above does hold (edema/fibrin/neutrophils early → lymphocytes/macrophages/giant cells/fibrosis later).

Erythema Induratum

Vasculitis → fat necrosis; tender, often-ulcerated nodules
  • Demographics: menopausal women and adolescents
  • Pathogenesis: likely a vasculitis leading to fat necrosis and inflammation
  • Gross: erythematous, tender nodule that most often ulcerates
  • Microscopic: granulomas and caseous necrosis; if caught early, may show the underlying necrotizing vasculitis

45 · Verrucae & Molluscum Contagiosum

Verrucae (Warts)

HPV-driven squamous proliferation — three forms named by location/appearance
  • Etiology: proliferation of squamous cells stimulated by HPV infection; most regress within 2 years
  • Important point: verrucae express E6 and E7 proteins (E6 targets p53, E7 targets retinoblastoma) but these are structurally different from the E6/E7 of the high-risk, cancer-associated HPV types — so verrucae are not a significant cancer risk (with rare exceptions)
  • Forms:
    • Verruca vulgaris (raised) — hands/periungual areas
    • Verruca plana (flat) — face and hands
    • Verruca plantaris/palmaris (callus-like) — soles and palms
  • Gross: papules (spiny/irregular rather than smoothly rounded for plantaris/palmaris)
  • Microscopic: epidermal hyperplasia (undulating pattern); koilocytes; keratohyalin granules
Kemp's own plantar wart storyHe describes having plantar warts (verruca plantaris) that spread to both feet before treatment, causing him to walk on the outer rims of his feet from pain — treated by electrocautery after a numbing injection ("this is going to hurt when I inject you... I think I left my fingerprints on it"). They resolved completely and never recurred.

Molluscum Contagiosum

Central umbilication + eosinophilic molluscum bodies — caused by the largest known DNA virus
  • Gross: common on the trunk and anogenital region; firm, pruritic, skin-colored papule up to ~0.4 cm with characteristic central umbilication
  • Microscopic: cup-like hyperplasia of the epidermis (the "cup" corresponds to the central umbilication); homogeneous eosinophilic inclusions in the stratum corneum/granulosum = molluscum bodies
  • Etiology: a poxvirus (~300 nm, DNA virus — the largest known virus, nearly visible by light microscopy)
Scale detail Kemp emphasizesHe notes that at ~300 nm, this poxvirus is "almost big enough" that you could nearly resolve it under a standard light microscope — an unusual and memorable fact for a virus.

46 · Impetigo & Superficial Fungal Infections

Impetigo

Honey-colored crust from a superficial bacterial infection that cleaves desmoglein 1 — same target as pemphigus foliaceus
  • Description: superficial skin infection from direct contact; most commonly involves the face and hands
  • Gross: erythematous macule → rapidly developing pustules → rupture → erosions covered with dried serum = honey-colored crust (the classic, highly testable description)
  • Microscopic: neutrophils just beneath the stratum corneum, producing a subcorneal pustule
  • Important point: heals completely, because there is no involvement of the dermis
  • Etiology: historically mostly Streptococcus pyogenes; now Staphylococcus aureus is the major (in some places, dominant) cause
  • Pathogenesis: a bacterial toxin cleaves desmoglein 1 — the same target protein attacked by autoantibodies in pemphigus foliaceus — causing loss of cell-cell adhesion in the upper epidermis
Kemp's explicit cross-topic comparisonHe deliberately draws the parallel between impetigo's bacterial toxin and pemphigus foliaceus's autoantibodies: both target desmoglein 1 and produce a superficial split — one infectious, one autoimmune. He also notes his own confusion about why impetigo (dermis-sparing) heals without scarring but pemphigus vulgaris (also epidermis-only) does not — flagging it as a point Robbins doesn't fully resolve.

Superficial Fungal Infections

Location tells you the name — "tinea + body region"
  • Tinea capitis: scalp in children ("cradle cap"-type term for scalp fungal infection)
  • Tinea barbae: beard area, adult males
  • Tinea corporis: the body
  • Tinea cruris: inguinal region ("jock itch")
  • Tinea pedis: "athlete's foot"
  • Tinea versicolor: characteristic fine peripheral scale; caused by Malassezia furfur (a yeast) — not a true "tinea"/dermatophyte despite the name
Study strategy Kemp suggestsUnderstanding the Latin/anatomic root of each "tinea ___" term tells you the location directly — much of this section is about knowing terminology rather than memorizing disconnected facts.

47 · Paraneoplastic Syndromes & Rash Differential

Paraneoplastic Syndromes of the Skin

Acanthosis nigricans, dermatomyositis, Sweet syndrome
  • Acanthosis nigricans: hyperkeratosis and pigmentation affecting flexural areas (axilla, neck, anogenital region); >50% of cases have an associated cancer, ~90% of those being GI tract cancers (especially gastric) — though it is also very commonly seen with obesity/diabetes and is not always paraneoplastic
  • Dermatomyositis: can be paraneoplastic (covered in more detail elsewhere in the course)
  • Sweet syndrome: fever, elevated neutrophil count, painful red plaques (face, neck, arms); microscopically dominated by neutrophils; ~1/5 of cases are associated with malignancy (often hematologic)
Kemp's clinical experience noteHe states that in his own experience seeing acanthosis nigricans, it's most often associated with obesity and/or diabetes rather than an underlying GI malignancy — a reminder that the "classic paraneoplastic association" is real but not the most common real-world driver of this finding.
