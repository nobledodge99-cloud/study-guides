Part 1: Dermatology Pharmacology Principles

Why Dermatology Pharmacology Is Different

  • Many drugs are applied directly at the site of disease — effect depends on drug + vehicle + site + barrier integrity + duration + surface area treated
  • Stratum corneum = outermost layer of epidermis, made of non-viable keratinocytes in lipid layers = primary barrier to topical penetration
  • Intact, dry, thick skin → limits absorption. Damaged, inflamed, hydrated, or occluded skin → absorbs more (can increase therapeutic effect or local toxicity/systemic exposure)

Body Site Affects Absorption & Safety

Site typeExamplesClinical implication
Thin skin (high absorption)Face, eyelids, skin folds, genitalsHigher toxicity risk — use lower-potency agents
Thick skin (low absorption)Palms, soles, thick plaquesMay need higher potency, occlusive vehicle, or keratolytic to improve delivery
  • Larger treatment area → greater systemic exposure risk
  • Children (especially infants) have a higher surface-area-to-body-mass ratio → greater relative systemic exposure than adults with the same application

Vehicle Selection — "the vehicle is part of the prescription"

VehicleBest forMain limitation
OintmentDry, thick, scaly, lichenified lesions (most occlusive → ↑ hydration/penetration)Greasy, low cosmetic acceptability, may worsen acne/folliculitis
CreamVersatile — many inflammatory lesionsLess occlusive than ointment
Lotion/solution/foamHair-bearing areas (scalp)May be less hydrating; can sting
GelAcne-prone/oily areasDrying, irritating

Topical vs. Systemic Decision

Localized/superficial disease → usually starts topical (direct delivery, lower systemic exposure). Systemic therapy needed when disease is severe, widespread, refractory, deep, involves nails/hair, or is immune-mediated.

Part 2a: Anti-inflammatory Therapies

Used for itch/erythema/edema/barrier-disruption conditions — atopic dermatitis (eczema), contact dermatitis, localized inflammatory rashes.

Topical Corticosteroids CORE

Role: First-line topical anti-inflammatory — broadly suppress local inflammatory signaling → ↓ erythema, edema, pruritus. Some local vasoconstriction contributes to ↓ redness.

Potency ladder (know this cold):

PotencyExampleTypical use
LowHydrocortisoneFace/folds/genitals, children, mild inflammation
MediumTriamcinoloneTrunk/extremities, moderate inflammation
HighBetamethasone, fluocinonideSevere inflammation, thicker skin, short courses
Super-highClobetasolThick plaques/palms/soles; very short courses/caution

Safety — risk ↑ with: higher potency, longer duration, thin skin (face/folds/genitals), large surface area, occlusion, damaged/inflamed skin, younger age.

Major AEs: skin atrophy (most important), striae, telangiectasias, hypopigmentation, bruising/fragility, steroid acne, folliculitis, perioral dermatitis; possible systemic absorption → HPA-axis suppression/Cushingoid effects with high exposure.

Safety principle: lowest effective potency, appropriate site, shortest duration needed.

Tacrolimus & Pimecrolimus (Topical Calcineurin Inhibitors) CORE

MOA: Inhibit calcineurin signaling in T cells → ↓ T-cell activation → ↓ inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-2). Do not cause skin atrophy.

Role: Steroid-sparing anti-inflammatory, primarily atopic dermatitis; useful for sensitive/thin areas (face, eyelids, neck, folds, genitals) or long-term intermittent control.

AEs: burning/stinging/pruritus/erythema at application site (often improves with continued use).

Boxed warning: possible malignancy risk (lymphoma, skin cancer) — avoid unnecessary UV exposure, use sun protection.

Avoid in: children <2 years, immunocompromised patients, under occlusive dressings; avoid prolonged continuous use.

Part 2b: Anti-infective & Antiparasitic Therapies

Framework: (1) identify likely cause (bacterial/fungal/viral/parasitic) → (2) topical vs. systemic (localized/superficial = topical; deep/widespread/recurrent/nail-hair = systemic) → (3) organism-specific issues (MRSA coverage? dermatophyte vs. Candida?).

Bacterial Skin Infections

Usually gram-positive: S. aureus, S. pyogenes.

Mupirocin CORE

Role: Core topical agent for localized infections (impetigo); also used for S. aureus/MRSA decolonization. Inhibits bacterial protein synthesis, active mainly vs. gram-positive organisms.

AEs: local irritation. Stewardship: prolonged/unnecessary use → resistance.

Oral Antibiotic Logic CORE CONCEPT

  • Non-purulent cellulitis → strep coverage → cephalexin (β-lactam) is the common outpatient example
  • Suspected MRSA (purulence, abscess, prior MRSA, penetrating trauma) → TMP-SMX, doxycycline, or clindamycin

Recognize & Associate

Bacitracin / polymyxin B / neomycin — OTC topical antibiotics; key concern is allergic contact dermatitis (esp. neomycin/bacitracin); not substitutes for systemic therapy when infection is deeper/spreading.

Fungal Skin & Nail Infections

OrganismPatternExamples
DermatophytesInfect keratinized tissue: skin, hair, nailsTinea pedis/corporis/cruris/capitis, onychomycosis
CandidaYeast; moist areas, folds, mucosaIntertrigo, diaper dermatitis, thrush, vulvovaginal candidiasis

Azoles (as a class) CORE

MOA: Inhibit fungal ergosterol synthesis. Use: dermatophytes and Candida.

Topical: clotrimazole, miconazole, ketoconazole → local irritation. Systemic: fluconazole, itraconazole → hepatotoxicity, QT prolongation, CYP3A4 interactions.

Terbinafine CORE

MOA: Inhibits squalene epoxidase (different target than azoles). Especially useful for dermatophytes.

Topical: localized tinea. Oral: onychomycosis (nail has to grow out — ~6 wks fingernails, ~12 wks toenails). Oral form utilized for alopecia - tinea infection affecting the hair.

Major AE: hepatotoxicity (oral) — avoid in active liver disease; LFT monitoring with prolonged therapy.

Nystatin CORE

MOA: Binds ergosterol, disrupts fungal cell membrane. Use: localized Candida infections only — intertrigo, diaper dermatitis, VVC, oral thrush. Minimal systemic absorption.

Does NOT treat dermatophytes or systemic fungal infections.

Recognize & Associate

Ciclopirox, efinaconazole, tavaboroleTopical onychomycosis options — lower cure rates than oral, used when systemic not appropriate
GriseofulvinOlder oral option for dermatophytes, esp. tinea capitis; not active against Candida

Viral Skin Infections

Acyclovir & Valacyclovir CORE

MOA: Nucleoside analogs → inhibit viral DNA replication; require activation by viral thymidine kinase (concentrates activity in infected cells, limits toxicity to uninfected cells).

Use: HSV (oral/genital herpes) and VZV (varicella, herpes zoster). Most effective when started early (prodrome). Do not eradicate latent virus.

Acyclovir: topical/oral/IV — topical has limited benefit (mild herpes labialis only). AEs: GI upset, headache; nephrotoxicity risk with dehydration/high dose/renal impairment.

Valacyclovir: prodrug of acyclovir, better oral bioavailability, less frequent dosing; similar AEs; renal dose adjustment may be needed.

Oral therapy preferred for: genital herpes, recurrent disease, herpes zoster, severe disease, immunocompromised patients.

Ectoparasiticides

Permethrin CORE

MOA: Topical neurotoxic agent — disrupts sodium channel function → paralysis/death of parasite.

Use: First-line for scabies (5% cream, applied to body, left on several hours before washing); also used for lice (1% product on hair/scalp).

AEs: local burning/stinging/itching/irritation. Generally preferred in young children and pregnancy vs. many alternatives.

Ivermectin CORE

MOA: Increases chloride influx through parasite channels → paralysis/death.

Topical: head lice. Oral: severe/crusted/refractory/outbreak-associated scabies.

AEs: pruritus, rash, dizziness, headache, GI symptoms. Oral use not established safe in pregnancy/very small children — generally avoided.

Treatment "failure" pearl: apparent failure often ≠ ineffective drug — think incorrect application, failure to repeat treatment, reinfection from untreated contact, or inadequate environmental measures. Pruritus can persist after parasites are killed.

Recognize & Associate

Spinosad (neuronal hyperexcitation, effective without nit combing) and malathion (organophosphate AChE inhibitor, flammable) — both for head lice.

Seborrheic Dermatitis

Malassezia-associated inflammatory condition (not invasive fungal infection). Affects scalp (dandruff), face, eyebrows, nasolabial folds, ears, chest.

Recognize & Associate

Ketoconazole (shampoo/cream), selenium sulfide (may discolor light/treated hair), zinc pyrithione — anti-yeast agents; more inflamed flares may add a short course of low-potency topical corticosteroid or calcineurin inhibitor.

Part 3a: Acne & Rosacea Therapies

Acne vulgaris driven by: follicular plugging, ↑ sebum, C. acnes, and inflammation → combination therapy targets different parts of the process.

Benzoyl Peroxide CORE

Role: mild–moderate (esp. inflammatory) acne. Reduces C. acnes, mild anti-inflammatory + mild keratolytic effect. Does not promote antibiotic resistance — often combined with topical antibiotics to reduce resistance development.

AEs: irritation, dryness, redness, peeling, burning; bleaches fabric/hair — counsel patients.

Topical Retinoids — tretinoin, adapalene, tazarotene CORE

MOA: Vitamin A derivatives — normalize follicular keratinization, ↓ comedone formation, prevent new lesions ("foundational" for follicular plugging).

AEs: dryness, peeling, erythema, burning/stinging, photosensitivity (worst early in treatment — start gradually, moisturize, apply at night, sunscreen by day).

Pregnancy: generally avoided; tazarotene has the strongest pregnancy-related contraindication.

Topical Antibiotics — clindamycin, erythromycin CORE

Role: inflammatory acne — ↓ C. acnes + anti-inflammatory. Never use as monotherapy (resistance risk) — combine with benzoyl peroxide.

AEs: local irritation, dryness, rarely allergic dermatitis.

Oral Tetracyclines — doxycycline, minocycline CORE

Role: moderate/more inflammatory acne when topical alone is insufficient — systemic anti-inflammatory + antibacterial. Used for a limited course, not indefinite therapy; continue topical therapy alongside.

AEs: GI upset, photosensitivity (esp. doxycycline).

Avoid in: pregnancy, young children (teeth/bone effects).

Key interaction: do not combine with isotretinoin → intracranial hypertension risk.

Isotretinoin CORE

Role: severe nodulocystic/scarring acne or acne refractory to other therapy — the most comprehensive agent (hits multiple pathways: ↓ sebum, ↓ follicular plugging, ↓ C. acnes, ↓ inflammation).

AEs: mucocutaneous — dry lips/skin/eyes, epistaxis (most common). More serious: hypertriglyceridemia, hepatotoxicity, musculoskeletal symptoms, mood-related concerns, intracranial hypertension (esp. with tetracyclines).

Most important issue: teratogenicity — severe birth defects; tightly regulated via iPLEDGE program; pregnancy testing/contraception counseling required. Monitor lipids and LFTs.

Part 3b: Immunomodulatory Psoriasis Therapies

Chronic, relapsing immune-mediated disease. Treatment escalates: topical → phototherapy → traditional systemic → biologic, based on severity/extent/psoriatic arthritis.

Topical Corticosteroids (Psoriasis) CORE

First-line for localized plaque psoriasis — same potency-matching principles as above. Major concern: skin atrophy/systemic absorption with prolonged/high-potency/large-area use.

Vitamin D Analogs — calcipotriene, calcitriol CORE

MOA: Regulate keratinocyte proliferation/differentiation (psoriasis = excess keratinocyte turnover). Steroid-sparing — often combined with topical corticosteroids.

AEs: local irritation/burning; hypercalcemia rare, but possible with excessive use over large areas.

Methotrexate CORE

Role: moderate–severe psoriasis, also relevant with psoriatic arthritis (helps skin + joints). Reduces immune/inflammatory activity.

AEs: hepatotoxicity, myelosuppression, mucositis, pulmonary toxicity, teratogenicity.

Monitoring: LFTs, CBC. Folic acid supplementation reduces toxicity.

Cyclosporine CORE

MOA: Calcineurin inhibitor → ↓ T-cell activation (systemic — potent immunosuppression).

Role: severe/refractory/rapidly progressive psoriasis — short-term disease control; not preferred for long-term maintenance due to toxicity.

AEs: nephrotoxicity, hypertension, ↑ infection risk, drug interactions.

Part 4: Abnormal Tissue–Directed Therapies

Goal here is not anti-inflammatory/anti-infective — it's to soften, remove, destroy, or modify abnormal tissue (hyperkeratotic lesions, warts, actinic keratoses, selected skin-cancer-related conditions). Local irritation/inflammation is often an expected part of treatment, not a sign of failure or allergy.

Salicylic Acid CORE

MOA: Keratolytic — breaks down/softens keratin, promotes shedding of thickened stratum corneum. Effect is concentration-dependent (lower = keratolysis/exfoliation; higher = more destructive to wart tissue).

Use: hyperkeratotic lesions (calluses, corns, scale) and common warts; also in some OTC acne products.

AEs: local irritation, burning, peeling, maceration. Systemic risk: salicylate toxicity with excessive use, large areas, occlusion, damaged skin, or in children.

Topical Fluorouracil (5-FU) CORE

MOA: Pyrimidine analog — interferes with DNA/RNA synthesis, greatest effect on rapidly proliferating abnormal cells.

Use: most commonly actinic keratosis; may be used in selected superficial BCC under specialist direction.

Counseling point: visible inflammation (erythema, burning, crusting, erosion, pain, peeling) is expected — not an allergic reaction or worsening disease. Sun protection important during therapy.

Imiquimod CORE

MOA: Immune response modifier — stimulates local innate immune signaling (interferon and other cytokines) rather than directly destroying tissue.

Use: external genital warts, actinic keratosis, superficial BCC.

AEs: local inflammatory reactions (erythema, itching, burning, erosion, crusting, flaking — part of the therapeutic effect); systemic flu-like symptoms possible but less common.

Podofilox & Podophyllum CORE

MOA: Antimitotic agents — interfere with microtubule function, inhibit mitosis, cause local tissue necrosis.

Use: anogenital warts. Podofilox = purified, patient-applied formulation. Podophyllum resin = older, less standardized, more toxic systemically.

AEs: local pain, burning, inflammation, erosion, ulceration. Avoid in pregnancy.

Clinical Integration Framework

Five questions for every dermatology pharmacology case:
  1. What is the skin problem? Inflammatory, infectious, acneiform, immune-mediated, or abnormal tissue growth?
  2. Local or systemic? Localized/superficial → topical first. Severe/widespread/deep/refractory/nail-hair/systemic → may need systemic therapy.
  3. Where is it applied? Face/folds/genitals vs. thick plaques/palms/soles/scalp — changes absorption & toxicity risk.
  4. How long is treatment needed? Short flare control vs. prolonged maintenance vs. long-term systemic therapy.
  5. What safety issue matters most? Irritation, atrophy, pregnancy risk, infection risk, organ toxicity, drug interactions, photosensitivity, monitoring.
Clinical theme: match the drug to the condition, the route to the severity, and the safety concern to the patient.
