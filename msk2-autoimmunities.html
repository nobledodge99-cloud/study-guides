00 · Intro, Objectives & Terms to Know

Learning Objectives

  • Explain the immunological mechanisms and components underlying joint, muscle, and skin autoimmunity, including T-cell– and antibody-mediated responses.
  • Describe the role of genetic and environmental factors in the initiation and progression of autoimmune disease.
  • Differentiate between the autoimmune diseases affecting the MSK and skin systems (RA, ankylosing spondylitis, autoimmune myositis, pemphigoid, vitiligo, alopecia areata, psoriasis, etc).
  • Evaluate the diagnostic markers and pathological features associated with these diseases, including rheumatoid factor, anti-CCP, and citrullination.
FramingThis lecture is deliberately not comprehensive — Dr. Nash focuses on the immune mechanism of pathogenesis, damage, and diagnosis for each disease. Detailed clinical diagnosis/pathology is covered elsewhere in the curriculum, so when you see a disease here, ask "which hypersensitivity type, which cell, which antigen?" rather than memorizing full clinical pictures.

Generation of New Epitopes: Citrullination

Peptidylarginine deaminase (PAD) converts positively-charged arginine residues in proteins to neutral citrulline. Loss of surface charge makes the protein more susceptible to proteolytic degradation. The resulting citrullinated peptides are presented by HLA class II to CD4 T cells — because citrullinated protein is not identical to the native protein, tolerance never developed against it, so it is now recognized as "foreign."

PearlMany self antigens will not trigger a response even after exposure without a danger signal. Molecular mimicry requires a pathogen epitope similar enough to a self antigen — and different enough from anything tolerance developed against — to break tolerance.

Genetics affects tolerance mechanisms and helps explain familial clustering of autoimmunity. The lecture's own framing: certain gene knockouts increase antigen persistence, lymphocyte activity duration, or reduce apoptosis — all pushing toward autoimmunity.

High-Yield FramingThree families to remember: lack of complement (↓ antigen clearance), lack of CD22/CTLA-4 (↑ duration of lymphocyte activity), and lack of Fas/FasL (↓ apoptosis → more necrosis → more danger-signal release).

Murine Model: CTL-Mediated Autoimmunity via Molecular Mimicry

A hybrid gene (rat insulin promoter + LCMV nucleoprotein) is expressed transgenically only in pancreatic β cells. By itself, NP expression in β cells provokes no T-cell response (no danger signal). When the mouse is then infected with LCMV, NP-specific CD8 T cells are activated by the actual infection, infiltrate the islets, and kill the NP-expressing β cells — producing diabetes. This models how a real infection could trigger autoimmune killing of cells that happen to (coincidentally or by mimicry) express a pathogen-like antigen.

Th1/Th17 Activation & Disease Associations

Dendritic cells receiving the correct maturation signals (e.g., β-glucan via dectin-1 → CARD9 → NF-κB) drive Th0 differentiation toward Th1 (IL-12/IL-12R/STAT4 → IFN-γ) or Th17 (IL-23/STAT3 → IL-17, IL-22) depending on cytokine milieu.

Disease AssociationsDiseases linked to this IL-12/IL-23–STAT axis (as annotated in lecture): ankylosing spondylitis, inflammatory bowel disease, psoriasis, multiple sclerosis, type 1 diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, SLE, ulcerative colitis.

Cognate B-cell/T-cell Help

T-cell–dependent B-cell activation, isotype switching, and somatic hypermutation all require CD40L (T cell) binding CD40 (B cell). For the T cell to deliver this signal, it must recognize antigen + class II MHC on that B cell. For the B cell to display antigen, it must have endocytosed it via BCR binding.

Key PointA B cell that internalizes a macromolecular complex (e.g., nucleosome with multiple linked proteins) can present any epitope from that complex — not just the one its BCR recognized. This lets a T cell specific for one component "help" a B cell specific for a different, linked component — a mechanism by which complexes can lead to help for self-reactive B cells.

Amplification Cycle of Autoimmune Damage

Damage from an autoimmune response releases more self antigen, which stimulates more of the response, which causes more damage — a self-reinforcing cycle. Example (B-cell activation cycle): a circulating autoreactive B cell binds self-antigen released from an injured cell → is activated by a self-peptide–specific T cell via class II MHC → differentiates into plasma cells secreting self-specific antibody → antibody triggers more inflammation and cell injury → more antigen is released → more B cells are recruited.

02 · Rheumatoid Arthritis

Rheumatoid Arthritis

A progressive, T-cell–predominant (with secondary antibody/immune-complex) arthritis of the small joints.

  • Progressive disease, mediated by type III and/or type IV hypersensitivity responses; most evident in the hands, but many joints can be affected.
  • T cells are usually the primary mediator, with antibodies playing a secondary role — but this relationship is not absolute.
  • Eventually, bones in the affected joint can actually fuse.

Pathogenesis

An unknown trigger sets up an initial focus of inflammation in the synovial membrane, attracting leukocytes into the tissue. Autoreactive CD4+ T cells activate macrophages, producing pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, IL-17, TNF-α) and sustaining inflammation. These cytokines induce fibroblasts to produce matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) (tissue destruction) and RANK ligand. RANKL activates osteoclasts, driving bone destruction. Th1, Th17, and antibodies all contribute, with a reinforcing cycle keeping the reaction going.

RANKRANK is a receptor (related to TNF) for RANK ligand; RANKL stimulates osteoclasts and drives bone degradation in RA.

Rheumatoid Factor (RF)

RF is an antibody — usually IgM — that reacts against self IgG as the antigen (antibody against antibody). RF-IgG complexes accumulate in the joints and contribute to type III hypersensitivity by activating complement and damaging joint tissue.

Anti-Citrullinated Protein Antibodies (ACPA / Anti-CCP)

Citrullination (arginine → citrulline via peptidylarginine deaminase) creates new epitopes on a variety of self proteins. Antibodies against these new epitopes are anti-citrullinated protein antibodies (anti-CCP/ACPA), and the new epitopes can be recognized by both T cells and B cells. Anti-CCP contributes to damage via: (1) immune-complex formation/deposition, (2) direct type II-style binding to cells with opsonization and complement activation. Damage releases more antigen for further immune stimulation.

Extra-Articular FindingAntigen-antibody complexes can also form under the skin (not just in joints), producing a rheumatoid rash — common in RA patients.

03 · Ankylosing Spondylitis & Psoriatic Arthritis

Ankylosing Spondylitis

A CD8/CTL-predominant spondyloarthropathy with the strongest HLA association in this lecture.

  • Primarily T-cell caused; mostly affects the spine, and (as in RA) bones can fuse.
  • Strong association with HLA-B27 (class I MHC) — more prevalent in Caucasians, tracking with AS prevalence in that population.
  • Main pathogenic mediator: CD8+ T cells (CTL). Th17, NK cells, and γδ T cells also contribute to a lesser degree.
Logic ChainHLA-B27 is class I → predicts CD8+/CTL-predominant pathogenesis, distinguishing AS from the more CD4-driven RA. CD4 cells are still needed to support robust CD8 responses.

Psoriatic Arthritis

  • Occurs in a subset of psoriasis patients.
  • Mediated by CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, as well as B cells/antibodies — a multi-mediator disease, unlike the single-mechanism dominance in AS.

04 · Polymyalgia Rheumatica & Giant Cell Arteritis

Polymyalgia Rheumatica (PMR)

CD4+ T-cell–mediated regional muscle pain, classically in older women.

  • Results in regional muscle pain; most common in older women.
  • Th1 and Th17 cells cause muscle inflammation → pain. Increased vascularization also occurs and likely exacerbates the process.
  • As with many inflammatory diseases, CRP is elevated — IL-6 signals the liver to produce this acute-phase protein, so circulating CRP indicates sufficient systemic inflammation.
AssociationGiant cell arteritis (GCA) frequently co-occurs with PMR. Look for thickened temporal arteries, plus headache, tenderness, visual impairment, weight loss, and night sweats.

05 · Polymyositis

Polymyositis

CD8+/CTL-mediated destruction of myocytes, driven by aberrant MHC class I expression.

  • Main symptom: muscle weakness (not primarily pain) — basic tasks like lifting the arms, combing hair, or climbing stairs become impaired.
  • Symptoms last weeks to months. Skin is not involved (that distinction defines dermatomyositis instead).
  • A variety of muscle groups can be affected, usually symmetrically.

Pathogenesis

Skeletal muscle normally expresses very little MHC class I. In polymyositis, myocytes abnormally upregulate class I MHC, allowing CTL to recognize self antigens on the cell surface and kill the myocyte. CTL activation also produces IFN-γ, which maintains the high MHC expression — a self-sustaining cycle of destruction and regeneration that produces progressive weakness.

Antisynthetase SyndromeA subset with anti-Jo-1 antibodies presents with the triad of myositis + interstitial lung disease + arthritis. Antibodies can be present even though CD8+ T cells are the primary driver — this defines disease subsets.

06 · Autoimmune Myositis (Antibody-Mediated)

Autoimmune Myositis — Overview

Not a single disease — a category of muscle-inflammation disorders, split into inflammatory and necrotizing forms.

  • Two main categories: inflammatory (includes polymyositis, dermatomyositis) and necrotizing.
  • Creatine kinase (CK) is elevated in both, but higher in necrotizing forms (more cell death).
  • Common symptoms: muscle weakness and pain.
  • Cell death releases nuclear contents → can stimulate production of ANA.
  • Statin use increases the risk of some forms of autoimmune myositis.

Dermatomyositis vs. Other Forms

Dermatomyositis affects both skin and muscle (unlike polymyositis). Polymyositis and inclusion-body myositis are differentiated from each other by histological examination.

Antibodies in Myositis

A wide range of antibodies occur across the myositis spectrum:

  • Myositis-specific antibodies — found only in myositis; diagnostic.
  • Myositis-associated antibodies — also seen in other diseases; not diagnostic alone.
PearlAntibody presence may be a mediator of disease, a result of disease, or both — don't assume every associated antibody is causal.

Autoimmune vs. Infectious Myositis

Myositis can also be caused by infection, not just autoimmunity. Key differential clue: infectious myositis is usually asymmetric, while autoimmune myositis is usually symmetric.

07 · Sjögren's Syndrome & Psoriasis

Sjögren's Syndrome

CD4+ T-cell–mediated exocrine gland destruction — "dryness" is the unifying theme.

  • Actually an autoimmunity of exocrine glands, but effects extend to the skin; muscle pain can also occur.
  • Inhibited secretions → dry eyes, dry nose, dry skin, dry mouth/throat, dry vagina; digestion can also be affected.
  • Caused by Th1 and Th17 cells damaging epithelial cells of the exocrine system.
Pattern RecognitionChronic inflammation is perpetuated by ongoing damage in the same amplification cycle seen with other CD4+-driven autoimmunities — same cell types (Th1/Th17), different target tissue/antigen.

Psoriasis

Th17-driven epithelial hyperproliferation, with a notable type I interferon component.

  • A group of autoimmunities affecting the skin; rashes can be extensive and severe.
  • Main pathogenic agent: Th17 cells. The Th17 response to autoantigens causes hyperproliferation of epithelial cells.

Koebnerization & Type I Interferon

In psoriasis patients, physical trauma to unaffected skin can initiate psoriasis symptoms there — a phenomenon called koebnerization. There is also potential involvement of IFN-α/β: plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs / interferon-producing cells) make large amounts of type I interferon. Though best known for antiviral defense, these cytokines here stimulate classical dendritic cells (→ more T-cell activation) and alter cell-surface molecule expression (→ increased sensitivity to NK killing and cytokine signaling).

08 · Vitiligo, Lichen Planus & Alopecia Areata

Vitiligo

CTL-mediated melanocyte destruction — the most "purely CTL" disease in this lecture.

  • Characterized by lack of pigment in patches of skin.
  • CTL kill melanocytes, destroying the source of pigment. Regeneration of melanocytes can occur if treatment blocks the killing.

Predisposing Genetic Factors

FactorEffect
MHC allele associationIncreased susceptibility (as with many autoimmunities)
CTLA-4 deficiencyIncreased T-cell activity (loss of inhibitory checkpoint)
IL-4 deficiencyMore robust Th1 cytokine levels and CTL activation
IL-10 deficiencyLimits immune regulation
Beyond CTLIn addition to the primary CTL response, Th17 cells and antibodies may also contribute to melanocyte damage.

Lichen Planus

CTL targeting basement-membrane keratinocytes — can involve skin, mucosa, nails, and hair.

  • Primarily CTL-mediated; can affect skin, mucosal epithelium, nails, and hair.
  • CTL target the keratinocytes of the basement membrane; their killing leads to visible symptoms and — like other autoimmunities here — sets up a cycle of antigen release and further stimulation.
  • CTL responses require helper T cells, which in turn require dendritic cell antigen presentation (Langerhans cells are the resident dendritic cells of skin).
  • Keratinocyte destruction leads to separation of skin layers.

Alopecia Areata

CTL destruction of hair follicles — with both antigen-dependent and antigen-independent killing.

  • Symptom: extensive (often patchy) hair loss from CTL destruction of hair follicles.

Pathogenesis Sequence

Dendritic cells pick up antigen from the hair follicle, but must first recognize a danger signal (e.g., a local infection, or cell contents released by extrinsic damage). The dendritic cell migrates to a lymph node (or equivalent tissue) to activate both CTL and helper T cells via antigen presentation. Activated CTL then migrate to the follicle and destroy antigen-bearing cells.

Antigen-Independent KillingCTL and NK cells can also kill via positive signal ligands on target cells, independent of specific antigen recognition (a phenomenon also seen in celiac disease) — antigen-dependent and antigen-independent killing can occur together in the same lesion.

09 · Bullous Pemphigoid & Pemphigus Vulgaris

Bullous Pemphigoid

A rare multi-isotype antibody disease (IgG1, IgG4, and even IgE) plus Th17 — subepidermal blistering.

  • Distinctive rash, often with blistering. Both T cells and antibodies contribute.
  • Blistering results from damage to underlying tissues.

Three Antibody Isotypes + Th17

MediatorMechanism
IgG4Does not fix complement, but interferes with cell/matrix function (binds collagen, promotes phagocytic internalization/depletion of collagen)
IgG1Activates complement → inflammation, lysis, phagocyte activation
IgE (rare in autoimmunity)Causes mast cell degranulation, signaling inflammation
Th17 + eosinophilsRecruited/activated; initiate the blood clotting cascade, further exacerbating inflammation and damage
AntigenThe primary autoantigen is collagen (BP180, type XVII collagen). IgG4 and IgG1 antibodies against it cause internalization/depletion of collagen, weakening the skin. This is one of the few autoimmune diseases with a partial type I hypersensitivity (IgE) component.

Pemphigus Vulgaris

Type II hypersensitivity — antibodies against desmoglein cause intraepidermal blistering.

  • Type II hypersensitivity form of autoimmunity — antigens are cell-surface. "Pemphigus" = autoimmunities causing skin blisters; "vulgaris" = common, i.e., the most common form of pemphigus.
  • Caused by autoantibodies specific for desmoglein, the adhesion molecule in desmosomes that holds keratinocytes together.
  • Epitope recognition progresses over time. Antibodies inhibit desmoglein adhesion, causing the outer skin/mucosal layers to separate → blisters.
  • Both skin and mucous membranes can be affected; the disease can be life-threatening.
Location of Split (High-Yield)Pemphigus vulgaris loosens cell-cell junctions within the epidermis (intraepidermal, desmoglein). Bullous pemphigoid loosens the junction between epidermis and basement membrane (subepidermal, hemidesmosome/collagen XVII). Pemphigoid blisters are typically tenser/less fragile than pemphigus blisters, given the deeper split.

10 · Scleroderma & Epidermolysis Bullosa Acquisita

Scleroderma

Antibody-mediated damage to endothelial cells/fibroblasts, plus stimulated collagen production, producing a leathery skin texture.

  • Hardening of the skin → leathery texture.
  • Contributing factors: calcium deposition in affected skin; amplified cold/stress response with digits turning blue or white; acid reflux is a commonly associated symptom (esophagus affected); fingers/toes tighten and thicken, with more visible blood vessels.

Pathogenesis

Various antigens can be targeted — the specific pattern of antigens helps determine which form of scleroderma is present. Antigens are mostly intracellular, with a few on endothelial cells and fibroblasts (contributing to vascular/connective-tissue dysfunction — type II hypersensitivity). Cell damage releases contents that form antigen-antibody complexes, causing inflammation and more damage (type III hypersensitivity). The process also stimulates collagen production, contributing to skin hardening.

Mast CellsChronically degranulated mast cells (visible on stained sections as cells present but empty of granules) suggest ongoing stimulation to release granules as fast as they form — mast cell contents likely also contribute to pathogenicity.

Epidermolysis Bullosa Acquisita

Anti-collagen antibody disease that mimics bullous pemphigoid's location, but with a different antigen.

  • Can appear at various skin/mucosal locations.
  • Like pemphigoid, there is separation of the epidermis from the basement membrane.
  • The autoantigen is collagen (type VII). Antibody binding to collagen activates complement → inflammation → junction damage → blistering.
Distinguish from Bullous PemphigoidBoth target collagen and cause subepidermal splitting, but EBA targets type VII collagen (anchoring fibrils) vs. bullous pemphigoid's type XVII collagen/BP180 (hemidesmosome). Complement-fixing antibody (implying IgG1, not the IgG4/IgE profile of pemphigoid) drives EBA's inflammatory damage.

11 · Secondary Autoimmune Effects

Dermatitis Herpetiformis

A side-effect antibody response in celiac disease that happens to cross-react with skin transglutaminase.

  • A potential side effect of celiac disease, producing a spread-out rash.
  • Celiac disease is primarily T-cell mediated, but antibody (usually IgA) against transglutaminase can also form.
  • Anti-transglutaminase IgA binds transglutaminase in the skin (not just the gut) and activates complement via the lectin pathway, interacting with neutrophils (which have Fc-α receptors) to cause inflammation.

Multiple Sclerosis & Guillain-Barré Syndrome

  • Primarily neurologic autoimmunities (damage to the myelin sheath), but their manifestation is musculoskeletal — impaired nerve signaling means impaired muscle contraction.

Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)

  • Involves antigen-antibody complexes with broad, systemic effects.
  • MSK-relevant effects: joint pain from immune-complex deposition/inflammation, and — as with other autoimmunities — osteoclast activation can lead to osteoporosis/bone damage. Also produces rash.

12 · Therapeutic Targets & Board Review

Therapeutic Targets in Autoimmunity

Detailed drug pharmacology is covered elsewhere — this is the conceptual map of "what could be blocked."

  • Co-stimulation blockade: naïve (and possibly memory) T-cell activation requires CD28 binding B7. Introducing CTLA-4 (which also binds B7) competitively blocks CD28 engagement, preventing full activation.
  • Cytokine/receptor blockade: blocking IL-2 binding its receptor halts proliferation of CTL, Th1, and Th17 cells (all require IL-2 for division). Blocking receptors for IL-17, IFN-γ, or other inflammatory cytokines inhibits their specific downstream effects.
  • Shared subunit blockade: IL-12 and IL-23 share the p40 subunit. Blocking p40 restricts production of both cytokines, interrupting the positive feedback loop between T cells/NK cells and macrophages.
  • Adhesion molecule blockade: effector cells require cell-cell binding to function. LFA-1 is necessary both for extravasation and for CTL killing — blocking it can prevent both steps.
  • IL-1 blockade: blocking IL-1 binding its receptor stops IL-1's inflammatory signaling.
Big PictureEvery target category carries the same tradeoff: blocking a pathway needed for autoimmune damage also blunts immunity where it's actually needed.
