00. Overview & Objectives

Understand the differences between OCS I/II and OCS III/IV standardized patient (SP) encounters, including:

  • Problem-focused decision-making and documentation
  • Differential diagnosis development, including documentation of rationale and supporting evidence
  • Verbal presentations

01. What's Different in Year 2?

There are three primary differences between OCS I/II and OCS III/IV standardized patient encounters:

  1. Problem-focused encounters: You'll switch from a screening exam with a checklist of physical exam elements you demonstrate, to a more realistic clinical encounter — you decide which history and physical exam elements to apply based on the patient's chief complaint. This doesn't mean there's no right or wrong answer: specific historical and physical exam elements are naturally tied to each case, and those are what you'll be graded on.
  2. Differential diagnosis development gets top priority during these encounters and their associated notes. As you develop your differential, plan to write out your rationale or supporting evidence for each diagnosis, drawn from your history and physical exam findings.
  3. Verbal presentations are new this year. You will present the case to your standardized patient as though they were your attending physician.

03. Focused History: What to Prioritize

These are more focused encounters — you'll still use all of the historical elements you've been taught, just with a different focus in mind.

  • The chief complaint, HPI elements, and review of systems are the elements that most directly inform your differential diagnosis — these are your key priorities
  • Past medical/surgical/social history are still important, but can be asked in a more abbreviated, consolidated way
Example of an efficient, consolidated question: Instead of asking about each family member and condition individually, you might ask: "As you consider your family members and extended family, is there any heart disease that runs in the family, or any conditions that run in your family?"

04. HPI Checklist

  • Onset
  • Location
  • Timing / duration / frequency (constant vs. intermittent — if intermittent, how often and for how long?)
  • Temporal factors (what else happened around onset? the setting connected to the onset)
  • Character
  • Severity (scale of 1–10, or mild/moderate/severe)
  • Aggravating / alleviating factors
  • Radiation, if applicable
  • Associated symptoms
The review of systems can follow right after the HPI. Include both a brief general ROS and targeted questions based specifically on your working differential diagnosis.

05. PMH, Medications, Allergies & Social History

Past Medical History — all of:

  • Chronic illness
  • Hospitalizations
  • Surgeries
  • Major injury

Medications — all of:

  • Prescription (with sig)
  • Over-the-counter
  • Supplements

Allergies — all of:

  • Medication (with reaction)
  • Environmental (with reaction)
  • Food (with reaction)

Social History

  • Occupation
  • Living situation
  • 4 substances (this typically refers to tobacco, alcohol, illicit drugs, and caffeine — confirm the specific set expected in your course)
  • Diet / exercise / sleep
  • Sexual history

06. Family History: Efficient Questioning

A useful consolidated prompt: "Think about your close family members. Are there any major medical issues with anyone?" Record what the patient tells you as they tell you, rather than asking about each condition and each relative one at a time.

07. Focused Physical Exam Construction

The physical exam you perform is chosen by you, based on your history findings — but it should follow a consistent construction:

  1. Vitals (documented in the note)
  2. General assessment — a brief statement of how the patient looks to you (documented in the note)
  3. Primary system — a full, general (screening) exam of the body system most relevant to the chief complaint
  4. Other exam items, added as appropriate:
    • Special test skill(s) to further examine any abnormal findings from the general (screening) exam
    • Additional system item skill(s) addressing associated symptoms not covered in the primary system's screening exam — expect to need this in every case
    • Targeted skill(s) that specifically address a diagnosis on your differential
Use your history, your differential diagnosis, and your general (screening) exam results together to determine exactly what additional exam items to include.

08. OMM: Somatic Dysfunction Evaluation

  • Evaluate for somatic dysfunction (SD) as a diagnosis — not simply AGR or TART findings in isolation
  • Feel free to explain to your patient what you are doing and why during this portion of the exam
  • You do not need to treat and recheck your lesion during the encounter

09. End-of-Encounter Summary to the Patient

Before you leave the room, once your exam is complete, briefly discuss with the patient what you believe may be causing their symptoms and how you plan to initially address it. Keep this brief — broad ideas are appropriate here, since the fuller reasoning goes into your differential diagnosis and plan.

10. Assessment: Structuring Your Differential

  • Primary problem (or chief complaint)
  • DDx #1, #2, #3 — each with its own support
What counts as "support": At least one unique item (a specific piece of evidence) found in the case that explains why you think that diagnosis is a good fit. Support items cannot simply be the primary problem/chief complaint itself, but they can be qualifications of the chief complaint. Example: for a chief complaint of abdominal pain, "pain located in the RLQ" can serve as support for appendicitis, since that detail differentiates it from other items on your differential.
  • Your working (provisional) diagnosis is the one you believe is most likely to be the actual diagnosis
  • Somatic Dysfunction (SD) diagnosis — named by region, based on findings listed in your objective/exam section

11. Assessment: Problem List & SDOH

  • Other Problem List: Problems not directly associated with the primary problem — for example, a known PMH issue, abnormal vital signs, or a significant risk factor uncovered in the history (like a family history of diabetes)
  • Social Determinants of Health (SDOH): Identify and include these in your assessment, under the problem list
Don't forget: An identified SDOH needs to be addressed somewhere in your plan as well — it shouldn't just sit in the assessment without a corresponding management idea.

12. Plan: The Five Categories

Address all five categories below as they pertain to the chief complaint and your working diagnosis. Every item in your plan should tie back to something identified in your assessment — especially your differential diagnosis items — showing how you intend to further refine your differential or address other identified problems.

1. Testing

  • Be specific — "an ultrasound" isn't enough; specify which ultrasound, which X-ray views, or what kind of CT scan
  • Labs should also specifically target items on your differential — look up the details needed to be precise
  • If you genuinely don't want to order testing because you consider this a clinical diagnosis, simply communicate that
  • Conditional statements are acceptable — e.g., "not planning on labs or imaging now, but will consider adding this if ___"

2. Treatment

  • Prescriptions should include dose, route, and frequency
  • OTC medications should be specified with similar detail
  • Also consider: immobilization, PT/OT, activity modifications, or anything else reasonable given your assessment

3. OMT (Chosen Treatment)

Deferring OMT is acceptable, if appropriate — just communicate that decision.

4. Patient Education

Aim for specifics rather than generic statements like "medication side effects." If you prescribed a medication, look it up and document the relevant, specific education points in your note.

5. Specific Follow-Up

  • Be specific about when and how long — avoid vague terms like "PRN," "if worse," "not better," or "after discharge/testing"
  • Conditional statements are fine as long as there's a definitive timeframe attached — e.g., "Follow-up in 2 weeks. Sooner if worsening, or to the ER if [specific red flags]." This gives a firm 2-week mark with clear conditions for earlier action.
Amoxicillin example of specificity: Don't just say "give an antibiotic" — say "Amoxicillin 875 mg PO BID x 7 days." As a second-year student, you're expected to specify the exact medication, dose, interval, and duration.

13. Verbal Presentation: Structure & Rubric

  • After your encounter, you'll have 5 minutes to prepare your thoughts before presenting
  • You will not have time to include everything from the encounter — highlight the important information
  • Re-enter the exam room and present the case to the SP, who is now acting as your attending physician

Verbal Presentation Grading Rubric (graded by the SP)

#Element
1Start with the age and sex of the patient
2The history section contains only history — not exam findings
3Begin the exam section with vitals
4The exam section contains only exam items — not history
5Discussed what you thought was going on, and why
6Discussed an initial management plan
7Organized presentation, not too difficult to follow
8Completed in 5 minutes or less
This is formative: You're expected to make mistakes as you learn and progress with this skill throughout the year. Meet with faculty or fellows if you're struggling.

14. Additional Documentation Tips

  • Look for efficient ways to acquire history items — the family history example (Section 06) applies to other history elements too
  • Identify and include your Social Determinants of Health in both your history and documentation — park these in your assessment, under the problem list, and make sure they tie into something found in the history and physical
  • Strive for greater specificity throughout your notes — don't just say you're giving "an antibiotic"; say which one, what dose, what interval, and for how long
Learning from feedback: Anything you learn during the visit, from SP feedback, or during the debrief can be used in your note as though you had actually performed it. Documenting a complete note with the key points and skills you should have obtained reinforces your ability to do so next time.

Be on time: Students who arrive after the pre-brief/debrief has started will not be permitted to participate — this counts as an unexcused absence. These events run on a tight schedule, so be proactive and ready to go at your assigned time.
A quiet, staffed documentation space is available in the SIM center for students who want to complete their SOAP note right after their encounter. Remember, this is still an individual exercise — do your own work.
