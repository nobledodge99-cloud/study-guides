Pt. 1 — Arthropathies

Joint & Soft Tissue Pathology — Dr. Kemp
SECTION 1

Arthritis: Overview

Arthritis means inflammation of a joint. Dr. Kemp frames the whole first lecture around five general forms of arthritis.

  • Osteoarthritis — degenerative, wear-and-tear
  • Rheumatoid arthritis / juvenile RA — autoimmune
  • Seronegative spondyloarthropathies — HLA-B27–associated group, no rheumatoid factor
  • Infectious arthritis — suppurative (bacterial) or Lyme-related
  • Crystal-induced arthritis — gout (urate) and pseudogout (calcium pyrophosphate)
BIG-PICTURE FRAMING: Osteoarthritis is a mechanical/degenerative disease of weight-bearing joints. Rheumatoid arthritis is an autoimmune disease of small joints. That single contrast resolves most of the "which one is it" questions in this material.

SECTION 2

Osteoarthritis

General Features

Also called degenerative joint disease (DJD) — a disease of the joints from degeneration of cartilage with subsequent ineffective repair.

  • Demographics: older patients, 50s–70s (primary OA, age-related); younger patients, 20s–40s (secondary OA — joint deformity, trauma, systemic disease)

Pathogenesis

Chondrocytes secrete the extracellular matrix that forms cartilage, but degeneration of the cartilage can exceed production. Chondrocytes also secrete matrix metalloproteinases, which degrade type II collagen and damage the cartilage. Low-level chronic inflammation contributes too — even ordinary age-related wear elicits some inflammatory response to injury.

Morphology

  • Gross: granular cartilage surface from fissures/clefts (cartilage is normally smooth); loose cartilage fragments (joint mice); polished, shiny exposed bone once cartilage is worn away (eburnation); osteophytes at the joint margins; classically no joint fusion — see the pearl below
  • Microscopic: fissures/clefts in the cartilage surface (cartilage also looks pale rather than the normal bluish hyaline tint); cysts from synovial fluid pressed into the clefts
  • Radiology: narrowing of the joint space from cartilage loss — can progress to bone-on-bone
FROM LECTURE: Dr. Kemp described seeing a bone specimen with severe eburnation during his PhD work at the University of Montana — the exposed bone was "absolutely shiny," polished from years of bone-on-bone wear.
POINT TO PONDER: OA doesn't fuse the joint itself the way RA does — there's no synovial inflammation or fibrosis in OA. But osteophytes growing out from adjacent vertebral bodies can connect and fuse to each other, producing what looks like joint fusion without the joint proper ever being involved. So "OA never causes fusion" isn't quite complete.

Symptoms

  • Typically presents in the 50s; pain characteristically worsens with use across the day and rests overnight (opposite pattern from RA)
  • Morning stiffness, crepitus, limited range of motion (from pain and from osteophytes mechanically limiting movement); osteophytes can impinge nerves → radicular pain
  • Joints involved: weight-bearing joints — hips, knees, lower lumbar vertebrae — plus the PIP, DIP, and 1st carpometacarpal joints (wear-and-tear isn't only from big joints; repetitive use of the hands, e.g. typing, counts too)
  • Heberden nodes — osteophytes at the DIP joints; Bouchard nodes — osteophytes at the PIP joints

SECTION 3

Rheumatoid Arthritis

General Features

Autoimmune disease of the joints producing a non-suppurative, proliferative, and inflammatory synovitis. The inflammation damages articular cartilage and can cause fusion (ankylosis) of the joints — think of it as fibrosis spanning the joint space, like scarring across it.

  • ~1% of the population; F > M (as with most autoimmune disorders); presents in the 3rd–5th decades of life

Pathogenesis

Driven by TH1 and TH17 T cells and antibodies:

  • TH1 cells secrete IFN-γ → activates macrophages and synovial cells
  • TH17 cells secrete IL-17 → activates neutrophils and monocytes
  • TNF and IL-1 from macrophages stimulate synovial cells to secrete proteases → tissue damage
  • Plasma cells associated with the inflammation produce antibodies
  • RANKL stimulates osteoclasts → bone breakdown

Laboratory Diagnosis

  • Autoantibodies for citrullinated peptides (citrullinated fibrinogen along with type II collagen, alpha-enolase, and vimentin deposit in the joint) — in ~70% of patients
  • Rheumatoid factor — serum IgM or IgA autoantibodies against the Fc portion of IgG — in ~80% (not specific, though)
  • Radiography: narrowing of the joint space and loss of articular cartilage (both OA and RA can show joint-space narrowing — the underlying pathology is very different)

Risk Factors

  • Vinculin — an epitope on the citrullinated protein — mimics an epitope on microbes; if presented by HLA-DQ alleles, this promotes antibody formation against citrullinated peptides
  • PTPN22 — a tyrosine phosphatase that normally inhibits T-cell activation
  • Infection and smoking may drive citrullination

Morphology

  • Pannus: edematous, thickened, hyperplastic synovium with inflammatory cell infiltrate, fibroblasts producing collagen/fibrosis, and granulation tissue as an intermediate stage — this is what lays down across the joint space and lets the two sides fuse
  • Microscopic: synovial hyperplasia and dense inflammatory infiltrates; angiogenesis, fibrin, fibrosis, inflammatory cells
  • Rheumatoid nodules: occur at the forearm, elbows, occiput, and lumbosacral region; look like necrotizing granulomas — central area of necrosis rimmed by giant cells
FROM LECTURE: Because a rheumatoid nodule looks like a necrotizing granuloma on histology, Dr. Kemp noted that in practice you'd stain it for acid-fast bacilli and do a silver stain for fungus to rule out an infectious cause before calling it a rheumatoid nodule.

Symptoms

  • Can start with systemic symptoms — fatigue, malaise, musculoskeletal pain — before progressing to localized joint pain
  • Symmetric, small-joint predominant: hands, feet, wrists, ankles, elbows, and knees; metacarpophalangeal and proximal interphalangeal joints especially — tendons, ligaments, and muscles can also be involved, producing ulnar deviation of the fingers and swan-neck or boutonniere deformities
  • Swollen, warm, painful joints (consistent with inflammation)
  • Morning stiffness that improves with movement (opposite of OA's "worse with use" pattern)
  • Waxing and waning course
OA AND RA CAN COEXIST: Slides describe a patient with both osteoarthritis (PIP/hip involvement) and rheumatoid arthritis (metacarpal involvement) at the same time — the two are not mutually exclusive, and chronic RA-related joint stress may even predispose to secondary OA changes.

SECTION 5

Seronegative Spondyloarthropathies

General Features

  • Absence of rheumatoid factor — hence "seronegative"
  • Pathologic changes are in the ligaments, not the synovium (unlike RA)
  • Involve the sacroiliac joints
  • Associated with HLA-B27
  • Bony proliferation leads to ankylosis (joint fusion) — hence "ankylosing" spondylitis

Ankylosing Spondylitis

The prototype/classic form — the one to know if you only know one. Affects the sacroiliac joints, but also peripheral joints: hips, knees, and shoulders.

Reactive Arthritis

Classic triad: arthritis, nongonococcal urethritis or cervicitis, and conjunctivitis.

  • M > F — unusual for an autoimmune-type reaction, but this is the pattern seen; typically 20s–30s; about 80% HLA-B27 positive
  • Etiology: an autoimmune reaction to a preceding infection — Chlamydia, Shigella, Salmonella, Yersinia, or Campylobacter
  • Affects the sacroiliac joint (as with ankylosing spondylitis), plus knees, ankles, and feet

SECTION 6

Infectious Arthritis

Mechanisms of Formation

  • Hematogenous dissemination
  • Direct inoculation (e.g., trauma penetrating the joint)
  • Contiguous spread from soft tissue or from osteomyelitis
NOTE: Joint infection (arthritis) and bone infection (osteomyelitis) are distinct entities — a joint infection doesn't necessarily mean the bone is involved, and vice versa, even though one can spread contiguously into the other.

Suppurative (Bacterial) Arthritis — Organisms by Age

  • H. influenzae — children <2 years of age, from epiphyseal osteomyelitis
  • Staphylococcus aureus — older children and adults (the same organism that's the most common cause of osteomyelitis in this age group)
  • Gonococcal — late adolescence/early adulthood (risk factors: sexually active; deficiencies of complement C5–C7 and C9)
  • Salmonella — patients with sickle cell disease (the same pairing seen with osteomyelitis)

Symptoms: painful, warm, swollen joint (features of acute inflammation) — usually involves only one joint (though hematogenous spread could occasionally involve more than one).

Lyme Arthritis

  • Early localized: skin lesion
  • Early disseminated: skin, cranial nerve involvement, heart, meninges (a viral-type meningitis); arthritis, especially of the knee — this arthritis is migratory, moving from joint to joint, and lasts weeks to months
  • Late disseminated: chronic arthritis, plus nonspecific symptoms — fatigue, cognitive complaints (which can be quite challenging for patients)

SECTION 7

Gout

Basic Features

Arthritis due to deposition of uric acid (urate) crystals.

Mechanism of Hyperuricemia

  • Under-excretion (main mechanism in primary gout): urate is normally filtered by the glomerulus and reabsorbed by the proximal convoluted tubule, with only a small amount excreted — if too little is excreted, urate accumulates
  • Overproduction (less common mechanism): from synthesis of purine nucleotides, with uric acid as an end product; also seen with partial or complete deficiency of HGPRT (hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase), which interrupts the purine salvage pathway
  • Secondary gout: tumor lysis in leukemias/lymphomas — high cell turnover means a lot of DNA/purine breakdown, producing a lot of uric acid

Pathogenesis

Hyperuricemia (plasma urate > 6.8 mg/dL) → urate deposits trigger inflammation. Macrophages and neutrophils engulf the crystals and activate the inflammasome; crystals can also directly damage lysosomes, causing leakage of their contents and further joint damage. Repeated attacks lead to tophi — aggregates of crystals surrounded by inflammatory tissue.

IMPORTANT POINT: Only about 10% of people with hyperuricemia actually develop gout — hyperuricemia alone is not sufficient. Age, genetic predisposition, alcohol consumption, obesity, and certain drugs also contribute.

Morphology

  • Acute arthritis: urate crystals — needle-shaped and negatively birefringent — plus an inflammatory infiltrate
  • Chronic tophaceous arthritis: chalky deposits on the synovium; hyperplastic, fibrotic, inflamed synovium (a pannus-like response, similar in concept to RA)
  • Tophi: uric acid crystals surrounded by a foreign body giant cell reaction
  • Gout can also affect the kidney: uric acid is one of the types of kidney stones, and gout can also lead to pyelonephritis
FROM LECTURE: Dr. Kemp described sectioning into a bump on a patient's toe expecting an abscess ("my first thought was actually pus") — it turned out to be a gouty tophus instead.

Symptoms

  • More common in males; typically presents after age 30
  • Sudden-onset joint pain associated with hyperemia and warmth; usually monoarticular, and about 50% involve the first metatarsophalangeal joint (the great toe)
  • If untreated, attacks recur and the disease becomes polyarticular
  • Chronic tophaceous gout develops about 12 years after the first acute attack

SECTION 8

Pseudogout (CPPD)

Calcium pyrophosphate crystal deposition disease — the second major crystal arthropathy.

General Features

  • >50 years of age; M = F (unlike gout, which favors males)
  • Forms: sporadic; hereditary (autosomal dominant mutation in a pyrophosphate transport channel); secondary (joint damage, hyperparathyroidism, hemochromatosis, diabetes mellitus)
  • Pathogenesis: basically similar to gout — activation of the inflammasome
DEMOGRAPHICS ARE A GUIDE, NOT A RULE: Dr. Kemp emphasized that demographic patterns (e.g., roughly equal M:F in pseudogout) are useful guides, but every patient is an individual — a classic presentation in an atypical demographic doesn't rule out the disease.

Morphology

  • Crystals develop near/in the articular cartilage, menisci, and intervertebral discs; they can rupture and seed the joint space
  • Chalky white and friable deposits
  • Crystals are rhomboid and positively birefringent (contrast with gout's negative birefringence)

Symptoms

Often asymptomatic, but can produce an acute, subacute, or chronic arthritis lasting days to weeks; can be monoarticular or polyarticular. Affects the knees, wrists, elbows, shoulders, and ankles — peripheral joints, not the major weight-bearing hips.

SECTION 9

Ganglion & Synovial Cysts; Tenosynovial Giant Cell Tumor

Ganglion Cyst

  • Location: commonly around the wrist
  • Firm, fluctuant, pea-sized, translucent nodule
  • Mechanism: cystic or myxoid degeneration of connective tissue — there is no true cell lining, so it's technically a pseudocyst even though it's called a "cyst"
FROM LECTURE: Ganglion cysts used to be called "Bible cysts" because an old-school treatment was to smash the wrist with a large book (a Bible) to rupture the cyst and let the body resolve it — Dr. Kemp noted the old treatment may have worked about as well as some newer ones.

Synovial Cyst

  • Mechanism: herniation of the synovium through the joint capsule, or enlargement of a bursa
  • Classic example: Baker cyst, in the popliteal space

Tenosynovial Giant Cell Tumor

Two forms — localized and diffuse. The diffuse form is called pigmented villonodular synovitis because it's inflammation of the synovium that appears dark/pigmented and clustered, like a bunch of grapes.

  • Location: localized → hand; diffuse → large joints
  • Molecular: t(1;2) — fuses the type VI collagen α-3 promoter to the M-CSF (colony stimulating factor for macrophages) gene, driving macrophage proliferation
  • Morphology: localized = a nodule attached to a tendon sheath; diffuse = red-brown, folded, fingerlike projections/nodules of synovium
  • Abundant macrophages, some containing hemosiderin and lipid (foamy) from associated blood breakdown products; macrophages can fuse into multinucleated giant cells
FROM LECTURE: Dr. Kemp recalled a case from residency — a "giant cell tumor of tendon sheath" from the hand — where a colleague identified the diagnosis purely from knowing it came from the hand, without even needing to look through the microscope.

SECTION 10

Lipoma & Liposarcoma

Soft Tissue Tumors — General

Except for skeletal-muscle-derived neoplasms (usually malignant — e.g., rhabdomyosarcoma), benign soft tissue tumors outnumber malignant ones roughly 100:1. Even so, soft tissue tumors cause a disproportionate share of deaths: less than 1% of cancers, but about 2% of all cancer deaths. Most are sporadic; some occur as part of a syndrome (NF1, Gardner syndrome, Li-Fraumeni), and some are secondary to radiation, burns, or toxins.

Lipoma

Benign tumor of fat. Grossly, a well-encapsulated mass of mature adipose tissue, most often subcutaneous — trunk or extremities. Extremely common; on surgical pathology these are a routine, high-volume specimen.

CLINICAL PEARL: Subcutaneous lipomas are common and behave benignly. But when a fatty mass arises inside a body cavity (retroperitoneum, mesentery, chest wall, etc.), it is rarely a true lipoma — it should be treated as at least an atypical lipoma, if not a well-differentiated liposarcoma.
FROM LECTURE: Dr. Kemp described a case with a roughly 2–3 kg fatty mesenteric mass that grossly resembled adipose tissue but had areas of fibrosis and necrosis — sectioning revealed it was a well-differentiated liposarcoma, illustrating why location changes the default assumption.

Liposarcoma — the Malignant Fat Tumor

  • Well-differentiated: adipocytes with scattered atypical spindle cells in the interstitium
  • Myxoid liposarcoma: basophilic extracellular matrix, branching capillaries, and primitive adipocytes that resemble fetal fat
  • Dedifferentiated liposarcoma: a low-grade (well-differentiated) component admixed with a higher-grade, spindle-cell component
  • Pleomorphic liposarcoma: anaplastic cells, bizarre nuclei, mitotic figures — the most aggressive variant

Sarcomas characteristically spread hematogenously rather than via lymphatics (unlike carcinomas, which spread via lymph nodes) — so a patient can have widespread metastatic liposarcoma (e.g., to the lungs) with negative lymph nodes.

Molecular Associations

  • Well-differentiated liposarcoma: amplification of 12q13–q15 (the MDM2 gene), which normally inhibits p53
  • Myxoid liposarcoma: t(12;16), which arrests adipose tissue differentiation (this is why the tumor resembles primitive/fetal fat rather than mature adipocytes)
  • Pleomorphic liposarcoma: complex, nonspecific karyotypes

SECTION 11

Nodular Fasciitis

A proliferation of fibroblasts and myofibroblasts that is benign but can easily be mistaken for a sarcoma — it has historically been misdiagnosed as a malignant neoplasm, and there's some debate over whether it's truly a neoplasm at all versus a pronounced reactive process.

  • Demographics: young adults
  • 1/4 to 1/2 of cases have a preceding history of trauma, with rapid growth over the following weeks to months — consistent with an exuberant reactive process
  • Molecular: t(17;22) — clonal, but self-limited (so despite being clonal — which leans toward "neoplasm" — it behaves more like a reactive process)

Morphology

  • Gross: usually <5 cm, well circumscribed
  • Microscopic: very cellular, resembling cultured fibroblasts; shows a gradient from cellular/loose/myxoid to fibrous
  • Can show a degree of pleomorphism and prominent mitotic figures — this is exactly what makes it mimic a sarcoma histologically; margins can also appear infiltrative
  • Location: often upper extremities
WHY IT MATTERS: Nodular fasciitis has a real history of being misdiagnosed as a malignant sarcoma because of its cellularity, mitotic activity, and sometimes infiltrative-appearing margins. Recognizing the clinical context — young adult, rapid growth after trauma, small size, upper extremity — helps avoid that trap.

SECTION 12

Fibromatosis

Fibromatoses are masses formed essentially by fibrous tissue — think of them as an overgrowth of scar-like tissue. There are two forms: superficial and deep.

Superficial Fibromatosis

  • Examples: palmar fibromatosis (causes Dupuytren contractures) and penile fibromatosis (Peyronie disease)
  • An ill-defined mass caused essentially by scarring
  • M > F — likely reflecting the contribution of Peyronie disease to that skew
  • Morphology: fascicles of fibroblasts with associated collagen

Deep Fibromatosis (Desmoid Tumors)

  • F > M (opposite sex predominance from the superficial form)
  • Infiltrate but do not metastasize — however, infiltration into surrounding tissue can still make them difficult to resect and cause real problems for the patient, even without metastatic potential
  • Molecular: mutations in CTNNB1 (beta-catenin) or APC — the same genes discussed in the GI tract (APC/beta-catenin/Wnt pathway)
  • Morphology: interlocking spindle cells with relatively bland nuclei and collagen
DIAGNOSTIC REALITY CHECK: Dr. Kemp was candid that many soft tissue spindle cell tumors look very similar to each other under the microscope — pathologists lean heavily on clinical history and immunohistochemistry to sort them out, not gestalt alone.

Rhabdomyosarcoma

A malignant tumor of skeletal muscle — three main forms: embryonal (including botryoid), alveolar, and pleomorphic.

FROM LECTURE: Dr. Kemp recalled an OB/GYN colleague's line about sarcoma botryoides: "this is a tumor you will have to learn for every single test you ever take, and you'll never in your entire life see" — rare, but very characteristic when it does show up.

SECTION 14

Leiomyoma & Leiomyosarcoma

Leiomyoma

A benign tumor of smooth muscle. By far the most common location is the uterus — the familiar "uterine fibroid." When not uterine, a soft tissue leiomyoma is most often found in the extremities.

  • Gross: a well-circumscribed nodule, tan-white to white-tan, firm to palpation
  • Microscopic: interlocking, intersecting fascicles of spindle cells; bland nuclei, minimal to no pleomorphism, few mitotic figures (consistent with a benign process)
  • Molecular: loss of function of fumarate hydratase (FH) — an enzyme in the Krebs cycle

Leiomyosarcoma

The malignant smooth muscle tumor.

  • Gross: infiltrative, unlike the well-circumscribed leiomyoma
  • Microscopic: similar interlocking fascicle pattern to leiomyoma, but with pleomorphism and mitotic activity — the expected malignant-versus-benign distinction
  • Demographics/location: older patients (roughly 40s–60s); common sites include retroperitoneum and thigh, in addition to the uterus
  • Immunohistochemistry: actin, desmin, and caldesmon are good markers of smooth muscle origin
COMPARE ON HISTOLOGY: Leiomyosarcoma is markedly more cellular than leiomyoma, with hyperchromatic, enlarged nuclei, a higher nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio, pleomorphism, and identifiable mitotic figures — leiomyoma should show essentially none of this.

SECTION 15

Synovial Sarcoma

The name is actually a misnomer — it is not derived from synovium. It accounts for roughly 10% of soft tissue sarcomas, making it one of the more common sarcoma subtypes, and typically presents at age 20–40.

  • Location: usually the lower extremity
  • Molecular: characteristic t(X;18) — fusion of the SS18 (SYT) gene with one of the SSX genes, leading to loss of normal cell-cycle control

Morphology

  • Biphasic (more common): gland-like structures within a spindle-cell neoplasm — the glandular component can resemble epithelium within a stromal background
  • Monophasic: just spindle cells in fascicles, which makes it harder to diagnose and increases the importance of molecular/IHC testing
  • The glandular cells stain positive for keratin and other epithelial antigens, supporting the biphasic pattern
Practice Questions
SECTION 16

Undifferentiated Pleomorphic Sarcoma

Formerly called malignant fibrous histiocytoma — essentially a "wastebasket" diagnosis for a sarcoma that can't be classified as anything else. The older name was misleading, since "histiocytoma" implies a macrophage/histiocyte origin, whereas this tumor doesn't actually have that origin — hence the renaming to "undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma."

On immunohistochemistry, it stains negative for smooth muscle, neural, and skeletal muscle markers — it doesn't fit any specific lineage, which is exactly why it's called "undifferentiated."

  • Demographics: most common in the 40s–70s
  • Location: deep soft tissue, most often the thigh (a common theme — when guessing an extremity location for a soft tissue sarcoma, the thigh is a good default)

Morphology

The name tells you most of what you need to know: pleomorphic cells (variable in size and shape), mitotic figures, and possible necrosis, arising in deep soft tissue and derived from mesenchymal tissue (a sarcoma) without a specific cell-type origin identifiable (undifferentiated).

SECTION 17

Bone Tumors: Overview & Osteogenic Tumors

General Categories

  • Hematopoietic: myeloma and lymphoma — and notably, multiple myeloma is the single most common bone tumor overall (excluding metastases)
  • Chondrogenic: derived from cartilage
  • Osteogenic: derived from bone
  • Unknown origin
  • Notochordal (occasionally)
LOCATION, LOCATION, LOCATION: As with many bone lesions, where a tumor sits in the bone — epiphysis, diaphysis, or metaphysis — is a major diagnostic clue, alongside patient age. That said, these are guides, not absolutes: exceptions exist (e.g., Ewing sarcoma is usually seen in children but can occasionally occur in adults) — always treat the patient as an individual, even while using demographic patterns for test purposes.

Clinical Presentation

Bone tumors can be asymptomatic (never diagnosed, especially if small or slow-growing), can present with a pathologic fracture, or can cause pain from nerve impingement.

Osteogenic Tumors

Two benign forms — osteoid osteoma and osteoblastoma — and one malignant form, osteosarcoma.

Osteoid osteoma vs. osteoblastoma
Osteoid OsteomaOsteoblastoma
Size< 2 cm> 2 cm
Reactive cortical bone collarPresentTypically absent
SymptomsNocturnal pain, relieved by aspirinPain, but not aspirin-responsive
LocationAppendicular skeleton (e.g., femur, tibia)Axial skeleton, commonly the spine

Interestingly, the two tumors look fairly similar histologically despite these differences — the size cutoff doesn't fully explain why the symptom pattern differs, but it's a well-established distinction worth remembering as-is.

Osteosarcoma

  • Accounts for ~20% of primary bone tumors and is the most common malignant tumor of bone
  • Most occur in patients under age 20; about 25% occur in older adults
  • Primary osteosarcoma: arises without an identifiable underlying disease (aside from possible genetic predisposition)
  • Secondary osteosarcoma: arises in the setting of another process — Paget disease, bone infarct, prior radiation — and tends to occur in older patients
  • Location: metaphysis of long bones — about 50% around the knee (distal femur or proximal tibia)

Osteosarcoma is characteristically both lytic and blastic — it forms bone (blastic) while also infiltrating and eroding (lytic). A hallmark radiographic finding is Codman triangle: the tumor grows beneath the periosteum and lifts it away from the bone at an angle, producing a triangular radiographic shadow.

Subtypes are classified by location (intramedullary, intracortical, or surface), grade (low vs. high), whether primary or secondary, and histologic pattern — osteoblastic, chondroblastic, fibroblastic, telangiectatic, small cell, or giant cell. Regardless of subtype, the diagnostic hallmark is that the tumor lays down osteoid (bone matrix) — seen as lacy, pink, eosinophilic material between malignant cells. If a cartilaginous component is present alongside osteoid formation, it's still called a chondroblastic osteosarcoma, not a chondrosarcoma.

Molecular

  • RB (retinoblastoma) mutation: RB normally negatively regulates the cell cycle
  • TP53 mutation: p53 is the "guardian of the genome," normally promoting DNA repair and apoptosis of irreparable cells
  • CDKN2A inactivation: this locus encodes both p16 (negatively regulates cyclin-dependent kinases) and p14 (supports p53) — losing both knocks out two separate cell-cycle brakes
  • MDM2 and CDK4 overexpression: these normally inhibit p53 and RB, respectively — overexpression excessively suppresses both tumor suppressors

SECTION 18

Osteochondroma & Chondroma

Cartilage-forming neoplasms can be benign or malignant. Benign forms: osteochondroma, chondroma, chondroblastoma, and chondromyxoid fibroma. The malignant form is chondrosarcoma.

Osteochondroma (Exostosis)

Essentially, bone that grows and branches off at an angle, then stops — a growth of bone capped by cartilage, where the cartilage cap defines it as a cartilaginous tumor even though the underlying structure is bone. The medullary cavity of the lesion is continuous with the medullary cavity of the underlying bone — a distinctive feature.

  • Usually solitary, though multiple lesions occur in certain syndromes
  • Many are asymptomatic and never diagnosed
  • Location: metaphysis of long bones, near the knee (same neighborhood as osteosarcoma); occasionally pelvis, scapula, or ribs
  • Gross: looks like a polyp/outgrowth of bone with a disorganized hyaline cartilage cap on top
MALIGNANT POTENTIAL: The disorganized hyaline cartilage cap of an osteochondroma can potentially give rise to a chondrosarcoma.

Multiple hereditary exostosis syndrome is autosomal dominant, associated with EXT1 or EXT2 mutations, and can also be seen in sporadic (more common) osteochondromas. These genes normally synthesize heparan sulfate proteoglycans; the resulting abnormal glycosaminoglycans are thought to prevent normal diffusion of hedgehog signaling, which regulates cartilage growth.

Chondroma

Commonly found in the hands and feet. Can be within the medullary cavity (enchondroma) or on the bone surface (juxtacortical chondroma). Typically presents at age 20–59.

LOCATION IS THE KEY DIFFERENTIATOR FROM CHONDROSARCOMA: Chondrosarcomas are usually found in the axial skeleton (hip, shoulder); chondromas are in the hand. A cartilaginous tumor found in the hand is essentially always a chondroma — a chondrosarcoma there would be rare enough to require a specialized bone pathologist to confirm.

Grossly resembles hyaline cartilage — blue-gray-white, semi-translucent. Microscopically, multiple nodules of hyaline cartilage.

Ollier disease and Maffucci syndrome are non-hereditary syndromes featuring multiple chondromas, with increased cellularity that can make them mimic chondrosarcoma histologically (making diagnosis more challenging — again, a case for an expert bone pathologist). Both are associated with IDH1/IDH2 mutations (the same mutations seen in astrocytoma and oligodendroglioma) — these interfere with DNA methylation regulation via 2-hydroxyglutarate accumulation.

SECTION 19

Chondrosarcoma

The second most common primary bone neoplasm. Affects males and females roughly equally.

Forms

  • Conventional (well-differentiated): can be central (intramedullary) or peripheral (juxtacortical); typically in patients over age 40
  • Clear cell: found in the epiphysis; can occur in younger patients (teens–20s)
  • Dedifferentiated: a low-grade (hyaline cartilage-like) component juxtaposed with a high-grade, fibrous-appearing malignant component
  • Mesenchymal: hyaline cartilage surrounded by sheets of small round cells

Location: usually the axial skeleton — pelvis, shoulder, occasionally ribs — and rarely the distal extremities (a cartilaginous tumor of the hand is a chondroma, not a chondrosarcoma).

Morphology

  • Conventional: grossly resembles abnormal, amorphous, gray-blue, semi-translucent cartilage; microscopically resembles hyaline cartilage but with increased cellularity and possible mitotic figures — more than one chondrocyte per lacuna is a useful clue favoring chondrosarcoma over chondroma
  • Dedifferentiated: low-grade hyaline cartilage-appearing tissue directly adjacent to a high-grade, fibrous/spindle-cell malignant component
  • Clear cell: tumor cells with clear cytoplasm
  • Mesenchymal: hyaline cartilage plus sheets of small round cells

SECTION 20

Giant Cell Tumor & Aneurysmal Bone Cyst

These are the benign bone tumors of unknown origin (the malignant ones of unknown origin are Ewing sarcoma and adamantinoma).

Giant Cell Tumor

  • Location: distinctive among bone tumors for being found in the epiphysis (most bone tumors are metaphyseal) — most often around the knee, though other locations occur
  • Gross: often destroys the cortex, leaving a thin shell of reactive bone

Two cell populations: uniform mononuclear cells, which are the actual neoplastic cells (derived from osteoblasts/osteoblast lineage precursors), and multinucleated giant cells, which are reactive osteoclasts rather than the neoplastic population — notably, the tumor's name describes the reactive component, not the neoplastic one.

IMPORTANT POINT: Although classified as benign, giant cell tumor of bone can metastasize to the lungs — a reminder that "benign" doesn't always mean "cannot spread."

The neoplastic mononuclear cells express RANKL, which binds RANK on osteoclast precursors and drives the recruitment/activation of the reactive giant cells (osteoclasts).

Aneurysmal Bone Cyst

  • Common in the first two decades of life
  • Location: metaphysis (epiphyseal involvement is uncommon, as with most bone tumors)
  • Complications: pain from expansion pushing on the periosteum; can cause partial nerve compression depending on location (particularly relevant in the vertebral column, where it can readily pinch a nerve)

As the name suggests — a large, dilated, blood-filled cystic space within the bone. Microscopically shows reactive changes: woven bone, fibroblasts and septa, and sometimes "blue bone" (densely calcified matrix).

SECTION 21

Ewing Sarcoma Family Tumors

This category now combines what used to be called Ewing sarcoma and primitive neuroectodermal tumor (PNET), now grouped together as "Ewing sarcoma family tumors."

  • ~80% occur in patients under age 20 — mostly a tumor of children and teens
  • Race: most commonly affects White patients; rare in Black or Asian patients
  • Location: notably found in the diaphysis of long bones (e.g., femur), unlike most bone tumors which favor the metaphysis; also occurs in flat bones such as the pelvis
  • Presentation: painful, enlarging mass — since these are small round cells that must divide rapidly to remain a large mass, they tend to expand against bone and cause pain relatively quickly

Morphology

A small, round, blue cell tumor — can show Homer Wright rosettes (a circle of cells around central fibrillary material, similar to what's seen in some CNS tumors), mitotic figures, and geographic necrosis.

Molecular

Characteristic t(11;22) translocation, fusing the EWSR1 gene to the FLI1 gene.

LOCATION AS A DIAGNOSTIC CLUE: Ewing sarcoma is one of the few tumors classically associated with the diaphysis — most bone tumors favor the metaphysis or epiphysis, so diaphyseal location plus a young patient should raise this on the differential.

SECTION 22

Plasmacytoma, Multiple Myeloma & Metastatic Bone Tumors

Plasmacytoma vs. Multiple Myeloma

Both are derived from plasma cells. Multiple myeloma behaves more like a leukemic process — malignant plasma cells are found throughout the bone marrow, with resulting osteolytic lesions. A plasmacytoma is a solitary mass composed of plasma cells rather than a marrow-wide process.

HIGH-YIELD FACT: Multiple myeloma is the single most common bone tumor, excluding metastatic disease.

On histology, an individual malignant plasma cell looks the same whether it's from a plasmacytoma or multiple myeloma — clinical context (solitary mass vs. diffuse marrow involvement) is what distinguishes them, not the cell morphology itself: eccentric nucleus, clock-face chromatin, and a perinuclear eosinophilic zone (the Golgi "hof").

Metastatic Tumors to Bone

In adults, the most common primary sources are prostate, breast, kidney, and lung — together accounting for about 75% of bony metastases. (Notably, colon cancer is not on this high-yield list.)

In children, common sources include neuroblastoma, Wilms tumor, osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, and rhabdomyosarcoma.

  • Metastases are most often found in the axial skeleton
  • Can be osteoblastic or osteolytic — prostate is classically associated with osteoblastic metastases; kidney, lung, and GI primaries are more typically osteolytic
  • Osteoblastic metastases work by tumor cells secreting substances that upregulate RANKL, which stimulates osteoblast activity
  • Complication: pathologic fracture at the site of metastatic bone disease

SECTION 23

Fibrous Cortical Defect / NOF & Fibrous Dysplasia

Fibrous Cortical Defect and Non-Ossifying Fibroma

  • Most common in children — found in roughly 30% of children age 2 and older
  • Often small (around 0.5 cm), so easily missed incidentally
  • Distinction between the two is based on size: smaller lesions are called fibrous cortical defect; larger lesions are called non-ossifying fibroma (NOF)
  • Microscopic: fibroblasts with associated macrophages — the same appearance in both

Fibrous Dysplasia

Best understood as a focal failure of normal bone development rather than a true tumor — all the components of bone are present, but they're disorganized and immature. The bone is essentially "trying" to form normally but can't complete the process correctly.

  • Demographics: roughly M = F; early adolescence
  • Can be monostotic (one bone) or polyostotic (multiple bones)
DON'T MEMORIZE THE EXHAUSTIVE LOCATION LIST: Dr. Kemp explicitly said not to memorize a long list of possible locations for fibrous dysplasia — knowing the overall concept and the associated syndromes below matters more than the location list.

Associated Syndromes

  • Mazabraud syndrome: fibrous dysplasia plus myxomas (myxomas appear myxoid/bluish on histology)
  • McCune-Albright syndrome: polyostotic fibrous dysplasia plus café-au-lait skin spots and endocrine abnormalities — most commonly precocious puberty

A gain-of-function mutation in GNAS1 produces a constitutively active G protein that promotes cellular proliferation and disrupts normal osteoblast differentiation — consistent with the idea that all the components to make bone are present, but differentiation/maturation is disrupted.

TIMING DETERMINES DISTRIBUTION: If the GNAS1 mutation occurs during embryogenesis (before the mutant cells have a chance to become restricted to one area), the result is the polyostotic form. If it occurs after the skeletal system has already formed, the mutation is confined to a single site, producing the monostotic form.

Morphology

Gross: intramedullary location, described as "gritty." Microscopic: curvilinear trabeculae of woven bone (with cells embedded, but lacking the organized lamellar pattern of mature bone) and minimal osteoblastic rimming — normally you'd expect osteoblasts lining newly formed woven bone, but here there's little to no rimming. The trabeculae are classically described as resembling Chinese characters. Cartilage nodules, cystic change, and hemorrhage can also be seen.

Symptoms

The monostotic form is often asymptomatic. Depending on location, fibrous dysplasia can cause nerve impingement or pain, and — like any bone lesion — the affected bone is weaker and more prone to fracture. The polyostotic form more commonly causes fractures and deformities, and can rarely undergo malignant transformation into a sarcoma.
