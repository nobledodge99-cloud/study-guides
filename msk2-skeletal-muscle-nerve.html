SECTION 1

Neuromuscular Junction Diseases — Overview

Disorders of the neuromuscular junction (NMJ) disrupt the transfer of the electrical signal from the presynaptic motor neuron terminal to the postsynaptic muscle membrane. The three classic autoimmune/genetic disorders — myasthenia gravis, Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome, and congenital myasthenic syndromes — are distinguished largely by where the defect sits (presynaptic vs. postsynaptic) and how weakness behaves with repetitive use. Two bacterial toxins, from Clostridium tetani and Clostridium botulinum, act directly on NMJ physiology and produce opposite clinical pictures.

Framework The NMJ story is a game of "where's the antibody/toxin, and what does that do to acetylcholine?" Postsynaptic AChR loss (myasthenia gravis) → weakness worsens with use. Presynaptic calcium channel loss (Lambert-Eaton) → weakness improves with use. Get this rule down first; everything else hangs off it.
SECTION 2

Myasthenia Gravis

Mechanism

An autoimmune disease caused by autoantibodies directed against neuromuscular junction components — most commonly the postsynaptic acetylcholine receptor (AChR), but also against muscle-specific receptor tyrosine kinase (MuSK) and, less commonly, low-density lipoprotein-receptor related protein (LRP4). Dr. Kemp noted LRP4 is a minor/lower-yield detail — worth recognizing the name, but AChR and MuSK are the antibodies to know well.

Demographics

Affects about 2/100,000 people, with a bimodal age distribution:

  • Early onset (<50 years): F > M; associated with thymic hyperplasia/enlargement
  • Late onset: M = F; associated with thymoma

Symptoms

  • Ptosis (drooping eyelids) and diplopia (double vision) from extra-ocular muscle weakness — involvement of extra-ocular muscles is unusual in most other myopathies, making it a distinguishing clue
  • Variable expression: ranges from isolated extra-ocular involvement to weakness severe enough to require mechanical ventilation
  • If anti-MuSK antibodies are present: neck, shoulder, facial, respiratory, and bulbar muscles are more typically involved
  • Weakness fluctuates and characteristically worsens with repetitive/sustained use (classically worse at end of day, better after rest/sleep)
High-yield vignette pattern Ptosis + diplopia, worse as the day progresses, better after sleep → myasthenia gravis. In a patient <50, expect thymic hyperplasia on chest CT; in a patient with late-onset disease, expect a thymoma.

Practice Questions

Q1. A 40-year-old male presents with double vision and drooping eyelids that are worse toward the end of the day and better in the morning after sleep. Assuming an abnormality is present, a CT scan of the chest would most likely reveal which of the following?

This presentation (fatigable ptosis/diplopia, worse with use) is classic for myasthenia gravis. Early-onset disease (<50 years) is associated with thymic hyperplasia rather than a discrete mass, thymoma, or unrelated cardiopulmonary findings.

Q2. A 65-year-old woman develops progressive ptosis, dysphagia, and neck weakness. A chest CT reveals an anterior mediastinal mass. Which antibody target is most specifically associated with this later-onset presentation of myasthenia gravis?

Late-onset myasthenia gravis (M=F) is associated with thymoma, and the anterior mediastinal mass here reflects that. The antibodies are still typically against the postsynaptic AChR. Calcium channel antibodies point to Lambert-Eaton; ryanodine receptor to malignant hyperthermia; anti-Jo1 (histidyl t-RNA synthetase) to dermatomyositis/polymyositis.

Q3. In a patient with myasthenia gravis whose weakness prominently involves the neck, shoulder, facial, respiratory, and bulbar muscles rather than the classic ocular pattern, which antibody is most likely responsible?

Anti-MuSK antibody-associated myasthenia gravis tends to spare the classic ocular pattern and instead prominently affects neck, shoulder, facial, respiratory, and bulbar musculature.
SECTION 3

Lambert-Eaton Myasthenic Syndrome

Mechanism

Caused by autoantibodies against presynaptic voltage-gated calcium channels, which inhibits calcium influx and therefore the release of acetylcholine into the synaptic cleft.

Symptoms

  • Weakness most often affects the extremities (proximal limb-girdle pattern)
  • Weakness characteristically improves with repetitive use — repeated firing builds up presynaptic calcium, which enhances acetylcholine release
Key association Lambert-Eaton is most often a paraneoplastic syndrome. There is usually an underlying malignancy, classically small cell lung carcinoma. Always think "who has this — and do they have lung cancer?"

Practice Questions

Q1. A 68-year-old man with a 50-pack-year smoking history reports proximal leg weakness that is worst on first standing but improves somewhat as he continues walking. Workup reveals antibodies against presynaptic calcium channels. Which underlying condition should be most strongly suspected?

Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome is most often paraneoplastic, classically associated with small cell lung carcinoma — especially relevant given this patient's heavy smoking history.

Q2. Which finding on repetitive nerve stimulation testing would best distinguish Lambert-Eaton syndrome from myasthenia gravis?

In Lambert-Eaton, repeated stimulation increases presynaptic calcium accumulation and augments acetylcholine release, producing an incremental (facilitating) response — the opposite of the decremental response seen in myasthenia gravis.
SECTION 4

Congenital Myasthenic Syndromes & NMJ Infections

Congenital Myasthenic Syndrome

Caused by inherited mutations affecting neuromuscular junction proteins — the defect can be presynaptic, synaptic, or postsynaptic. Symptoms can mimic either myasthenia gravis or Lambert-Eaton syndrome, depending on which protein/location is affected.

Infections Affecting the Neuromuscular Junction

OrganismMechanismClinical result
Clostridium tetaniBlocks inhibitory neurons, leading to increased release of acetylcholineSustained muscle contraction (tetanus)
Clostridium botulinumInhibits acetylcholine releaseFlaccid paralysis
Opposite toxins, opposite results Tetanus toxin removes the brakes on the system (blocks inhibitory neurons) → too much ACh → rigid, sustained contraction. Botulinum toxin cuts the signal at the source (blocks ACh release) → flaccid paralysis. Contrast this with the antibody-mediated NMJ diseases above, which act at the receptor/channel level rather than on neurotransmitter release regulation.

Practice Questions

Q1. A patient with an infected puncture wound develops trismus (lockjaw) and generalized muscle rigidity. This is best explained by which mechanism?

Clostridium tetani toxin blocks inhibitory neurons, which increases acetylcholine release and produces sustained, rigid muscle contraction — unlike botulinum toxin, which inhibits ACh release and causes flaccid paralysis.

Q2. An infant develops constipation, poor feeding, a weak cry, and diffuse hypotonia ("floppy baby") after exposure to honey. Which mechanism is responsible?

Infant botulism from Clostridium botulinum spores (classically in honey) inhibits acetylcholine release at the NMJ, producing flaccid paralysis/hypotonia rather than the rigidity seen with tetanus.
SECTION 5

Skeletal Muscle Atrophy

Etiologies

Loss of innervation, older age, disuse, cachexia, and myopathies can all cause skeletal muscle atrophy.

Pattern-Based Clues

  • If atrophy is clustered/grouped → suggests a neurogenic origin
  • If atrophy is peri-fascicular (at the edges of fascicles) → suggests dermatomyositis
  • If atrophy selectively affects type II fibers with sparing of type I → suggests disuse or corticosteroid use

On cross-section, normal skeletal muscle fibers are mostly uniform in size. In atrophy of neurogenic origin, denervated fibers cluster together in groups ("grouped atrophy") because a single damaged motor neuron affects all the myocytes within its motor unit.

Neuropathic vs. Myopathic Atrophy

NeuropathicMyopathic
Overall changesMuscle fiber atrophyMuscle fiber atrophy
Specific changesGrouped atrophy and fiber type groupingSegmental necrosis of individual myofibers; myofiber hypertrophy; cytoplasmic inclusions

Practice Questions

Q1. A muscle biopsy shows clusters of small, angulated atrophic fibers grouped together among fascicles of normal-sized fibers. This "grouped atrophy" pattern is most consistent with which process?

Grouped atrophy reflects loss of innervation to an entire motor unit — because one motor neuron innervates many myocytes, denervation atrophies them together as a cluster, a hallmark of neurogenic atrophy.

Q2. A biopsy from a bedridden patient on long-term high-dose corticosteroids would most likely show selective atrophy of which fiber type?

Disuse and corticosteroid-associated atrophy selectively affects type II (fast-twitch) fibers while sparing type I fibers.
SECTION 6

Peripheral Neuropathy — Axonal vs. Demyelinating

Categories of Neuropathy

  • Inflammatory: unknown cause (Guillain-Barré, chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy/CIDP, sarcoidosis), infections (leprosy, AIDS, Lyme disease, varicella-zoster), vasculitis, SLE/RA
  • Associated with hematologic disease/neoplasm: paraneoplastic, lymphomas, monoclonal gammopathies
  • Metabolic/nutritional: diabetes mellitus, renal failure, thiamine or B12 deficiency
  • Toxic: alcohol, arsenic, lead, organophosphates
  • Traumatic
  • Hereditary: hereditary motor and sensory neuropathies (Charcot-Marie-Tooth), amyloid neuropathies, disorders of lipid metabolism (Refsum, Tangier disease)

Clinical Presentation

Axonal polyneuropathyDemyelinating polyneuropathy
Sensory vs. motorSensory > motorSensory loss not the usual presenting sign
ReflexesLoss of stretch reflexes at ankle—
Sensory signsNumbness, tingling, burning; usually symmetrical—
Weakness pattern—Affects proximal and distal muscles

Pathology of Axonal Degeneration

Early/acute: disruption of the myelin sheath; formation of "ovoids" (axonal fragments surrounded by disintegrating myelin, best seen by EM and teased-nerve preparations); phagocytosis of nerve fiber fragments by Schwann cells and macrophages. Axonal regeneration produces small, closely-packed myelinated fibers, guided by Schwann cells, with shorter and thinner myelin internodes.

Late/chronic: loss of myelinated axons; increased endoneurial connective tissue; clusters of regenerating nerve fibers.

Pathology of Acute Segmental Demyelination

Damage to the myelin sheath or Schwann cells causes loss of a myelin segment, usually adjacent to the node of Ranvier, producing thin myelin sheaths. With remyelination, the internodes are of unequal size (shorter and thinner). Repeated cycles of demyelination and remyelination lead to onion bulb formation — proliferation of Schwann cells and basement membrane around the axon.

Correction from lecture — muscle atrophy and neuropathy type While axonal injury can lead to skeletal muscle atrophy, demyelination most often does not. With neuronal (axonal) injury the muscle loses its stimulus entirely; with demyelination, the stimulus still reaches the muscle — it's just slower in arriving.

Practice Questions

Q1. A patient presents with symmetric numbness, tingling, and burning in the feet, along with loss of ankle reflexes, but relatively preserved motor strength. This presentation is most consistent with which pattern of neuropathy?

Axonal polyneuropathy classically presents with sensory symptoms out of proportion to motor symptoms, symmetric numbness/tingling/burning, and loss of ankle stretch reflexes.

Q2. Which finding would be expected in a nerve biopsy from a patient with a chronic demyelinating polyneuropathy who has undergone repeated cycles of demyelination and remyelination?

Onion bulbs are the classic finding of repeated segmental demyelination/remyelination cycles. Axonal ovoids are seen in early axonal degeneration; grouped atrophy is a muscle (not nerve) finding of denervation; peri-fascicular atrophy points to dermatomyositis.

Q3. Why does demyelinating neuropathy, unlike axonal injury, most often fail to produce significant skeletal muscle atrophy?

With axonal injury, the muscle loses its stimulus entirely. With demyelination, conduction is slowed but the stimulus still reaches the muscle, so significant denervation atrophy is less likely.
SECTION 7

Rhabdomyolysis

Description

Necrosis of skeletal muscle myofibers from a wide range of conditions. Major complication: acute kidney injury.

Causes

CategoryExamples
TraumaCrush injury, burns, frostbite
TemperatureHypo/hyperthermia
Exertion (incl. seizures), ischemia, metabolicHypokalemia, hypophosphatemia, hypocalcemia, diabetic ketoacidosis, hypothyroidism
InfectionViral (influenza A/B, HIV); bacterial (S. aureus, Streptococcus pyogenes)
MyopathiesPolymyositis/dermatomyositis, infectious myopathies
ElectricalIncluding defibrillation
DrugsAlcohol, cocaine, amphetamine, lipid-lowering drugs
ToxinsSnake/spider venom, heavy metals
GeneticDisorders of glycolysis/lipid metabolism, mitochondrial disorders, purine nucleotide cycle defects

Laboratory Testing

Rhabdomyolysis lab pattern • CK: >500 IU/L is diagnostic; >5,000 IU/L raises risk of kidney injury
• Potassium: >6.0 mmol/L (note: hypokalemia can also be a cause of rhabdomyolysis)
• Phosphorus: >6.0 mmol/L (note: low phosphorus can also be a cause)
• Calcium: <8.0 mg/dL (deposits into damaged muscle)
• BUN:creatinine ratio: <10:1, often <6:1
• Anion gap: elevated (increased organic acids)
• Myoglobinuria: common, serum myoglobin >0.5–1.5 mg/dL
Urine dipstick pitfall A urine dipstick positive for "blood" in a patient with suspected rhabdomyolysis may actually reflect myoglobin rather than hemoglobin — both trip the dipstick's heme-based reaction. Confirmatory testing (e.g., microscopy showing no RBCs, or specific myoglobin assay) is needed to distinguish myoglobinuria from true hematuria/hemoglobinuria.

Pathology

Coagulative necrosis of the muscle fiber; resolving lesions may show dystrophic calcium deposition. Myoglobin casts can be identified in the kidney (contributing to acute tubular injury).

Practice Questions

Q1. A 23-year-old male spends 5 hours outside in cold weather after drinking at a party and is found the next morning with frostbite of the hands and feet. Laboratory testing is most likely to reveal which of the following?

Hypothermia predisposes to rhabdomyolysis. Of the choices, only elevated serum phosphorus fits the expected pattern. Rhabdomyolysis instead causes decreased serum calcium and increased serum potassium (unless hypokalemia was the underlying cause), and a BUN:creatinine ratio <10:1 rather than >40:1.

Q2. A patient with rhabdomyolysis has a CK of 8,200 IU/L. Which complication is this patient at greatest risk for?

CK >5,000 IU/L confers significant risk for acute kidney injury, the major complication of rhabdomyolysis (via myoglobin cast formation and tubular injury).
SECTION 8

Dermatomyositis

Terminology: Myopathy = any abnormal condition/disease of muscular tissue. Muscular dystrophy = an inborn abnormality of muscle associated with dysfunction and progressive deterioration.

Clinical Features

  • Proximal muscle weakness — slow onset, symmetric, with myalgias; distal involvement occurs late
  • Heliotrope rash: lilac discoloration of the upper eyelids with periorbital edema
  • Gottron papules: scaling erythematous rash on the extensor surfaces — knuckles, elbows, knees
  • Can affect the esophagus, lungs (interstitial lung disease), and heart (cardiac failure)

Pathogenesis

Autoimmune condition that damages blood vessels, leading to muscle injury, through activation of C5b-9 (the complement membrane attack complex).

Autoantibodies

AntibodyTarget / Association
Anti-Mi2Against helicases used in nucleosome remodeling; associated with Gottron papules and heliotrope rash
Anti-Jo1Against histidyl t-RNA synthetase; associated with interstitial lung disease, arthritis, "mechanic's hands"
Anti-P155/P140Against transcriptional regulators; associated with juvenile dermatomyositis and paraneoplastic disease

Morphology

  • Telangiectasias of nail folds, gums, and eyelids; loss of capillaries in muscle
  • Mononuclear cell infiltrates, predominantly in perimysial connective tissue and around blood vessels
  • Peri-fascicular atrophy (atrophy concentrated at the edges of fascicles) — a hallmark finding
  • Segmental fiber necrosis and regeneration may be seen
  • C5b-9 can be demonstrated by immunohistochemistry

Juvenile vs. Adult Dermatomyositis

Practice Questions

Q1. A 50-year-old woman presents with symmetric proximal muscle weakness, a lilac discoloration of her upper eyelids, and a scaling rash over her knuckles and elbows. Muscle biopsy would most likely show which pattern of atrophy?

This presentation (heliotrope rash, Gottron papules, proximal weakness) is classic for dermatomyositis, whose hallmark biopsy finding is peri-fascicular atrophy. Grouped atrophy suggests neurogenic disease; type II-selective atrophy suggests disuse/steroids; rimmed vacuoles suggest inclusion body myositis.

Q2. Which complement component is activated in the pathogenesis of dermatomyositis, damaging the intramuscular blood vessels?

Dermatomyositis pathogenesis centers on activation of C5b-9, the complement membrane attack complex, which damages the microvasculature and secondarily injures muscle.

Q3. A patient with dermatomyositis is found to have "mechanic's hands" and interstitial lung disease. Which autoantibody is most likely present?

Anti-Jo1 antibodies (against histidyl t-RNA synthetase) are associated with interstitial lung disease, arthritis, and "mechanic's hands."
SECTION 9

Polymyositis & Inclusion Body Myositis

Polymyositis

  • Presents with myalgia and weakness like dermatomyositis, but no skin involvement
  • Can involve the lungs and heart; has associated autoantibodies
  • Pathogenesis: mediated by CD8+ cytotoxic T cells (in contrast to the complement-mediated vasculopathy of dermatomyositis)
  • Key distinguishing point: no peri-fascicular atrophy, unlike dermatomyositis

Inclusion Body Myositis

  • The most common inflammatory myopathy in patients >65 years
  • Slowly progressive muscle weakness involving the quadriceps and distal upper extremities; can affect esophagus and pharyngeal muscles
  • No myositis-associated autoantibodies

Like polymyositis: endomysial inflammatory cells (CD8+) with focal invasion of myofibers; admixed degenerating and regenerating myofibers.

Unlike polymyositis: cytoplasmic inclusions ("rimmed vacuoles") containing TDP-43 (also seen in frontotemporal dementia), β-amyloid (also seen in Alzheimer disease), and ubiquitin; endomysial fibrosis and fatty replacement. Dr. Kemp described inclusion body myositis as essentially the muscle equivalent of a neurodegenerative process — the same proteinopathy machinery, just in myofibers instead of neurons.

Three inflammatory myopathies, one differentiator each Dermatomyositis = skin findings + peri-fascicular atrophy + complement-mediated. Polymyositis = same weakness pattern, no skin/no peri-fascicular atrophy, CD8+ T cell-mediated. Inclusion body myositis = older patient, distal + proximal (quadriceps) weakness, rimmed vacuoles, no autoantibodies.

Practice Questions

Q1. A 72-year-old man reports slowly progressive weakness affecting his quadriceps and the finger flexors of both hands over two years. He has no rash and no detectable myositis-specific autoantibodies. Biopsy is most likely to show which finding?

This age, distribution (quadriceps + distal upper extremity), and absence of autoantibodies point to inclusion body myositis, whose defining finding is rimmed vacuole cytoplasmic inclusions containing TDP-43, β-amyloid, and ubiquitin.

Q2. A patient has proximal muscle weakness and myalgias without any skin findings. Biopsy shows CD8+ endomysial inflammatory infiltrate with focal myofiber invasion, but no peri-fascicular atrophy. What is the most likely diagnosis?

Polymyositis shares the weakness pattern of dermatomyositis but lacks skin involvement and peri-fascicular atrophy; it is mediated by CD8+ cytotoxic T cells rather than complement-mediated vasculopathy.
SECTION 10

Toxic Myopathies

Etiology

Prescription or recreational drugs, or hormonal imbalances/disease processes.

  • Statins (atorvastatin, simvastatin, pravastatin) — associated myopathy; because statins can also cause liver injury, patients are often monitored with routine liver function tests in addition to watching for muscle weakness/pain
  • Chloroquine/hydroxychloroquine — interfere with lysosomal function, creating a lysosomal storage myopathy
  • ICU myopathy — weakness/myopathy developing in critically ill patients; the mechanism is complex and not fully understood, so this is more a "be aware it exists" entity than one with well-defined pathophysiology to memorize
  • Thyrotoxic myopathy — can show elevated creatine kinase
  • Alcohol — can cause rhabdomyolysis, myoglobinuria, and renal failure

Practice Questions

Q1. A patient on long-term hydroxychloroquine for lupus develops progressive proximal weakness. Biopsy is most likely to show which underlying mechanism?

Chloroquine/hydroxychloroquine interfere with lysosomal function, producing a lysosomal storage myopathy — distinct from the immune-mediated inflammatory myopathies.
SECTION 11

Disorders of Glycogen or Lipid Metabolism

Two General Presentation Patterns

  • Acute/episodic: symptomatic only with exercise or fasting, which can lead to cramping and even rhabdomyolysis
  • Chronic/progressive: slowly progressive muscle damage

Named Forms

Practice Questions

Q1. A teenager reports muscle cramping and dark urine only after strenuous exercise, with normal function otherwise. This episodic pattern, tied to glycogen breakdown, is most consistent with which condition?

McArdle disease (myophosphorylase deficiency) classically causes episodic exercise-induced cramping and myoglobinuria due to impaired glycogen breakdown.

Q2. An infant presents with severe hypotonia and cardiomegaly due to impaired lysosomal conversion of glycogen to glucose. Which condition is this?

Pompe disease is the severe, infantile form of acid maltase deficiency, notable for prominent cardiac involvement in addition to hypotonia.
SECTION 12

Ion Channel Myopathies

General Features

  • Symptoms: myotonia and/or hypotonic paralysis associated with abnormal serum potassium concentrations
  • Inheritance: most are autosomal dominant
  • Can present with hypertonia or hypotonia; if hypotonic, serum potassium can be increased, decreased, or normal
  • Quick mnemonic: "ion channel" → think sodium and potassium channels, which is why these conditions track with abnormal serum potassium

Board favorite: RYR1 — the rest are lower priority Dr. Kemp explicitly singled out RYR1 as the one to know well because of its link to malignant hyperthermia. He noted that even the source textbook doesn't give a named syndrome or full feature list for every gene in this table (KCNJ2, SCN4A, and CACNA1S get comparatively minor treatment) — don't try to memorize every entry with equal weight; anchor on RYR1 first.

Practice Questions

Q1. Shortly after induction of general anesthesia with succinylcholine, a patient develops sudden high fever, tachycardia, tachypnea, and rigid muscle spasms. A mutation in which gene is most likely responsible?

This is the classic presentation of malignant hyperthermia, linked to RYR1 mutations, triggered by volatile anesthetics and depolarizing muscle relaxants like succinylcholine.

Q2. A patient with periodic paralysis is also found to have cardiac arrhythmias and mild skeletal abnormalities (low-set ears, clinodactyly). Which condition and gene fit this triad?

The triad of periodic paralysis, arrhythmias, and skeletal abnormalities is characteristic of Andersen-Tawil syndrome, caused by KCNJ2 mutations.
SECTION 13

Mitochondrial Myopathies

General Features

  • Pathogenesis: impaired ability of mitochondria to generate ATP, affecting skeletal muscle
  • Effects: weakness, increased serum creatine kinase, rhabdomyolysis
  • Symptoms: chronic progressive external ophthalmoplegia is common (extraocular muscles have the highest mitochondrial density per mass)
  • Morphology: ragged red fibers — aggregates of mitochondria seen on biopsy

Specific Disorders

DisorderFeatures
MELAS (mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, lactic acidosis, and stroke-like episodes)Neurologic and muscle dysfunction; reversible stroke-like episodes not corresponding to a vascular territory (though infarction can occur); altered cytochrome c oxidase expression; mutation in the gene encoding tRNA-leucine (MTTL1)
Kearns-Sayre syndromeOphthalmoplegia, pigmentary retinal degeneration, complete heart block

Practice Questions

Q1. A young patient presents with recurrent stroke-like episodes that don't correspond to a specific vascular territory, along with lactic acidosis and myopathy. Which mitochondrial disorder is most likely, and what gene is classically implicated?

MELAS is defined by stroke-like episodes not respecting vascular territories, lactic acidosis, and myopathy, classically from a mutation in the tRNA-leucine gene (MTTL1).

Q2. A patient presents with progressive external ophthalmoplegia, pigmentary retinal degeneration, and complete heart block. Which diagnosis fits this triad?

Kearns-Sayre syndrome is defined by the triad of ophthalmoplegia, pigmentary retinal degeneration, and complete heart block.

Q3. A muscle biopsy from a patient with a suspected mitochondrial myopathy shows aggregates of mitochondria on trichrome stain. What is this finding called?

Ragged red fibers — aggregates of mitochondria seen on modified Gomori trichrome stain — are the classic morphologic finding across the mitochondrial myopathies.
SECTION 14

Muscular Dystrophy: Duchenne & Becker

Three-Way Framework

FeatureMuscular DystrophiesCongenital Muscular DystrophyCongenital Myopathies
Present at birthNoYesYes
ProgressiveYesYesNo
How to use this table "Progressive" is the split that matters most on exam questions: if a floppy/hypotonic infant's weakness is static rather than worsening, think congenital myopathy, not a dystrophy.

Dystrophinopathies — Duchenne & Becker

  • Inheritance: X-linked. Duchenne = frameshift or point mutations; Becker = point mutations (in-frame, partially preserved protein)
  • Gene/protein: dystrophin, locus Xp21 — hence "dystrophinopathies"
  • Microscopic: myofiber necrosis and regeneration; muscle progressively replaced by adipose tissue and fibrosis; atrophic fibers surrounded by fibrous tissue; heart shows hypertrophy and fibrosis
  • Laboratory: creatine kinase markedly elevated early, then declines later in life as muscle mass is lost; genetic testing confirms the dystrophin mutation

Mechanism

The dystrophin-glycoprotein complex stabilizes the muscle cell membrane during contraction. Defects in this complex leave muscle cells vulnerable to membrane tears and disrupted intracellular signaling. Over time, degeneration outpaces the muscle's capacity for repair.

Clinical Features

  • Normal at birth
  • Weakness starts in the pelvis first (clumsiness, difficulty keeping up with peers), then progresses to the shoulders
  • Pseudohypertrophy of leg muscles (fat/fibrous replacement makes calves look enlarged)
  • Contractures, scoliosis, and worsening respiratory function develop
  • Dystrophin is also expressed in heart (→ cardiomyopathy) and brain (→ cognitive impairment)
FeatureDuchenneBecker
Incidence1:3,500 live birthsLess common
SeverityFatal by early adulthood (25–30 years)Less severe; near-normal life expectancy

Practice Questions

Q1. A 5-year-old boy has calves that appear enlarged but are weak, along with difficulty rising from the floor (climbing up his own legs to stand). Creatine kinase is markedly elevated. Biopsy would most likely show which of the following?

This vignette (pseudohypertrophic calves, Gower sign, elevated CK, young male) is classic for Duchenne muscular dystrophy, where biopsy shows myofiber necrosis/regeneration with progressive replacement by adipose and fibrous tissue.

Q2. Which of the following best distinguishes Becker from Duchenne muscular dystrophy at the molecular level?

Both are X-linked dystrophinopathies affecting the same gene (Xp21). Becker's point mutations tend to preserve a partially functional protein, producing a milder, later, near-normal-lifespan phenotype compared to Duchenne's more disruptive frameshift mutations.

Q3. The gene mutated in both Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophy is located at which chromosomal locus?

The dystrophin gene sits at Xp21, which is why Duchenne and Becker are grouped together as "dystrophinopathies."

Q4. Over the course of Duchenne muscular dystrophy, what is the typical trend in serum creatine kinase (CK)?

CK is markedly elevated early (reflecting ongoing myofiber necrosis) but declines later as functional muscle is replaced by fat and fibrosis, leaving less muscle tissue to release the enzyme.
SECTION 15

Other Muscular Dystrophies

Myotonic Dystrophy

  • Symptom: myotonia — sustained involuntary contraction (e.g., stiffness/inability to relax a grip)
  • Molecular: autosomal dominant; 95% have a trinucleotide (CTG) repeat expansion in the gene for dystrophia myotonica protein kinase (DMPK), ranging from >45 up to thousands of repeats
  • Mechanism: mutant DMPK mRNA sequesters muscleblind-like proteins involved in RNA splicing, causing abnormal gene expression — including a deficiency of the chloride channel CLC1
  • Demographics/features: manifests in late childhood with gait abnormalities (weak foot dorsiflexion), then hand/wrist involvement; can affect heart, eyes (cataracts), and endocrine system
  • Congenital myotonic dystrophy: patients with a CLC1 mutation specifically can present in infancy rather than late childhood — this is the entity referenced as "congenital myotonia" in the hypotonic-infant differential (Section 16)

Limb-Girdle Muscular Dystrophy

  • Symptoms: proximal musculature of trunk and limbs
  • Inheritance: heterogeneous — 7 autosomal dominant forms, 15 autosomal recessive forms; affects 1 in 25,000–50,000 people
  • Proteins involved: components of the dystrophin-glycoprotein complex, vesicle transport, membrane repair machinery, and the cytoskeleton

Emery-Dreifuss Muscular Dystrophy

  • Symptom triad: progressive muscle weakness/wasting; contractures of the elbows, ankles, and Achilles tendon (and spine); heart disease — 40% of patients die from arrhythmias
  • Inheritance: X-linked (protein: emerin) or autosomal dominant (protein: lamin A/C)
  • Proteins involved: structural proteins of the nuclear envelope
  • Mechanism: repetitive mechanical stress damages a structurally compromised nucleus

Fascioscapulohumeral Dystrophy

  • Symptoms: weakness of facial muscles and shoulder girdle (by age 20); weakness of lower trunk and foot dorsiflexors; normal life expectancy
  • Inheritance: autosomal dominant
  • Mechanism: overexpression of DUX4 (exact downstream mechanism unknown)
Lower testing priority Dr. Kemp specifically noted that because the downstream mechanism of DUX4 overexpression is unknown, FSHD is difficult to write detailed exam questions about — know the name/inheritance/clinical pattern, but don't over-invest here relative to Duchenne/Becker or Emery-Dreifuss.
High-yield vignette pattern Elbow/ankle/Achilles contractures + cardiac arrhythmia running in the family → Emery-Dreifuss. If inherited from an affected mother (autosomal dominant transmission), think lamin A/C; if X-linked pattern, think emerin.

Practice Questions

Q1. A 31-year-old male has slowly developed muscle weakness, contractures of his elbows and ankles, and contractures of his Achilles tendons. His mother had a similar disease process and died from a cardiac dysrhythmia. Mutation of the gene for which protein is responsible?

This is Emery-Dreifuss muscular dystrophy. Since the disease was transmitted from an affected mother (rather than an X-linked pattern), the autosomal dominant form is implicated, caused by mutations in lamin A/C rather than the X-linked protein emerin.

Q2. An adult patient has difficulty releasing his grip after a handshake, along with foot dorsiflexion weakness, cataracts, and mild endocrine abnormalities. Genetic testing shows a large CTG trinucleotide repeat expansion. Which protein's mRNA is most directly implicated?

This is myotonic dystrophy — grip myotonia, foot dorsiflexion weakness, cataracts, and endocrine involvement, with a CTG repeat expansion in DMPK whose mutant mRNA sequesters muscleblind-like splicing proteins.

Q3. A 22-year-old has progressive facial and shoulder-girdle weakness with a normal life expectancy and no cardiac involvement. What is the underlying molecular abnormality?

Facioscapulohumeral dystrophy presents with facial/shoulder-girdle weakness by age 20, autosomal dominant inheritance, normal life expectancy, and is linked to overexpression of DUX4.

Q4. A 35-year-old man has slowly progressive weakness of the trunk and proximal limb muscles, sparing the face and extraocular muscles. His father had a similar course. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

Proximal, trunk-and-limb weakness with autosomal dominant transmission across generations fits limb-girdle muscular dystrophy. Duchenne is X-linked (would not pass father-to-son) and myasthenia gravis/Kearns-Sayre have distinct extraocular and systemic features not described here.
SECTION 16

Congenital Muscular Dystrophies & the Hypotonic Infant

Defects in the Extracellular Matrix Surrounding Myofibers

  • Ullrich congenital muscular dystrophy (UCMD): mutation in collagen VI alpha genes; features hypotonia, proximal contractures, and distal hyperextensibility, with mismatched expression of perlecan and collagen VI
  • Merosin deficiency: mutation in merosin (laminin-alpha2)

Defects in Receptors for the Extracellular Matrix

  • Mechanism: disruption of post-translational modification of alpha-dystroglycan (severe mutations can cause fetal demise)
  • Pathogenesis: alpha-dystroglycan is important for CNS and eye development
  • Features: floppy infant with seizures, intellectual disability, and blindness

Differential Diagnosis of the Hypotonic Infant

A floppy/hypotonic infant's weakness can originate from several distinct levels of the neuromuscular axis:

  • Primary disease of skeletal muscle → congenital myasthenic syndrome, congenital myotonia (the CLC1-mutation, infancy-onset form of myotonic dystrophy discussed in Section 15), congenital myopathies (central core disease, nemaline myopathy, centronuclear myopathy, congenital fiber type disproportion), or congenital muscular dystrophies
  • Abnormalities of the brain
  • Neuronopathies → spinal muscular atrophy
Localize first Before reaching for a specific diagnosis, place the hypotonic infant along the axis: brain → anterior horn cell (neuronopathy/SMA) → NMJ → muscle itself. The clinical clues (seizures/cognitive findings vs. pure motor weakness vs. myotonia) point to the level of the lesion.

Practice Questions

Q1. A floppy newborn has proximal joint contractures with strikingly hyperextensible distal joints (fingers, ankles). Biopsy shows mismatched perlecan and collagen VI expression. Which condition is this?

Ullrich congenital muscular dystrophy, from collagen VI alpha gene mutations, classically produces this combination of proximal contractures with distal hyperextensibility and mismatched perlecan/collagen VI expression.

Q2. A floppy infant has seizures, intellectual disability, and blindness. Which mechanism best explains this presentation?

Because alpha-dystroglycan is important for CNS and eye development in addition to muscle, its disrupted post-translational modification produces the combination of a floppy infant with seizures, intellectual disability, and blindness.

Q3. Merosin deficiency congenital muscular dystrophy results from a mutation in which protein?

Merosin deficiency, as the name implies, results from mutation of merosin (laminin-alpha2), one of the extracellular matrix protein defects that produce a congenital muscular dystrophy.

Q4. Compared with an infant who has Ullrich congenital muscular dystrophy (a collagen VI defect), an infant with an alpha-dystroglycanopathy is more likely to additionally present with which finding?

Because alpha-dystroglycan is required for normal CNS and eye development (unlike the purely extracellular-matrix defects in Ullrich CMD/merosin deficiency), alpha-dystroglycanopathies characteristically add seizures, intellectual disability, and blindness to the floppy-infant picture.

Spinal Muscular Atrophy (SMA)

  • Description: autosomal recessive disorder causing loss of lower motor neurons (a neuronopathy); incidence 1 in 6,000 births
  • Forms: SMA type I (Werdnig-Hoffmann disease) — onset in the 1st year of life, death within 2 years; SMA type III (Kugelberg-Welander) — onset in later childhood/adolescence, milder course
  • Mechanism: related to the level of SMN protein, which is involved in spliceosome formation. The SMN1 gene produces the functional protein; the SMN2 gene modifies expression of SMN1
  • Mutations: all forms of SMA involve mutation of SMN1; the number of SMN2 copies a patient has determines disease severity (more SMN2 copies → milder disease)
  • Morphology: zones of atrophic myocytes admixed with normal or hypertrophied myocytes (a neurogenic, "group" pattern of atrophy rather than the endomysial fibrosis/fatty replacement seen in dystrophies)
Lower testing priority — don't memorize the exact incidence Dr. Kemp explicitly flagged that this SMA content overlaps with the CNS/neurodegenerative disease lecture, is included here mainly for completeness, and that there was no dedicated exam question on it. He also cautioned against memorizing exact incidence figures (sources vary — he cited both ~1:6,000 and ~1:60,000 depending on population) since these numbers shift by population and institution. Know the SMN1/SMN2 mechanism and the type I vs. type III distinction; don't stress over the precise incidence.

Practice Questions

Q1. A hypotonic infant undergoes muscle biopsy showing central, sharply demarcated zones with loss of sarcomeres and mitochondria within myofibers. This patient is also at increased risk for a life-threatening reaction to volatile anesthetics. Which gene/protein is implicated?

This is central core disease, caused by autosomal dominant mutations in ryanodine receptor-1 — the same gene implicated in malignant hyperthermia, explaining the anesthesia risk.

Q2. An infant develops profound hypotonia ("floppy baby") in the first months of life and dies within 2 years. Genetic testing shows a mutation in SMN1. Which of the following most influences how severe this patient's disease course will be?

In spinal muscular atrophy, all patients have an SMN1 mutation, but the number of SMN2 gene copies (which partially compensates for SMN1 loss) determines disease severity — more copies generally correlates with a milder phenotype.

Q3. Which congenital myopathy is characterized on biopsy by myonuclei positioned in the center of the muscle fiber rather than at the periphery?

Centronuclear myopathy (myotubularin, dynamin-2, or amphiphysin-2 mutations) is defined by nuclei located in the center of the myocyte, a distinctive finding on biopsy.

Q4. Nemaline rods seen on muscle biopsy represent aggregates of which structural material?

Nemaline rods are aggregates of Z-band material, seen in nemaline myopathy (alpha-tropomyosin 3, nebulin, or alpha-actin-1 mutations).

Q5. A newborn is hypotonic at birth. Over the following year, the weakness neither worsens nor improves, and biopsy shows selective atrophy of type 1 fibers. How does this natural history help distinguish the diagnosis from a muscular dystrophy?

Congenital myopathies, including congenital fiber type disproportion, are present at birth but non-progressive — the key feature separating this category from both classic muscular dystrophies (not present at birth, progressive) and congenital muscular dystrophies (present at birth, progressive).

Q1. A 6-year-old male with recurrent sinusitis is found to have a sinus mass. Genetic testing reveals a translocation producing FOXO1 fusion to PAX3. What is the most likely microscopic morphology of the mass?

The patient's age, sinus location, and FOXO1-PAX3 fusion are all classic for alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma, which shows pulmonary alveolar-like septation with septa lined by tumor cells (rare cross striations may be seen but are not the defining morphology).

Q2. A 3-year-old presents with a botryoid (grape-like) mass protruding from the vagina. Biopsy shows primitive cells, some with cross striations. Which rhabdomyosarcoma subtype is this, and what is its relative frequency among rhabdomyosarcomas?

The botryoid variant, young age, and primitive cells with occasional cross striations (rhabdomyoblasts) point to embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma, which is the most common subtype (about 60% of cases) and classically arises in the GU tract as well as the head/neck and sinuses.

Q3. An adult presents with a deep soft-tissue mass. Biopsy shows large, bizarre eosinophilic cells without a clear line of differentiation on H&E. Which immunohistochemical stain would help confirm a diagnosis of pleomorphic rhabdomyosarcoma?

Pleomorphic rhabdomyosarcoma occurs in adults and often looks morphologically nonspecific (bizarre eosinophilic cells); myogenin immunohistochemistry is used to confirm skeletal muscle origin.

Q4. Among the three major subtypes of rhabdomyosarcoma, which is the most common overall, and what is its approximate relative incidence?

Embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma is the most common subtype overall, accounting for roughly 60% of cases, compared to about 20% each for alveolar and pleomorphic.

SECTION 1

Neuromuscular Junction Diseases — Overview

Disorders of the neuromuscular junction (NMJ) disrupt the transfer of the electrical signal from the presynaptic motor neuron terminal to the postsynaptic muscle membrane. The three classic autoimmune/genetic disorders — myasthenia gravis, Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome, and congenital myasthenic syndromes — are distinguished largely by where the defect sits (presynaptic vs. postsynaptic) and how weakness behaves with repetitive use. Two bacterial toxins, from Clostridium tetani and Clostridium botulinum, act directly on NMJ physiology and produce opposite clinical pictures.

Framework The NMJ story is a game of "where's the antibody/toxin, and what does that do to acetylcholine?" Postsynaptic AChR loss (myasthenia gravis) → weakness worsens with use. Presynaptic calcium channel loss (Lambert-Eaton) → weakness improves with use. Get this rule down first; everything else hangs off it.
SECTION 2

Myasthenia Gravis

Mechanism

An autoimmune disease caused by autoantibodies directed against neuromuscular junction components — most commonly the postsynaptic acetylcholine receptor (AChR), but also against muscle-specific receptor tyrosine kinase (MuSK) and, less commonly, low-density lipoprotein-receptor related protein (LRP4). Dr. Kemp noted LRP4 is a minor/lower-yield detail — worth recognizing the name, but AChR and MuSK are the antibodies to know well.

Demographics

Affects about 2/100,000 people, with a bimodal age distribution:

  • Early onset (<50 years): F > M; associated with thymic hyperplasia/enlargement
  • Late onset: M = F; associated with thymoma

Symptoms

  • Ptosis (drooping eyelids) and diplopia (double vision) from extra-ocular muscle weakness — involvement of extra-ocular muscles is unusual in most other myopathies, making it a distinguishing clue
  • Variable expression: ranges from isolated extra-ocular involvement to weakness severe enough to require mechanical ventilation
  • If anti-MuSK antibodies are present: neck, shoulder, facial, respiratory, and bulbar muscles are more typically involved
  • Weakness fluctuates and characteristically worsens with repetitive/sustained use (classically worse at end of day, better after rest/sleep)
High-yield vignette pattern Ptosis + diplopia, worse as the day progresses, better after sleep → myasthenia gravis. In a patient <50, expect thymic hyperplasia on chest CT; in a patient with late-onset disease, expect a thymoma.

Practice Questions

Q1. A 40-year-old male presents with double vision and drooping eyelids that are worse toward the end of the day and better in the morning after sleep. Assuming an abnormality is present, a CT scan of the chest would most likely reveal which of the following?

This presentation (fatigable ptosis/diplopia, worse with use) is classic for myasthenia gravis. Early-onset disease (<50 years) is associated with thymic hyperplasia rather than a discrete mass, thymoma, or unrelated cardiopulmonary findings.

Q2. A 65-year-old woman develops progressive ptosis, dysphagia, and neck weakness. A chest CT reveals an anterior mediastinal mass. Which antibody target is most specifically associated with this later-onset presentation of myasthenia gravis?

Late-onset myasthenia gravis (M=F) is associated with thymoma, and the anterior mediastinal mass here reflects that. The antibodies are still typically against the postsynaptic AChR. Calcium channel antibodies point to Lambert-Eaton; ryanodine receptor to malignant hyperthermia; anti-Jo1 (histidyl t-RNA synthetase) to dermatomyositis/polymyositis.

Q3. In a patient with myasthenia gravis whose weakness prominently involves the neck, shoulder, facial, respiratory, and bulbar muscles rather than the classic ocular pattern, which antibody is most likely responsible?

Anti-MuSK antibody-associated myasthenia gravis tends to spare the classic ocular pattern and instead prominently affects neck, shoulder, facial, respiratory, and bulbar musculature.
SECTION 3

Lambert-Eaton Myasthenic Syndrome

Mechanism

Caused by autoantibodies against presynaptic voltage-gated calcium channels, which inhibits calcium influx and therefore the release of acetylcholine into the synaptic cleft.

Symptoms

  • Weakness most often affects the extremities (proximal limb-girdle pattern)
  • Weakness characteristically improves with repetitive use — repeated firing builds up presynaptic calcium, which enhances acetylcholine release
Key association Lambert-Eaton is most often a paraneoplastic syndrome. There is usually an underlying malignancy, classically small cell lung carcinoma. Always think "who has this — and do they have lung cancer?"

Practice Questions

Q1. A 68-year-old man with a 50-pack-year smoking history reports proximal leg weakness that is worst on first standing but improves somewhat as he continues walking. Workup reveals antibodies against presynaptic calcium channels. Which underlying condition should be most strongly suspected?

Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome is most often paraneoplastic, classically associated with small cell lung carcinoma — especially relevant given this patient's heavy smoking history.

Q2. Which finding on repetitive nerve stimulation testing would best distinguish Lambert-Eaton syndrome from myasthenia gravis?

In Lambert-Eaton, repeated stimulation increases presynaptic calcium accumulation and augments acetylcholine release, producing an incremental (facilitating) response — the opposite of the decremental response seen in myasthenia gravis.
SECTION 4

Congenital Myasthenic Syndromes & NMJ Infections

Congenital Myasthenic Syndrome

Caused by inherited mutations affecting neuromuscular junction proteins — the defect can be presynaptic, synaptic, or postsynaptic. Symptoms can mimic either myasthenia gravis or Lambert-Eaton syndrome, depending on which protein/location is affected.

Infections Affecting the Neuromuscular Junction

OrganismMechanismClinical result
Clostridium tetaniBlocks inhibitory neurons, leading to increased release of acetylcholineSustained muscle contraction (tetanus)
Clostridium botulinumInhibits acetylcholine releaseFlaccid paralysis
Opposite toxins, opposite results Tetanus toxin removes the brakes on the system (blocks inhibitory neurons) → too much ACh → rigid, sustained contraction. Botulinum toxin cuts the signal at the source (blocks ACh release) → flaccid paralysis. Contrast this with the antibody-mediated NMJ diseases above, which act at the receptor/channel level rather than on neurotransmitter release regulation.

Practice Questions

Q1. A patient with an infected puncture wound develops trismus (lockjaw) and generalized muscle rigidity. This is best explained by which mechanism?

Clostridium tetani toxin blocks inhibitory neurons, which increases acetylcholine release and produces sustained, rigid muscle contraction — unlike botulinum toxin, which inhibits ACh release and causes flaccid paralysis.

Q2. An infant develops constipation, poor feeding, a weak cry, and diffuse hypotonia ("floppy baby") after exposure to honey. Which mechanism is responsible?

Infant botulism from Clostridium botulinum spores (classically in honey) inhibits acetylcholine release at the NMJ, producing flaccid paralysis/hypotonia rather than the rigidity seen with tetanus.
SECTION 5

Skeletal Muscle Atrophy

Etiologies

Loss of innervation, older age, disuse, cachexia, and myopathies can all cause skeletal muscle atrophy.

Pattern-Based Clues

  • If atrophy is clustered/grouped → suggests a neurogenic origin
  • If atrophy is peri-fascicular (at the edges of fascicles) → suggests dermatomyositis
  • If atrophy selectively affects type II fibers with sparing of type I → suggests disuse or corticosteroid use

On cross-section, normal skeletal muscle fibers are mostly uniform in size. In atrophy of neurogenic origin, denervated fibers cluster together in groups ("grouped atrophy") because a single damaged motor neuron affects all the myocytes within its motor unit.

Practice Questions

Q1. A muscle biopsy shows clusters of small, angulated atrophic fibers grouped together among fascicles of normal-sized fibers. This "grouped atrophy" pattern is most consistent with which process?

Grouped atrophy reflects loss of innervation to an entire motor unit — because one motor neuron innervates many myocytes, denervation atrophies them together as a cluster, a hallmark of neurogenic atrophy.

Q2. A biopsy from a bedridden patient on long-term high-dose corticosteroids would most likely show selective atrophy of which fiber type?

Disuse and corticosteroid-associated atrophy selectively affects type II (fast-twitch) fibers while sparing type I fibers.
SECTION 6

Peripheral Neuropathy — Axonal vs. Demyelinating

Categories of Neuropathy

  • Inflammatory: unknown cause (Guillain-Barré, chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy/CIDP, sarcoidosis), infections (leprosy, AIDS, Lyme disease, varicella-zoster), vasculitis, SLE/RA
  • Associated with hematologic disease/neoplasm: paraneoplastic, lymphomas, monoclonal gammopathies
  • Metabolic/nutritional: diabetes mellitus, renal failure, thiamine or B12 deficiency
  • Toxic: alcohol, arsenic, lead, organophosphates
  • Traumatic
  • Hereditary: hereditary motor and sensory neuropathies (Charcot-Marie-Tooth), amyloid neuropathies, disorders of lipid metabolism (Refsum, Tangier disease)

Pathology of Axonal Degeneration

Early/acute: disruption of the myelin sheath; formation of "ovoids" (axonal fragments surrounded by disintegrating myelin, best seen by EM and teased-nerve preparations); phagocytosis of nerve fiber fragments by Schwann cells and macrophages. Axonal regeneration produces small, closely-packed myelinated fibers, guided by Schwann cells, with shorter and thinner myelin internodes.

Late/chronic: loss of myelinated axons; increased endoneurial connective tissue; clusters of regenerating nerve fibers.

Pathology of Acute Segmental Demyelination

Damage to the myelin sheath or Schwann cells causes loss of a myelin segment, usually adjacent to the node of Ranvier, producing thin myelin sheaths. With remyelination, the internodes are of unequal size (shorter and thinner). Repeated cycles of demyelination and remyelination lead to onion bulb formation — proliferation of Schwann cells and basement membrane around the axon.

Correction from lecture — muscle atrophy and neuropathy type While axonal injury can lead to skeletal muscle atrophy, demyelination most often does not. With neuronal (axonal) injury the muscle loses its stimulus entirely; with demyelination, the stimulus still reaches the muscle — it's just slower in arriving.

Practice Questions

Q1. A patient presents with symmetric numbness, tingling, and burning in the feet, along with loss of ankle reflexes, but relatively preserved motor strength. This presentation is most consistent with which pattern of neuropathy?

Axonal polyneuropathy classically presents with sensory symptoms out of proportion to motor symptoms, symmetric numbness/tingling/burning, and loss of ankle stretch reflexes.

Q2. Which finding would be expected in a nerve biopsy from a patient with a chronic demyelinating polyneuropathy who has undergone repeated cycles of demyelination and remyelination?

Onion bulbs are the classic finding of repeated segmental demyelination/remyelination cycles. Axonal ovoids are seen in early axonal degeneration; grouped atrophy is a muscle (not nerve) finding of denervation; peri-fascicular atrophy points to dermatomyositis.

Q3. Why does demyelinating neuropathy, unlike axonal injury, most often fail to produce significant skeletal muscle atrophy?

With axonal injury, the muscle loses its stimulus entirely. With demyelination, conduction is slowed but the stimulus still reaches the muscle, so significant denervation atrophy is less likely.

Practice Questions

Q1. A 23-year-old male spends 5 hours outside in cold weather after drinking at a party and is found the next morning with frostbite of the hands and feet. Laboratory testing is most likely to reveal which of the following?

Hypothermia predisposes to rhabdomyolysis. Of the choices, only elevated serum phosphorus fits the expected pattern. Rhabdomyolysis instead causes decreased serum calcium and increased serum potassium (unless hypokalemia was the underlying cause), and a BUN:creatinine ratio <10:1 rather than >40:1.

Q2. A patient with rhabdomyolysis has a CK of 8,200 IU/L. Which complication is this patient at greatest risk for?

CK >5,000 IU/L confers significant risk for acute kidney injury, the major complication of rhabdomyolysis (via myoglobin cast formation and tubular injury).
SECTION 8

Dermatomyositis

Terminology: Myopathy = any abnormal condition/disease of muscular tissue. Muscular dystrophy = an inborn abnormality of muscle associated with dysfunction and progressive deterioration.

Clinical Features

  • Proximal muscle weakness — slow onset, symmetric, with myalgias; distal involvement occurs late
  • Heliotrope rash: lilac discoloration of the upper eyelids with periorbital edema
  • Gottron papules: scaling erythematous rash on the extensor surfaces — knuckles, elbows, knees
  • Can affect the esophagus, lungs (interstitial lung disease), and heart (cardiac failure)

Pathogenesis

Autoimmune condition that damages blood vessels, leading to muscle injury, through activation of C5b-9 (the complement membrane attack complex).

Practice Questions

Q1. A 50-year-old woman presents with symmetric proximal muscle weakness, a lilac discoloration of her upper eyelids, and a scaling rash over her knuckles and elbows. Muscle biopsy would most likely show which pattern of atrophy?

This presentation (heliotrope rash, Gottron papules, proximal weakness) is classic for dermatomyositis, whose hallmark biopsy finding is peri-fascicular atrophy. Grouped atrophy suggests neurogenic disease; type II-selective atrophy suggests disuse/steroids; rimmed vacuoles suggest inclusion body myositis.

Q2. Which complement component is activated in the pathogenesis of dermatomyositis, damaging the intramuscular blood vessels?

Dermatomyositis pathogenesis centers on activation of C5b-9, the complement membrane attack complex, which damages the microvasculature and secondarily injures muscle.

Q3. A patient with dermatomyositis is found to have "mechanic's hands" and interstitial lung disease. Which autoantibody is most likely present?

Anti-Jo1 antibodies (against histidyl t-RNA synthetase) are associated with interstitial lung disease, arthritis, and "mechanic's hands."
SECTION 9

Polymyositis & Inclusion Body Myositis

Polymyositis

  • Presents with myalgia and weakness like dermatomyositis, but no skin involvement
  • Can involve the lungs and heart; has associated autoantibodies
  • Pathogenesis: mediated by CD8+ cytotoxic T cells (in contrast to the complement-mediated vasculopathy of dermatomyositis)
  • Key distinguishing point: no peri-fascicular atrophy, unlike dermatomyositis

Inclusion Body Myositis

  • The most common inflammatory myopathy in patients >65 years
  • Slowly progressive muscle weakness involving the quadriceps and distal upper extremities; can affect esophagus and pharyngeal muscles
  • No myositis-associated autoantibodies

Like polymyositis: endomysial inflammatory cells (CD8+) with focal invasion of myofibers; admixed degenerating and regenerating myofibers.

Unlike polymyositis: cytoplasmic inclusions ("rimmed vacuoles") containing TDP-43 (also seen in frontotemporal dementia), β-amyloid (also seen in Alzheimer disease), and ubiquitin; endomysial fibrosis and fatty replacement. Dr. Kemp described inclusion body myositis as essentially the muscle equivalent of a neurodegenerative process — the same proteinopathy machinery, just in myofibers instead of neurons.

Three inflammatory myopathies, one differentiator each Dermatomyositis = skin findings + peri-fascicular atrophy + complement-mediated. Polymyositis = same weakness pattern, no skin/no peri-fascicular atrophy, CD8+ T cell-mediated. Inclusion body myositis = older patient, distal + proximal (quadriceps) weakness, rimmed vacuoles, no autoantibodies.

Practice Questions

Q1. A 72-year-old man reports slowly progressive weakness affecting his quadriceps and the finger flexors of both hands over two years. He has no rash and no detectable myositis-specific autoantibodies. Biopsy is most likely to show which finding?

This age, distribution (quadriceps + distal upper extremity), and absence of autoantibodies point to inclusion body myositis, whose defining finding is rimmed vacuole cytoplasmic inclusions containing TDP-43, β-amyloid, and ubiquitin.

Q2. A patient has proximal muscle weakness and myalgias without any skin findings. Biopsy shows CD8+ endomysial inflammatory infiltrate with focal myofiber invasion, but no peri-fascicular atrophy. What is the most likely diagnosis?

Polymyositis shares the weakness pattern of dermatomyositis but lacks skin involvement and peri-fascicular atrophy; it is mediated by CD8+ cytotoxic T cells rather than complement-mediated vasculopathy.
SECTION 10

Toxic Myopathies

Etiology

Prescription or recreational drugs, or hormonal imbalances/disease processes.

  • Statins (atorvastatin, simvastatin, pravastatin) — associated myopathy; because statins can also cause liver injury, patients are often monitored with routine liver function tests in addition to watching for muscle weakness/pain
  • Chloroquine/hydroxychloroquine — interfere with lysosomal function, creating a lysosomal storage myopathy
  • ICU myopathy — weakness/myopathy developing in critically ill patients; the mechanism is complex and not fully understood, so this is more a "be aware it exists" entity than one with well-defined pathophysiology to memorize
  • Thyrotoxic myopathy — can show elevated creatine kinase
  • Alcohol — can cause rhabdomyolysis, myoglobinuria, and renal failure

Practice Questions

Q1. A patient on long-term hydroxychloroquine for lupus develops progressive proximal weakness. Biopsy is most likely to show which underlying mechanism?

Chloroquine/hydroxychloroquine interfere with lysosomal function, producing a lysosomal storage myopathy — distinct from the immune-mediated inflammatory myopathies.

Dystrophinopathies — Duchenne & Becker

  • Inheritance: X-linked. Duchenne = frameshift or point mutations; Becker = point mutations (in-frame, partially preserved protein)
  • Gene/protein: dystrophin, locus Xp21 — hence "dystrophinopathies"
  • Microscopic: myofiber necrosis and regeneration; muscle progressively replaced by adipose tissue and fibrosis; atrophic fibers surrounded by fibrous tissue; heart shows hypertrophy and fibrosis
  • Laboratory: creatine kinase markedly elevated early, then declines later in life as muscle mass is lost; genetic testing confirms the dystrophin mutation

Mechanism

The dystrophin-glycoprotein complex stabilizes the muscle cell membrane during contraction. Defects in this complex leave muscle cells vulnerable to membrane tears and disrupted intracellular signaling. Over time, degeneration outpaces the muscle's capacity for repair.

Clinical Features

  • Normal at birth
  • Weakness starts in the pelvis first (clumsiness, difficulty keeping up with peers), then progresses to the shoulders
  • Pseudohypertrophy of leg muscles (fat/fibrous replacement makes calves look enlarged)
  • Contractures, scoliosis, and worsening respiratory function develop
  • Dystrophin is also expressed in heart (→ cardiomyopathy) and brain (→ cognitive impairment)
FeatureDuchenneBecker
Incidence1:3,500 live birthsLess common
SeverityFatal by early adulthood (25–30 years)Less severe; near-normal life expectancy

Practice Questions

Q1. A 5-year-old boy has calves that appear enlarged but are weak, along with difficulty rising from the floor (climbing up his own legs to stand). Creatine kinase is markedly elevated. Biopsy would most likely show which of the following?

This vignette (pseudohypertrophic calves, Gower sign, elevated CK, young male) is classic for Duchenne muscular dystrophy, where biopsy shows myofiber necrosis/regeneration with progressive replacement by adipose and fibrous tissue.

Q2. Which of the following best distinguishes Becker from Duchenne muscular dystrophy at the molecular level?

Both are X-linked dystrophinopathies affecting the same gene (Xp21). Becker's point mutations tend to preserve a partially functional protein, producing a milder, later, near-normal-lifespan phenotype compared to Duchenne's more disruptive frameshift mutations.

Q3. The gene mutated in both Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophy is located at which chromosomal locus?

The dystrophin gene sits at Xp21, which is why Duchenne and Becker are grouped together as "dystrophinopathies."

Q4. Over the course of Duchenne muscular dystrophy, what is the typical trend in serum creatine kinase (CK)?

CK is markedly elevated early (reflecting ongoing myofiber necrosis) but declines later as functional muscle is replaced by fat and fibrosis, leaving less muscle tissue to release the enzyme.
SECTION 15

Other Muscular Dystrophies

Myotonic Dystrophy

  • Symptom: myotonia — sustained involuntary contraction (e.g., stiffness/inability to relax a grip)
  • Molecular: autosomal dominant; 95% have a trinucleotide (CTG) repeat expansion in the gene for dystrophia myotonica protein kinase (DMPK), ranging from >45 up to thousands of repeats
  • Mechanism: mutant DMPK mRNA sequesters muscleblind-like proteins involved in RNA splicing, causing abnormal gene expression — including a deficiency of the chloride channel CLC1
  • Demographics/features: manifests in late childhood with gait abnormalities (weak foot dorsiflexion), then hand/wrist involvement; can affect heart, eyes (cataracts), and endocrine system
  • Congenital myotonic dystrophy: patients with a CLC1 mutation specifically can present in infancy rather than late childhood — this is the entity referenced as "congenital myotonia" in the hypotonic-infant differential (Section 16)

Limb-Girdle Muscular Dystrophy

  • Symptoms: proximal musculature of trunk and limbs
  • Inheritance: heterogeneous — 7 autosomal dominant forms, 15 autosomal recessive forms; affects 1 in 25,000–50,000 people
  • Proteins involved: components of the dystrophin-glycoprotein complex, vesicle transport, membrane repair machinery, and the cytoskeleton

Emery-Dreifuss Muscular Dystrophy

  • Symptom triad: progressive muscle weakness/wasting; contractures of the elbows, ankles, and Achilles tendon (and spine); heart disease — 40% of patients die from arrhythmias
  • Inheritance: X-linked (protein: emerin) or autosomal dominant (protein: lamin A/C)
  • Proteins involved: structural proteins of the nuclear envelope
  • Mechanism: repetitive mechanical stress damages a structurally compromised nucleus

Fascioscapulohumeral Dystrophy

  • Symptoms: weakness of facial muscles and shoulder girdle (by age 20); weakness of lower trunk and foot dorsiflexors; normal life expectancy
  • Inheritance: autosomal dominant
  • Mechanism: overexpression of DUX4 (exact downstream mechanism unknown)
Lower testing priority Dr. Kemp specifically noted that because the downstream mechanism of DUX4 overexpression is unknown, FSHD is difficult to write detailed exam questions about — know the name/inheritance/clinical pattern, but don't over-invest here relative to Duchenne/Becker or Emery-Dreifuss.
High-yield vignette pattern Elbow/ankle/Achilles contractures + cardiac arrhythmia running in the family → Emery-Dreifuss. If inherited from an affected mother (autosomal dominant transmission), think lamin A/C; if X-linked pattern, think emerin.

Practice Questions

Q1. A 31-year-old male has slowly developed muscle weakness, contractures of his elbows and ankles, and contractures of his Achilles tendons. His mother had a similar disease process and died from a cardiac dysrhythmia. Mutation of the gene for which protein is responsible?

This is Emery-Dreifuss muscular dystrophy. Since the disease was transmitted from an affected mother (rather than an X-linked pattern), the autosomal dominant form is implicated, caused by mutations in lamin A/C rather than the X-linked protein emerin.

Q2. An adult patient has difficulty releasing his grip after a handshake, along with foot dorsiflexion weakness, cataracts, and mild endocrine abnormalities. Genetic testing shows a large CTG trinucleotide repeat expansion. Which protein's mRNA is most directly implicated?

This is myotonic dystrophy — grip myotonia, foot dorsiflexion weakness, cataracts, and endocrine involvement, with a CTG repeat expansion in DMPK whose mutant mRNA sequesters muscleblind-like splicing proteins.

Q3. A 22-year-old has progressive facial and shoulder-girdle weakness with a normal life expectancy and no cardiac involvement. What is the underlying molecular abnormality?

Facioscapulohumeral dystrophy presents with facial/shoulder-girdle weakness by age 20, autosomal dominant inheritance, normal life expectancy, and is linked to overexpression of DUX4.

Q4. A 35-year-old man has slowly progressive weakness of the trunk and proximal limb muscles, sparing the face and extraocular muscles. His father had a similar course. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

Proximal, trunk-and-limb weakness with autosomal dominant transmission across generations fits limb-girdle muscular dystrophy. Duchenne is X-linked (would not pass father-to-son) and myasthenia gravis/Kearns-Sayre have distinct extraocular and systemic features not described here.
SECTION 16

Congenital Muscular Dystrophies & the Hypotonic Infant

Defects in the Extracellular Matrix Surrounding Myofibers

  • Ullrich congenital muscular dystrophy (UCMD): mutation in collagen VI alpha genes; features hypotonia, proximal contractures, and distal hyperextensibility, with mismatched expression of perlecan and collagen VI
  • Merosin deficiency: mutation in merosin (laminin-alpha2)

Defects in Receptors for the Extracellular Matrix

  • Mechanism: disruption of post-translational modification of alpha-dystroglycan (severe mutations can cause fetal demise)
  • Pathogenesis: alpha-dystroglycan is important for CNS and eye development
  • Features: floppy infant with seizures, intellectual disability, and blindness

Differential Diagnosis of the Hypotonic Infant

A floppy/hypotonic infant's weakness can originate from several distinct levels of the neuromuscular axis:

  • Primary disease of skeletal muscle → congenital myasthenic syndrome, congenital myotonia (the CLC1-mutation, infancy-onset form of myotonic dystrophy discussed in Section 15), congenital myopathies (central core disease, nemaline myopathy, centronuclear myopathy, congenital fiber type disproportion), or congenital muscular dystrophies
  • Abnormalities of the brain
  • Neuronopathies → spinal muscular atrophy
Localize first Before reaching for a specific diagnosis, place the hypotonic infant along the axis: brain → anterior horn cell (neuronopathy/SMA) → NMJ → muscle itself. The clinical clues (seizures/cognitive findings vs. pure motor weakness vs. myotonia) point to the level of the lesion.

Practice Questions

Q1. A floppy newborn has proximal joint contractures with strikingly hyperextensible distal joints (fingers, ankles). Biopsy shows mismatched perlecan and collagen VI expression. Which condition is this?

Ullrich congenital muscular dystrophy, from collagen VI alpha gene mutations, classically produces this combination of proximal contractures with distal hyperextensibility and mismatched perlecan/collagen VI expression.

Q2. A floppy infant has seizures, intellectual disability, and blindness. Which mechanism best explains this presentation?

Because alpha-dystroglycan is important for CNS and eye development in addition to muscle, its disrupted post-translational modification produces the combination of a floppy infant with seizures, intellectual disability, and blindness.

Q3. Merosin deficiency congenital muscular dystrophy results from a mutation in which protein?

Merosin deficiency, as the name implies, results from mutation of merosin (laminin-alpha2), one of the extracellular matrix protein defects that produce a congenital muscular dystrophy.

Q4. Compared with an infant who has Ullrich congenital muscular dystrophy (a collagen VI defect), an infant with an alpha-dystroglycanopathy is more likely to additionally present with which finding?

Because alpha-dystroglycan is required for normal CNS and eye development (unlike the purely extracellular-matrix defects in Ullrich CMD/merosin deficiency), alpha-dystroglycanopathies characteristically add seizures, intellectual disability, and blindness to the floppy-infant picture.

Spinal Muscular Atrophy (SMA)

  • Description: autosomal recessive disorder causing loss of lower motor neurons (a neuronopathy); incidence 1 in 6,000 births
  • Forms: SMA type I (Werdnig-Hoffmann disease) — onset in the 1st year of life, death within 2 years; SMA type III (Kugelberg-Welander) — onset in later childhood/adolescence, milder course
  • Mechanism: related to the level of SMN protein, which is involved in spliceosome formation. The SMN1 gene produces the functional protein; the SMN2 gene modifies expression of SMN1
  • Mutations: all forms of SMA involve mutation of SMN1; the number of SMN2 copies a patient has determines disease severity (more SMN2 copies → milder disease)
  • Morphology: zones of atrophic myocytes admixed with normal or hypertrophied myocytes (a neurogenic, "group" pattern of atrophy rather than the endomysial fibrosis/fatty replacement seen in dystrophies)
Lower testing priority — don't memorize the exact incidence Dr. Kemp explicitly flagged that this SMA content overlaps with the CNS/neurodegenerative disease lecture, is included here mainly for completeness, and that there was no dedicated exam question on it. He also cautioned against memorizing exact incidence figures (sources vary — he cited both ~1:6,000 and ~1:60,000 depending on population) since these numbers shift by population and institution. Know the SMN1/SMN2 mechanism and the type I vs. type III distinction; don't stress over the precise incidence.

Practice Questions

Q1. A hypotonic infant undergoes muscle biopsy showing central, sharply demarcated zones with loss of sarcomeres and mitochondria within myofibers. This patient is also at increased risk for a life-threatening reaction to volatile anesthetics. Which gene/protein is implicated?

This is central core disease, caused by autosomal dominant mutations in ryanodine receptor-1 — the same gene implicated in malignant hyperthermia, explaining the anesthesia risk.

Q2. An infant develops profound hypotonia ("floppy baby") in the first months of life and dies within 2 years. Genetic testing shows a mutation in SMN1. Which of the following most influences how severe this patient's disease course will be?

In spinal muscular atrophy, all patients have an SMN1 mutation, but the number of SMN2 gene copies (which partially compensates for SMN1 loss) determines disease severity — more copies generally correlates with a milder phenotype.

Q3. Which congenital myopathy is characterized on biopsy by myonuclei positioned in the center of the muscle fiber rather than at the periphery?

Centronuclear myopathy (myotubularin, dynamin-2, or amphiphysin-2 mutations) is defined by nuclei located in the center of the myocyte, a distinctive finding on biopsy.

Q4. Nemaline rods seen on muscle biopsy represent aggregates of which structural material?

Nemaline rods are aggregates of Z-band material, seen in nemaline myopathy (alpha-tropomyosin 3, nebulin, or alpha-actin-1 mutations).

Q5. A newborn is hypotonic at birth. Over the following year, the weakness neither worsens nor improves, and biopsy shows selective atrophy of type 1 fibers. How does this natural history help distinguish the diagnosis from a muscular dystrophy?

Congenital myopathies, including congenital fiber type disproportion, are present at birth but non-progressive — the key feature separating this category from both classic muscular dystrophies (not present at birth, progressive) and congenital muscular dystrophies (present at birth, progressive).

Practice Questions

Q1. A 6-year-old male with recurrent sinusitis is found to have a sinus mass. Genetic testing reveals a translocation producing FOXO1 fusion to PAX3. What is the most likely microscopic morphology of the mass?

The patient's age, sinus location, and FOXO1-PAX3 fusion are all classic for alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma, which shows pulmonary alveolar-like septation with septa lined by tumor cells (rare cross striations may be seen but are not the defining morphology).

Q2. A 3-year-old presents with a botryoid (grape-like) mass protruding from the vagina. Biopsy shows primitive cells, some with cross striations. Which rhabdomyosarcoma subtype is this, and what is its relative frequency among rhabdomyosarcomas?

The botryoid variant, young age, and primitive cells with occasional cross striations (rhabdomyoblasts) point to embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma, which is the most common subtype (about 60% of cases) and classically arises in the GU tract as well as the head/neck and sinuses.

Q3. An adult presents with a deep soft-tissue mass. Biopsy shows large, bizarre eosinophilic cells without a clear line of differentiation on H&E. Which immunohistochemical stain would help confirm a diagnosis of pleomorphic rhabdomyosarcoma?

Pleomorphic rhabdomyosarcoma occurs in adults and often looks morphologically nonspecific (bizarre eosinophilic cells); myogenin immunohistochemistry is used to confirm skeletal muscle origin.

Q4. Among the three major subtypes of rhabdomyosarcoma, which is the most common overall, and what is its approximate relative incidence?

Embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma is the most common subtype overall, accounting for roughly 60% of cases, compared to about 20% each for alveolar and pleomorphic.
