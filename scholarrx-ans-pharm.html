1. Autonomic Receptors

The parasympathetic, sympathetic, and somatic divisions each wire up differently between the central nervous system and their target tissue, and the receptor at each stop along the way differs by division.

Autonomic & Somatic Efferent Pathways
DivisionPreganglionic fiberGanglionic synapsePostganglionic fiber / transmissionTarget tissueReceptor(s) at target
ParasympatheticLong fiber, from brainstem or sacral cordACh → nicotinic (NN) receptorShort fiber, releases AChSmooth muscle, gland cells, nerveM1, M2, M3
Sympathetic — standardShort fiber, from spinal cordACh → nicotinic (NN) receptor at sympathetic chain ganglionLong fiber, releases norepinephrine (or ACh onto sweat glands)Sweat glands / smooth muscle, gland cells, nerve / renal vasculature smooth muscleSweat glands: M3; other smooth muscle: α1, α2, β1, β2; renal vasculature: α1, α2, β1, D1

SomaticVoluntary motor nerve, direct from spinal cord—ACh released at the neuromuscular junctionSkeletal muscleNM
Both parasympathetic and standard sympathetic pathways relay through a ganglion using ACh acting on nicotinic receptors; the difference lies in the postganglionic transmitter (ACh for parasympathetic, mostly norepinephrine for sympathetic) and in the receptors expressed on the target tissue. The adrenal medulla is functionally a modified sympathetic ganglion that secretes catecholamines into the bloodstream rather than releasing them at a synapse. The somatic pathway to skeletal muscle has no ganglion at all.

2. Acetylcholine Receptors

Acetylcholine acts on two structurally and functionally distinct receptor families: nicotinic receptors, which are ligand-gated ion channels, and muscarinic receptors, which are G-protein-coupled receptors.

ACh Receptor Families
ReceptorStructure / signalingSubtypes & locations
NicotinicLigand-gated ion channel; opening allows K+ efflux and Na+ influx, and in some cases Ca2+ influxNN: autonomic ganglia and adrenal medulla  |  NM: neuromuscular junction of skeletal muscle
MuscarinicG-protein-coupled receptor; signals through second-messenger cascadesFive subtypes, M1–M5, found in heart, smooth muscle, brain, exocrine glands, and on sweat glands (sympathetic cholinergic innervation)
The nicotinic/muscarinic split maps onto ion channel versus GPCR signaling: nicotinic receptors produce fast, direct depolarization, while muscarinic receptors act more slowly through second messengers and account for the broad tissue distribution of parasympathetic and sympathetic-cholinergic effects.

3. Micturition Control

The micturition center in the pons coordinates bladder filling and emptying by balancing sympathetic and parasympathetic input, plus somatic control of the external sphincter.

  • Parasympathetic input (pelvic nerve) acts on M3 receptors on the detrusor muscle → contraction → promotes voiding.
  • Sympathetic input (hypogastric nerve) acts on β3 receptors on the detrusor (relaxation) and α1 receptors on the internal urethral sphincter (contraction) → promotes storage/retention.
  • Somatic input (pudendal nerve) acts on nicotinic receptors at the external urethral sphincter for voluntary control.

In short: sympathetic activity favors urinary retention, and parasympathetic activity favors voiding. A number of autonomic drugs act directly on these bladder receptors to treat urinary dysfunction.

Drug Class Acting on Bladder Receptors
Drug classMechanismApplications
Muscarinic agonists (e.g., bethanechol)M3 receptor activation → detrusor smooth muscle contraction → increased bladder emptyingUrinary retention
The bladder is a good example of reciprocal autonomic control: the same organ receives opposing sympathetic and parasympathetic input, with the pons acting as the switchboard that decides whether the bladder stores or empties.

The mnemonic shorthand many students use ("q hugs i in the middle, i inhibits, s stimulates") reflects a real pattern here: Gq receptors drive contraction/secretion, Gi receptors are broadly inhibitory, and Gs receptors activate adenylyl cyclase to raise cAMP. Notice β1 and β2 are both Gs-coupled — their differing effects come from which tissue expresses which subtype, not from different signaling machinery.

6. Autonomic Drugs (Synapse Mechanisms)

Cholinergic and noradrenergic synapses each have a dedicated synthesis, storage, release, and reuptake machinery, and this machinery is where many autonomic drugs (and drugs of abuse) act.

Cholinergic synapse

Choline and acetyl-CoA are combined by choline acetyltransferase (ChAT) to form acetylcholine, which is packaged into synaptic vesicles. Depolarization triggers Ca2+ influx, which drives vesicle fusion and ACh release into the synaptic cleft. Botulinum toxin blocks this Ca2+-dependent vesicle fusion step, preventing ACh release.

Noradrenergic synapse

Tyrosine is converted stepwise to DOPA, then dopamine, then norepinephrine within the presynaptic terminal. The vesicular monoamine transporter (VMAT) packages dopamine/norepinephrine into secretory vesicles; tetrabenazine blocks VMAT. Release of norepinephrine is modulated by presynaptic α2-autoreceptors, which provide negative feedback on further release. After release, norepinephrine is cleared from the synaptic cleft primarily by reuptake through the norepinephrine transporter (NET).

Amphetamines

Amphetamines enter the presynaptic terminal through the norepinephrine transporter (NET) and are then taken up into the secretory vesicles by VMAT, displacing stored norepinephrine into the cytoplasm. Once cytoplasmic norepinephrine concentration rises high enough, the direction of NET transport reverses, expelling norepinephrine into the synaptic cleft rather than clearing it — this reverse-transport mechanism accounts for the elevated synaptic norepinephrine seen with amphetamine use.

Several major drug targets sit directly in this pathway: ChAT and vesicular ACh packaging (cholinergic side), and tyrosine hydroxylase, VMAT, presynaptic α2-autoreceptors, and NET (noradrenergic side). Recognizing which step a drug acts on (synthesis, packaging, release, reuptake, or autoreceptor feedback) is the fastest way to predict its net effect on synaptic transmitter levels.

7. Cholinomimetic Agents

Cholinomimetics either act directly on cholinergic receptors or indirectly by inhibiting acetylcholinesterase (AChE) to raise synaptic ACh levels. As a class, they warrant caution in patients with COPD, asthma, or peptic ulcer disease, since increased cholinergic tone can worsen bronchoconstriction and acid secretion.

8. Anticholinesterase Poisoning

Anticholinesterase poisoning most often results from organophosphate exposure (e.g., fenthion, parathion, malathion), compounds that irreversibly inhibit acetylcholinesterase. Organophosphates are common insecticide ingredients, so this poisoning is classically seen in agricultural workers.

Clinical Effects & Reversal
CategoryFindings / mechanismManagement
Muscarinic effectsDiarrhea, urination, miosis, bronchospasm/bronchorrhea, bradycardia, emesis, lacrimation, sweating, salivation — the classic muscarinic excess pictureReversed by atropine, a competitive muscarinic antagonist; atropine crosses the blood-brain barrier and also relieves CNS symptoms
Nicotinic effectsNeuromuscular blockade, mechanistically similar to that seen with succinylcholineReversed by pralidoxime, which regenerates AChE via dephosphorylation if given early; must be co-administered with atropine, since pralidoxime alone can transiently worsen muscarinic symptoms; pralidoxime does not readily cross the blood-brain barrier
CNS effectsRespiratory depression, lethargy, seizures, comaAddressed by the combination of atropine (crosses BBB) and supportive care; pralidoxime has minimal CNS effect
The two antidotes have complementary jobs: atropine blocks the muscarinic (and CNS) consequences of excess ACh, while pralidoxime attacks the underlying enzyme problem to relieve nicotinic neuromuscular blockade — which is why both are given together rather than either alone.

Grouping by organ system, rather than by drug name, is usually the faster way to recall which agent fits which clinical scenario.

10. Atropine

Atropine is a prototypical muscarinic antagonist, used clinically to treat bradycardia and for various ophthalmic indications, and its adverse-effect profile follows directly from blocking muscarinic tone throughout the body.

Atropine: Organ Effects
Organ systemActionNotes
EyeIncreased pupil dilation, cycloplegiaBlocks the muscarinic effects of anticholinesterase poisoning, but not the nicotinic effects
AirwayBronchodilation, decreased secretions
HeartIncreased heart rate
StomachDecreased acid secretion
GutDecreased motility
BladderDecreased urgency in cystitis

Adverse effects

  • Increased body temperature (due to decreased sweating), increased heart rate, dry mouth, dry and flushed skin, cycloplegia, constipation, disorientation
  • Can precipitate acute angle-closure glaucoma in older adults (due to mydriasis), urinary retention in men with prostatic hyperplasia, and hyperthermia in infants
  • Jimson weed (Datura) ingestion produces marked mydriasis, sometimes called a "gardener's pupil"
Atropine's adverse effects are simply an extension of its mechanism: block muscarinic receptors everywhere, and you get dry secretions, tachycardia, urinary retention, constipation, mydriasis, and impaired sweating with resulting hyperthermia — particularly dangerous at the extremes of age.

Direct sympathomimetics act on adrenergic and/or dopamine receptors with varying selectivity, and that selectivity profile predicts both their hemodynamic effects and their clinical use.

Receptor selectivity is the organizing principle: predominantly β2 agents relax airway and uterine smooth muscle; predominantly β1 agents boost cardiac output for shock and stress testing; dopamine's effect shifts from D1/D2 at low dose toward α-mediated vasoconstriction at high dose; and epinephrine's mixed α/β profile makes it the default choice in anaphylaxis, where both bronchodilation and vasoconstriction are needed.

12. Physiologic Effects of Sympathomimetics

The net hemodynamic effect of a sympathomimetic depends on its relative α versus β activity, and reflex autonomic responses can partially offset the drug's direct effect.

Norepinephrine (α > β) raises both systolic and diastolic blood pressure through α1-mediated vasoconstriction. The resulting rise in mean arterial pressure triggers a baroreceptor-mediated reflex bradycardia. Isoproterenol, in contrast, has little α activity but produces β2-mediated vasodilation; this lowers mean arterial pressure and produces a reflex (and direct β1) increase in heart rate.

Comparative Hemodynamic Profile
DrugBlood pressure patternHeart rate patternPeripheral (vascular) resistance
Norepinephrine (α > β)Increased systolic and diastolic pressure; widened pulse pressure from unopposed α1 vasoconstrictionReflex bradycardia (baroreceptor response to elevated pressure)Increased (unopposed α1 vasoconstriction)
Epinephrine (β > α)Increased systolic pressure; diastolic pressure changes little or falls slightly, since β2 vasodilation partially offsets α1 effectsIncreased (direct β1 stimulation)Initially decreased at β2-predominant doses; increased at high, α1-predominant doses
Isoproterenol (β1 ≈ β2)Decreased blood pressure overall (vasodilation predominates, negligible α activity)Increased (direct β1 effect plus reflex tachycardia)Decreased (unopposed β2 vasodilation)
This is a good test of whether you actually understand receptor selectivity rather than just memorizing drug names: predict the direction of the blood-pressure and heart-rate change from the α:β ratio alone, then check whether a reflex response (bradycardia if pressure rises, tachycardia if pressure falls) would blunt or exaggerate the direct effect.

13. Sympatholytics (α2-Agonists)

Centrally acting α2-agonists reduce sympathetic outflow from the CNS, which explains both their blood-pressure-lowering effect and their sedating/CNS-depressant side-effect profile.

DrugApplicationsAdverse effects

These three agents share a mechanism (central α2 agonism → decreased sympathetic outflow) but have quite different niche indications — opioid withdrawal and behavioral disorders for clonidine/guanfacine, pregnancy-safe antihypertension for methyldopa, and spasticity for tizanidine — and abrupt clonidine discontinuation is a classic cause of rebound hypertension.

15. ß-Blockers

Beta-blockers (atenolol, betaxolol, bisoprolol, carvedilol, esmolol, labetalol, metoprolol, nadolol, nebivolol, propranolol, timolol) share a core mechanism — blunting β-adrenergic drive — that gets applied across a strikingly wide range of clinical problems.

Beta-blockade is a single mechanism with strikingly broad reach: the same blunting of sympathetic drive that helps angina and hypertension also lowers portal pressure in cirrhosis, reduces tremor in thyroid storm, and even helps prevent migraines — a good reminder that a receptor's tissue distribution (heart, kidney, liver vasculature, ciliary body) is what turns one mechanism into many indications.
