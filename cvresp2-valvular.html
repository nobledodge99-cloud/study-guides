1. Definitions & General Auscultation Principles

Core Definitions

TermDefinition
AfterloadThe tension in the LV wall developed during systole — the load against which the heart beats. Typically reflects systemic vascular resistance, but may be fixed in the setting of aortic or mitral stenosis (the stenotic valve itself becomes the obstruction, independent of vascular tone).
PreloadThe volume of the right or left ventricle at end-diastole — the stretch of the myocardium from ventricular filling. Reflects volume status: high in volume overload (e.g., CHF), low when dehydrated/hypovolemic.

Epidemiology of Valvular Heart Disease

  • Ranks well below coronary heart disease, stroke, hypertension, obesity, and diabetes as a public health threat overall
  • Rheumatic fever is the dominant cause of valvular heart disease in low- and middle-income countries
  • RHD prevalence ranges from ~1/100,000 school-age children (Costa Rica) to ~150/100,000 (China)
  • Accounts for 12–65% of cardiovascular hospital admissions and 2–10% of hospital discharges in some endemic countries
  • Disease progresses more rapidly in economically deprived, tropical/subtropical regions (Indian subcontinent, Southeast Asia, Central America, Middle East) — frequently causing serious symptoms in patients <20 years old, attributed to repeated infection with more virulent rheumatogenic streptococcal strains
  • ~30–35 million people live with rheumatic heart disease worldwide
High-Income Countries Valve disease here is instead dominated by degenerative/inflammatory processes causing valve thickening, fibrosis, calcification, and dysfunction — prevalence rises significantly with age.

Universal Murmur Maneuver Principles

Respiratory cycle: Right-sided murmurs increase with inhalation (increased venous return to the right heart); left-sided murmurs increase with exhalation.

Maneuvers that increase venous return (↑preload): squatting, passive leg raise — generally increase most left- and right-sided murmurs (except HCM, which decreases).

Maneuvers that decrease venous return (↓preload): standing, Valsalva — generally decrease most murmurs (except HCM, which increases).
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Why is afterload considered 'fixed' in a patient with severe aortic stenosis, rather than variable with systemic vascular resistance?

Explanation: In aortic or mitral stenosis, the stenotic valve itself becomes a fixed mechanical obstruction that the ventricle must pump against, making afterload relatively fixed rather than variable with changes in systemic vascular resistance.

2. In general, how do right-sided and left-sided heart murmurs respond to the respiratory cycle?

Explanation: Right-sided murmurs increase with inhalation due to increased venous return to the right heart, while left-sided murmurs increase with exhalation -- a general principle useful for lateralizing an unclear murmur.

3. In high-income countries, valvular heart disease is most commonly driven by which process, in contrast to low- and middle-income countries where rheumatic fever dominates?

Explanation: In high-income countries, valvular heart disease is dominated by degenerative and inflammatory processes leading to valve thickening, fibrosis, and calcification, with prevalence increasing significantly with age, in contrast to the rheumatic fever-driven disease seen in lower-income, endemic regions.

2. Aortic Stenosis: Etiology & Pathophysiology

Aortic stenosis (AS) is the most common valve lesion among adults with chronic valvular heart disease and the most common cause of LV outflow obstruction in adults. Most symptomatic patients are male.

Three Common Etiologies

EtiologyNotes
Calcific disease of a trileaflet (normal) aortic valveMost prevalent cause in North America and Europe; is the most common surgical valve repair indication in developed countries; many elderly patients; shares risk factors with atherosclerosis (hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, smoking) — but atherosclerosis therapies do not slow AS progression once mild disease is present
Superimposed calcification of a congenitally abnormal (bicuspid) valveSee below
Sequela of rheumatic feverMost common cause worldwide

Calcific AS — Mechanism

A normal trileaflet or congenitally bicuspid valve undergoes gradual lipid accumulation, inflammation, and calcification (aortic sclerosis) → leaflets become thick and stiff and fail to open fully during systole → disease accelerates as mechanical stress/shear force wears on the valve. NOTCH1 gene intercellular signaling has been implicated in mediating this process.

Bicuspid Aortic Valve — Key Facts

FactDetail
NatureCongenital
PresentationCan present in adolescence, but more common age 50–65 after years of calcification/degeneration
Aortic dilationDilated ascending aorta in ~1/2 of patients
Associated anomalyCoarctation of the aorta can co-occur
Heritability~30% of offspring have disease in the aorta, valve, or both
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Which etiology of aortic stenosis is the most prevalent in North America and Europe, versus the most common cause worldwide?

Explanation: Calcific disease of a trileaflet valve is the most prevalent cause of AS in North America and Europe, while rheumatic fever remains the most common cause of AS worldwide, reflecting differing disease burdens between high-income and lower-income regions.

2. A patient with a known bicuspid aortic valve is being counseled about associated risks. Which of the following should be included in this discussion?

Explanation: Bicuspid aortic valve carries a heritable component (~30% of offspring affected), is associated with dilated ascending aorta in about half of patients, and can co-occur with coarctation of the aorta -- all important points for patient counseling.

3. Which gene's intercellular signaling has been implicated in mediating the progression of calcific aortic valve disease?

Explanation: NOTCH1 gene signaling, which mediates communication between adjacent cells in tissue morphogenesis and homeostasis, has been implicated in the progression of calcific aortic valve disease once mild disease is present.

3. Aortic Stenosis: Murmur, Signs & Mimics

The Murmur

FeatureDetail
QualityHarsh, crescendo-decrescendo systolic ejection murmur; may begin with an ejection click
LocationRight upper sternal border (2nd ICS)
RadiationTo the carotids
As stenosis worsensPeak of murmur moves closer to S2, eventually softening/obliterating S2
Gallavardin Phenomenon Transmission of the murmur to the apex, where it becomes higher-pitched and more musical/acoustically pure — can mimic mitral regurgitation, but (unlike true MR) does not radiate to the axilla.

Distinguishing AS From Its Mimics

ConditionKey Distinguishing Feature
Aortic sclerosisShares a similar murmur quality but does not radiate to the carotids and does not obliterate S2
Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM)Murmur may be indistinguishable by sound alone — use maneuvers: ↓preload (standing from sitting) → HCM murmur increases; ↑preload (Valsalva, leg raise) → HCM murmur decreases. Neither maneuver affects the AS murmur.

Physical Exam Signs

SignDetail
Pulsus parvus et tardusParvus = smaller; Tardus = late — a smaller, delayed carotid upstroke relative to the heart sounds
Apical impulseSustained (longer contraction needed to empty the hypertrophied LV), but its location remains unmoved
Pulse pressureDecreased
S4Can be present (reflects a stiff, hypertrophied LV and atrial contraction against it)

Severe AS — Additional Findings

  • Palpable LV heave or thrill
  • Weak or absent aortic component of S2 (A2)
  • Reversed/paradoxical S2 splitting: normally A2 precedes P2; in severe AS, the stiff/thickened valve delays aortic closure so much that A2 now follows P2. A2 remains fixed in this delayed position, while P2 still varies normally with respiration.
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A murmur initially heard as a harsh crescendo-decrescendo systolic murmur at the right upper sternal border is also heard at the apex, where it sounds higher-pitched and more musical, without radiating to the axilla. What is this phenomenon called?

Explanation: The Gallavardin phenomenon describes transmission of the aortic stenosis murmur to the apex, where it becomes higher-pitched and musical, potentially mimicking mitral regurgitation -- but true MR would radiate to the axilla, which this does not.

2. A patient's murmur is suspected to be either aortic stenosis or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Which maneuver-based finding would suggest HCM rather than AS?

Explanation: The HCM murmur characteristically increases with maneuvers that decrease preload, such as standing from a sitting position (less blood return, exacerbating the dynamic outflow obstruction), whereas the AS murmur is unaffected by this or Valsalva/leg-raise maneuvers.

3. In severe aortic stenosis, why does 'reversed' or paradoxical splitting of S2 occur?

Explanation: In severe AS, the markedly increased pressure and delayed emptying of the left ventricle delays aortic valve closure so significantly that A2 now occurs after P2 (reversed from normal), with A2 remaining fixed in this delayed position while P2 continues to vary normally with respiration.

4. Aortic Stenosis: Symptoms, Diagnosis & Treatment

Pathophysiology → Symptoms

Progressive afterload → concentric LV hypertrophy → decreased compliance and difficulty supplying blood through the thickened muscle (decreased O2 perfusion, despite increased O2 demand to pump against higher pressures) → can progress to myocardial dysfunction (a form of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy). Cardiac output is maintained until stenosis is severe.

Increased LA pressure → LA enlargement → atrial fibrillation (loss of the atrial kick, which is especially poorly tolerated when LV filling already depends on it) → pressure transmitted back to lungs and right heart.
Classic Symptom TriadMechanism
AnginaThickened myocardium outgrows capillary supply + increased O2 demand
SyncopeFixed obstruction prevents CO from rising during exercise; peripheral vasodilation during exercise + fixed CO → BP falls → decreased cerebral perfusion
DyspneaDiastolic dysfunction/heart failure from chronic pressure overload
Sudden death can also occur, particularly once symptoms have developed.

Diagnosis

ModalityFindings
EKGLVH, secondary repolarization changes; normal in ~10% of patients
Chest X-rayNormal or enlarged cardiac silhouette; calcification of the aortic valve; dilation/calcification of the ascending aorta
EchocardiogramAortic valve calcification, valve opening/area, severity of LVH, LV function
Cardiac catheterizationCoronary angiography and hemodynamics (often to assess for concurrent CAD before surgery)

Surgical Intervention — Full Criteria

Symptomatic severe AS — always an indication.
Asymptomatic severe AS (valve area <1.0 cm²) — surgery indicated if any of:
  • Undergoing other cardiac surgery (e.g., CABG)
  • LVEF <50%
  • Mean gradient exceeds 55 mmHg
  • Blood pressure fails to rise >20 mmHg with exercise
  • Severe valvular calcium
  • Rapid increase in peak aortic gradient (>0.3 m/sec/year)
  • Symptoms provoked by exercise/stress testing in a previously asymptomatic patient
Prognosis Without Treatment Prognosis is poor after the onset of heart failure, angina, or syncope — 50% mortality within 3 years of symptom onset.

Balloon Aortic Valvuloplasty

May be considered in patients with severe AS who are not candidates for surgical intervention. Its capacity to help is limited — aortic valve replacement remains the standard of care for severe AS.

Aortic Valve Replacement Options

Valve TypeKey Fact
Mechanical valveRequires lifelong warfarin (INR 2–3) + low-dose aspirin
Bioprosthetic (bovine/porcine)Life expectancy of the valve: 10–15 years; preferred for patients >60 years old (only needs anticoagulation ~3 months post-op)
TAVR (transcatheter AVR)Indicated for patients with intermediate or high surgical risk; minimally invasive; a very commonly used option today for patients needing AVR
Medical Management Caveats There is no effective medical therapy to reverse AS. Concomitant hypertension should be treated cautiously (e.g., ACE inhibitors, carefully titrated) — aggressively lowering BP in AS is poorly tolerated since cardiac output is relatively fixed, and can precipitate syncope. In AFib, beta-blockers help with rate control since rapid ventricular rates worsen diastolic filling.
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A patient with severe aortic stenosis undergoes exercise stress testing. Which finding on this test would be an indication for surgery even if the patient is otherwise asymptomatic?

Explanation: Failure of blood pressure to rise more than 20 mmHg with exercise is one of the specific criteria indicating surgery in an asymptomatic patient with severe aortic stenosis, reflecting an inadequate hemodynamic response to increased demand.

2. What is the approximate 3-year mortality rate for a patient with severe aortic stenosis once symptoms of heart failure, angina, or syncope have developed, if left untreated?

Explanation: Once symptoms (heart failure, angina, or syncope) develop in severe aortic stenosis, prognosis is poor, with approximately 50% mortality within 3 years if the valve is not replaced.

3. A 45-year-old patient requires aortic valve replacement and wishes to avoid lifelong anticoagulation. Which valve type would best meet this preference, and what is its main trade-off?

Explanation: A bioprosthetic (bovine or porcine) valve only requires anticoagulation for about 3 months after surgery, avoiding the lifelong warfarin needed with a mechanical valve, but trades this off against a shorter valve life expectancy of 10-15 years, generally making it preferred for older patients (>60) rather than younger ones who would likely outlive the valve.

5. Aortic Regurgitation: Etiology & Pathophysiology

In aortic regurgitation (AR), the aortic valve fails to close properly, so blood flows backward from the aorta into the LV during diastole. Initially, the dilated LV compensates well; eventually the heart becomes deconditioned and weak.

Etiology

CategoryCauses
Leaflet dysfunction (leading causes)Bicuspid aortic valve, rheumatic heart disease (thickening, deformity, shortening of cusps), infective endocarditis (perforation/erosion of leaflets, whether the valve was previously normal, rheumatic, or congenitally deformed)
Other leaflet causesMembranous subaortic stenosis (high-velocity jet causes secondary leaflet thickening/scarring); cusp prolapse; traumatic rupture/avulsion of a cusp (uncommon)
Aortic root disease (leading cause)Longstanding hypertension
Bicuspid Valve & AR A patient born with a congenital bicuspid aortic valve may develop predominantly AR (rather than AS); about 20% of these patients will require aortic valve surgery between ages 10 and 40.

Pathophysiology

Chronic AR → increased LV pressure → progressive eccentric LV hypertrophy and increased LV end-diastolic volume (chronic volume overload stimulates myocyte remodeling). Simultaneously: decreased diastolic pressure and increased systolic pressure → increased pulse pressure.
The Aorta as a "Failed Pump" Normally, the aorta acts like an elastic reservoir — it dilates as blood leaves the LV during systole, then its wall elasticity causes it to "recoil" and help propel blood forward once the aortic valve closes. In AR, this normal recoil process fails because the incompetent valve lets blood leak backward instead of being propelled forward — contributing to the widened pulse pressure and inefficient forward flow.
  • The LV can compensate (via dilatation, maintaining forward output without a large diastolic pressure rise) for some time — but eventually ventricular enlargement and interstitial fibrosis lead to both systolic and diastolic dysfunction
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. What is the leading cause of aortic root disease resulting in aortic regurgitation?

Explanation: Longstanding hypertension is the leading cause of aortic root disease (as opposed to primary leaflet dysfunction) resulting in aortic regurgitation, distinct from bicuspid valve, rheumatic disease, and endocarditis, which are the leading causes of leaflet dysfunction.

2. Approximately what percentage of patients with a congenital bicuspid aortic valve who develop predominant aortic regurgitation will require valve surgery between ages 10 and 40?

Explanation: Approximately 20% of patients with a bicuspid aortic valve who develop predominant aortic regurgitation will require aortic valve surgery somewhere between ages 10 and 40.

3. How does the normal 'pump' function of the aorta fail in chronic aortic regurgitation?

Explanation: The aorta normally functions somewhat like an elastic pump: it dilates as blood is ejected during systole, then its elastic recoil helps propel blood forward once the valve closes. In AR, the incompetent aortic valve allows blood to leak backward into the LV instead, disrupting this normal forward-propelling mechanism and contributing to the widened pulse pressure.

The Murmur

A high-pitched, blowing, decrescendo, early diastolic murmur, heard best at the apex (radiating to the axilla) or along the left sternal border (3rd–4th ICS) — best heard with the patient leaning forward.
Board Tip Severity of AR correlates with the duration of the murmur, not its intensity.

Named/Eponymous Findings of Chronic Severe AR (Widened Pulse Pressure)

EponymFinding
Corrigan pulse ("water-hammer" pulse)Rapidly rising, forceful pulse that collapses suddenly as arterial pressure falls rapidly in late systole
de Musset signHead bobbing with each heartbeat
Quincke's pulseVisible capillary pulsation (flushing/paling) at the nail bed root when light pressure is applied to the nail tip
Traube's sign ("pistol shot" sound)A booming, pistol-shot sound heard over the femoral arteries
Duroziez's signA "to-and-fro" murmur heard over the femoral artery with light stethoscope compression

Austin Flint Murmur — Detail

A lower-pitched, mid-to-late diastolic murmur at the apex, thought to reflect turbulence at the mitral inflow area from mixing of regurgitant aortic blood with forward-flowing mitral blood.
Distinguishing From Mitral Stenosis Unlike the murmur of mitral stenosis, the Austin Flint murmur has no opening snap, and it will decrease in duration/intensity (become shorter and softer) in response to a vasodilator challenge (e.g., a nitrate) — lowering afterload reduces the aortic-to-LV diastolic pressure gradient driving the murmur.
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A patient with chronic severe aortic regurgitation is found to have visible flushing and paling of the nail bed with light pressure applied to the nail tip. What is this eponymous finding called?

Explanation: Quincke's pulse describes the visible capillary pulsation (alternating flushing and paling) seen at the nail bed root when light pressure is applied to the nail tip, a classic finding in chronic severe aortic regurgitation from the widened pulse pressure.

2. How can the Austin Flint murmur be distinguished from the murmur of mitral stenosis, and how does it respond to a vasodilator challenge?

Explanation: The Austin Flint murmur lacks the opening snap characteristic of mitral stenosis, and because it results from the aortic regurgitant jet interacting with mitral inflow, lowering afterload with a vasodilator (nitrate) reduces the aortic-to-LV diastolic pressure gradient, making the murmur shorter and softer -- a response mitral stenosis does not share.

3. What determines the clinical severity of aortic regurgitation based on the murmur characteristics, according to board-level teaching?

Explanation: The severity of aortic regurgitation correlates with the duration of the diastolic murmur, not its intensity -- a longer murmur indicates a more prolonged pressure gradient and generally more severe regurgitation.

7. Aortic Regurgitation: Symptoms, Diagnosis & Treatment

Symptoms

SymptomMechanism
Exertional dyspnea, fatigueProgressive LV dysfunction
PND, pulmonary edemaBackward failure once LV dysfunction develops
Angina/atypical chest painCoronary perfusion is driven by diastolic blood pressure, which is low in AR
Presyncope/syncopeLow diastolic pressure → poor cerebral perfusion
CHF symptomsProgressive LV failure

Diagnosis

ModalityFindings
EKGLVH
Chest X-rayCardiomegaly with LV prominence; dilated aorta on occasion
EchocardiographyMainstay for severity, mechanism, LV function, and aortic root anatomy

Treatment — Surgical Indications

Symptomatic severe AR — always an indication.
Asymptomatic severe AR — surgery indicated if any of:
  • LVEF <50%
  • Normal LVEF but LV end-systolic dimension (LVESD) >50 mm
  • Normal LVEF but LV end-diastolic dimension (LVEDD) >65 mm
  • Undergoing other heart surgery
Don't Confuse With MR Criteria These AR thresholds (LVEF <50%, LVESD >50 mm, LVEDD >65 mm) are numerically different from the mitral regurgitation surgical criteria (Section 11: LVEF <60%, LVESD >40 mm) — a common source of exam confusion since both are "asymptomatic + LV dimension/function" criteria.

Medical Therapy (When Not an Operative Candidate)

SituationTherapy
Severe AR, not an operative candidateVasodilator therapy — ACE inhibitors, hydralazine, calcium channel blockers
Significant aortic root dilatationBeta-blockers and/or ARB — especially with Marfan syndrome

Surgical Options

  • AV repair: separation of fused cusps, reshaping/removing excess tissue so cusps close tightly, patching a hole in a cusp
  • AV replacement: mechanical or bioprosthetic (bovine, porcine, or human tissue) valve
  • TAVR (transcatheter AVR): minimally invasive; common option for patients needing AVR
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Why does chronic severe aortic regurgitation predispose to angina, even in the absence of coronary artery disease?

Explanation: Coronary artery perfusion occurs predominantly during diastole and is driven by diastolic blood pressure. Since AR characteristically lowers diastolic pressure, coronary perfusion can become inadequate even without underlying coronary artery disease, producing angina.

2. An asymptomatic patient with severe aortic regurgitation has a normal LVEF but an LV end-diastolic dimension of 68 mm. Based on surgical criteria, what is the appropriate next step?

Explanation: Even with a normal LVEF, an asymptomatic patient with severe AR and an LV end-diastolic dimension greater than 65 mm meets surgical criteria, since this indicates significant ventricular remodeling from chronic volume overload.

3. A patient with aortic regurgitation and significant aortic root dilatation is found to also have Marfan syndrome. Which medical therapy is specifically favored in this scenario?

Explanation: In patients with AR and significant aortic root dilatation, beta-blockers and/or ARBs are favored, and this combination is specifically emphasized in patients with underlying Marfan syndrome, given the added benefit for aortic root protection in this population.

8. Mitral Stenosis: Etiology, Symptoms & Murmur

Etiology

CauseFrequency/Detail
(Untreated) rheumatic heart diseaseMost common etiology — accounts for ~1/3 of MS cases; typically seen in the 3rd–4th decade of life; thick leaflets, fused commissures, retracted/thickened/fused chordae, calcium deposition
Congenital mitral stenosis—
Elderly patientsCalcified valves
Two Different Statistics — Don't Confuse Them "Pure/predominant MS occurs in ~40% of patients with a history of rheumatic heart disease" (i.e., of people who had RHD, 40% develop MS) is a different statistic from "rheumatic disease accounts for ~1/3 of MS cases" (i.e., of people with MS, 1/3 got it from RHD). Both facts can be true simultaneously since they answer different questions.

Natural History

Symptom onset generally begins in the 2nd decade of life; most patients experience some disability by the 4th decade. Once seriously symptomatic, disease progresses toward death within 2–5 years without treatment.

Symptoms by Severity

SeverityFindings
Mild-moderateLA pressure and CO can be normal; symptomatic only with extreme exertion, or asymptomatic
SevereLeft heart failure symptoms (dyspnea, orthopnea, PND) — pulmonary edema is uncommon; atrial fibrillation (thrombus risk); pulmonary hypertension; right heart failure develops over time; can result in hemoptysis (from rupture of pulmonary bronchial venous connections due to pulmonary venous hypertension)
Progressive/Precipitating Factors As MS progresses, lesser and lesser degrees of stress (tachycardia, thyrotoxicosis, pregnancy) can precipitate dyspnea. New-onset persistent atrial fibrillation is often a key turning point, accelerating symptom progression.

The Murmur

FeatureDetail
QualityLow-pitched, mid-diastolic, rumbling — best heard with the bell
Location/positionBest at the apex, in the left lateral decubitus position
AugmentationIncreases with anything that raises cardiac output (e.g., exercise)
Timing featurePresystolic accentuation — increased intensity just before S1
DurationReflects how long LA pressure exceeds LV diastolic pressure — duration increases with severity

The Opening Snap

A high-pitched sound occurring shortly after S2 (specifically following A2), from a stiff mitral valve. S1 is loud. The A2-to-opening-snap interval is a severity clue: long when LA pressure is low; shortens as LA pressure rises (more severe disease).
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. What is the most common overall etiology of mitral stenosis, and approximately what fraction of MS cases does it account for?

Explanation: Untreated rheumatic heart disease is the most common etiology of mitral stenosis overall, accounting for approximately one-third of MS cases, typically presenting in the 3rd-4th decade of life with thickened, fused, calcified valve structures.

2. The interval between A2 and the opening snap in mitral stenosis provides a clue to disease severity. What does a SHORT A2-to-opening-snap interval indicate?

Explanation: The A2-to-opening-snap interval shortens as left atrial pressure rises, since higher LA pressure causes the stiff mitral valve to open (snap) sooner after aortic valve closure -- a shorter interval therefore indicates more severe, hemodynamically significant mitral stenosis.

3. Which symptom is notably UNCOMMON in severe mitral stenosis, despite the presence of significant left heart failure symptoms like dyspnea, orthopnea, and PND?

Explanation: Despite the left heart failure symptoms seen in severe mitral stenosis, frank pulmonary edema is actually uncommon -- an important nuance, since the chronic elevation in pulmonary venous pressure leads to compensatory changes (e.g., pulmonary lymphatic drainage, vascular remodeling) that make acute pulmonary edema less frequent than might be expected.

9. Mitral Stenosis: Diagnosis & Treatment

Additional Signs

Mitral faciesRosy/flushed cheeks over the malar bones, sometimes with a bluish tinge; seen in longstanding severe MS, often with pulmonary hypertension and low cardiac output

Diagnostic Workup

ModalityFindings
Labs (BNP)Increases due to chamber stretch; more useful for following progression over time than for acute diagnosis
EKGLeft atrial enlargement, right axis deviation, RVH, atrial fibrillation
Chest X-rayLA enlargement ± cephalization (of pulmonary vessels)
EchocardiographyLeaflet thickening/mobility, submitral scarring, commissural calcium, LA size, calculated mitral valve area and RV pressure; classic "hockey stick" sign of the anterior mitral leaflet
Cardiac catheterizationDetects coronary artery disease; guides stent/bypass timing relative to valve repair/replacement
Hockey Stick Sign Thickening and calcification of the anterior mitral leaflet tip causes it to bow abnormally during diastole, creating a characteristic hockey-stick-shaped appearance on echo.

Valve Area — The Key Number

Normal mitral valve area is 4–6 cm². An area <2 cm² impedes flow, creating a pressure gradient across the valve — this drives increased atrial contraction ("atrial kick"), leading over time to LA enlargement and impaired LV diastolic filling.

Treatment

ProblemApproach
Heart failureVery limited medical options; consider a loop diuretic
Rheumatic MS preventionAntibiotics — secondary prevention of Group A strep pharyngitis (penicillin)
Atrial fibrillationRate control (beta-blockers, CCBs); rhythm control only if LA is not severely dilated and disease is early (<6–12 months); anticoagulation (20–30% risk of systemic embolization if untreated) — use CHA2DS2-VASc score, prefer VKA/warfarin over DOACs (insufficient trial data/safety evidence for DOACs in this population)

Procedural/Surgical Options

ProcedureKey Criteria
Balloon valvuloplasty<0.5% mortality; requires an echo score of ≤8; indicated for symptomatic patients with NYHA functional class >2, or any asymptomatic patient with pulmonary hypertension; improves symptoms by increasing mitral valve area and reducing the transvalvular gradient

📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. What is the normal mitral valve area, and below what threshold does an impediment to flow with a pressure gradient typically develop?

Explanation: The normal mitral valve area is 4-6 cm2. When the area falls below 2 cm2, it impedes blood flow and creates a pressure gradient across the valve, driving compensatory changes including increased atrial contraction and eventual left atrial enlargement.

2. A patient with mitral stenosis is being evaluated for balloon valvuloplasty versus mitral valve replacement. An echo score of 6 is reported. Which procedure is favored based on this score, and what is its approximate mortality?

Explanation: An echo score of 8 or less favors balloon valvuloplasty, which carries less than 0.5% mortality, whereas valve replacement (1-3% mortality) is reserved for higher echo scores (>8-10) or when combined stenosis and regurgitation are present.

3. For a patient with mitral stenosis and atrial fibrillation requiring anticoagulation, which anticoagulant class is preferred, and why?

Explanation: Vitamin K antagonists (warfarin) are preferred over DOACs for anticoagulation in mitral stenosis with atrial fibrillation, since DOACs lack sufficient clinical trial data supporting their safety and efficacy in this specific valvular AFib population.

10. Mitral Regurgitation: Etiology, Symptoms & Signs

MR = something is preventing the mitral valve from closing properly, allowing blood to flow backward across the valve. Can coexist with mitral stenosis, and is often associated with mitral valve prolapse.

Etiology — The Five Functional Components of the Mitral Apparatus

Any abnormality of the leaflets, annulus, chordae tendineae, papillary muscles, or the myocardium itself can cause MR.
TypeMechanism
Primary (degenerative) MRLeaflets and/or chordae tendineae are primarily abnormal
Secondary (functional) MRLeaflets/chordae are usually normal; regurgitation is caused by LV remodeling, annular dilation, papillary muscle displacement, dyssynchrony, posterior leaflet tethering, or a combination
Why This Distinction Matters Patient management differs substantially between primary and secondary MR.

Acute MR — Causes

  • Papillary muscle rupture during acute MI
  • Blunt chest trauma
  • Infective endocarditis — can tear or destroy leaflets

Chronic Severe MR

Marked LA enlargement with increased LA compliance — meaning only a minimal rise in LA pressure/pulmonary venous pressure occurs despite a large increase in LA volume (a compensatory mechanism that can mask severity for a time).

Symptoms & Signs

SymptomsSigns
May be asymptomaticChronic LA and LV enlargement
ArrhythmiasAtrial fibrillation
Heart failure (left-sided): dyspnea, orthopnea, PNDLV dysfunction; hyperdynamic LV impulse (becomes displaced later in disease); brisk carotid upstroke with normal pulse pressure; S3
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Which five functional components make up the mitral valve apparatus, any of which can be responsible for mitral regurgitation?

Explanation: The mitral valve apparatus consists of five functional components -- leaflets, annulus, chordae tendineae, papillary muscles, and the myocardium itself -- and abnormality of any of these can produce mitral regurgitation.

2. A patient's mitral regurgitation is found to result from left ventricular remodeling and annular dilation, with structurally normal leaflets and chordae. How is this classified?

Explanation: Secondary (functional) MR is characterized by normal leaflets and chordae tendineae, with regurgitation instead caused by processes like LV remodeling, annular dilation, papillary muscle displacement, or leaflet tethering -- distinguishing it from primary (degenerative) MR where the leaflets/chordae themselves are abnormal.

3. Why can chronic severe mitral regurgitation remain relatively well-tolerated for a period of time despite significant valve dysfunction?

Explanation: In chronic severe MR, the left atrium enlarges and becomes more compliant, meaning it can accommodate a large increase in regurgitant volume with only a minimal rise in LA pressure and pulmonary venous pressure -- a compensatory mechanism that can delay the onset of overt symptoms.

11. Mitral Regurgitation: Murmur, Diagnosis & Treatment

The Murmur

FeatureDetail
GradeIII/VI
QualityClassically holosystolic (can also be decrescendo)
LocationMaximal at the apex, radiating to the axilla (occasionally to the base)
Maneuvers: anything that increases preload (handgrip, squatting) increases the murmur; anything that decreases afterload (standing, Valsalva, nitrates) decreases the murmur.
MR vs. TR Both can be holosystolic murmurs — the key distinguisher is that TR gets louder with inspiration (a right-sided murmur), while MR does not change with respiration (a left-sided murmur).

Diagnosis

ModalityFindings
EKGLeft axis deviation, LA enlargement, LVH, strain pattern
Chest X-rayEnlarged cardiac silhouette
Transthoracic echoLV size increase, regurgitant jet into LA, LA size increase, PA pressure, RV function
Cardiac catheterizationAdjunct evaluation

Treatment — Surgical Indications

Surgery indicated once symptomatic, or in asymptomatic patients with:
  • LV dysfunction: EF <60%
  • LV dilation with end-systolic dimension >40 mm
  • Pulmonary hypertension
Repair > Replacement Repair is preferred over replacement — valve reconstruction (valvuloplasty + annuloplasty ring) carries fewer long-term complications (thromboembolism, hemorrhage, late valve failure) than replacement.

MitraClip — Transcatheter Repair

A newer device treating both degenerative (primary) and functional (secondary) MR: a small metallic clip covered in polyester fabric, delivered via catheter from the femoral vein to the left atrium, where it sits flush on the mitral valve to reduce regurgitation without open-heart surgery.
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A holosystolic murmur is heard at the apex. Which finding would help distinguish tricuspid regurgitation from mitral regurgitation?

Explanation: Tricuspid regurgitation, being a right-sided murmur, characteristically increases in intensity with inspiration, while mitral regurgitation (a left-sided murmur) does not show this respiratory variation -- a key bedside distinguishing feature.

2. An asymptomatic patient with severe mitral regurgitation has an ejection fraction of 55% and an LV end-systolic dimension of 45 mm. Based on surgical criteria, what is the appropriate recommendation?

Explanation: Even in an asymptomatic patient, an LV end-systolic dimension greater than 40 mm meets surgical criteria for mitral regurgitation, reflecting significant LV remodeling from the chronic volume overload, regardless of a currently preserved EF.

3. For mitral regurgitation requiring surgical intervention, which approach is generally preferred, and why?

Explanation: Repair (valve reconstruction with valvuloplasty and an annuloplasty ring) is generally preferred over replacement for mitral regurgitation, since it is associated with fewer long-term complications including thromboembolism, hemorrhage, and late valve failure.

12. Mitral Valve Prolapse

Also known as: systolic click-murmur syndrome, Barlow syndrome (floppy-valve syndrome), and billowing mitral leaflet syndrome. MVP is the most common cause of primary MR, and in North America is the most common cause of isolated severe MR requiring surgical repair.

Etiology

  • Usually unknown cause; some genetic etiology involving reduced type III collagen production or collagen fibril fragmentation
  • Pathology: myxomatous degeneration of the mitral valve, affecting the posterior leaflet more than the anterior; the mitral annulus is usually dilated
  • Frequently seen with connective tissue disorders: Marfan syndrome, osteogenesis imperfecta, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome
  • Rarely a sequela of acute rheumatic fever; also seen with ischemic heart disease and various cardiomyopathies

Clinical Demographics

PopulationDetail
Women, ages 15–30More commonly affected; clinical course generally benign
Older patients >50, usually menHigher risk of chordal rupture requiring surgical treatment — often precipitated by an acute myocardial infarction in this population
Most patients are asymptomatic throughout life.

Symptoms (When Present)

  • Palpitations (from arrhythmias), lightheadedness, syncope, anxiety/panic attacks, chest pain
  • Rarely: sudden death

Auscultation

FeatureDetail
ClickMid-to-late (non-ejection) systolic click — thought to be caused by sudden tensing of the chordae tendineae as the leaflet reaches maximal excursion (like a parachute reaching full extension)
MurmurHigh-pitched, mid-to-late systolic crescendo-decrescendo murmur (sometimes described as "whooping" or "honking"); heard best at the apex
Leaflet-Specific Radiation Pattern Posterior leaflet prolapse → murmur radiates to the base of the heart. Anterior leaflet prolapse → murmur radiates posteriorly to the back and axilla.

📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A patient with MVP has posterior leaflet prolapse. Where would the associated murmur be expected to radiate?

Explanation: Posterior leaflet prolapse causes a murmur that radiates to the base of the heart, while anterior leaflet prolapse causes radiation posteriorly to the back and axilla -- an important leaflet-specific distinguishing feature.

2. Which patient population with MVP is at highest risk for chordal rupture requiring surgical treatment?

Explanation: While MVP is more common overall in women aged 15-30 (who typically have a benign course), older patients over age 50 -- usually men -- are at higher risk for chordal rupture requiring surgical treatment.

3. Infective endocarditis prophylaxis is recommended for which subset of patients with mitral valve prolapse?

Explanation: Infective endocarditis prophylaxis is only needed in MVP patients who have a prior history of endocarditis -- it is not indicated for every patient simply diagnosed with MVP.

13. Tricuspid Valve Disease

Tricuspid Stenosis (TS)

FactDetail
PrevalenceLess prevalent than mitral stenosis
CauseGenerally rheumatic heart disease — usually occurs together with mitral stenosis

HemodynamicsDiastolic pressure gradient between RA and RV; augmented with inspiration, declines with expiration

TS — Presentation & Exam

Because TS usually follows/accompanies mitral stenosis, presentation overlaps (pulmonary congestion, fatigue). The diagnosis is often missed unless specifically considered — classically suspected when a patient fails to improve after mitral valve commissurotomy for MS.
  • Physical exam: hepatic congestion (cirrhosis, jaundice, anasarca, ascites); JVD with giant "a" waves and slow "y" descent; presystolic liver pulsation

TS — Murmur & Evaluation

TS — Treatment

Due to marked systemic venous congestion: salt restriction, diuretic therapy, bed rest — all in preparation for surgery. TS usually accompanies tricuspid regurgitation. Commissurotomy preferred; full valve replacement otherwise.

📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A patient with known mitral stenosis undergoes successful mitral commissurotomy but fails to improve clinically. What diagnosis should be strongly considered, and why might it have been missed initially?

Explanation: Tricuspid stenosis usually occurs together with mitral stenosis and shares overlapping symptoms (pulmonary congestion, fatigue), so the diagnosis is often missed unless specifically considered -- classically suspected when a patient fails to improve after mitral commissurotomy for MS.

2. What is the most common overall cause of tricuspid regurgitation, and what is its underlying mechanism?

Explanation: Functional (secondary) tricuspid regurgitation, arising from tricuspid annular dilatation and/or leaflet tethering (often due to right heart or pulmonary pressure changes), accounts for the majority (75-90%) of TR cases, distinct from the less common primary form seen with congenitally deformed valves.

3. A holosystolic murmur at the left lower sternal border becomes noticeably louder during inspiration. What is this finding called, and what valve lesion does it indicate?

Explanation: Carvallo's sign describes the increase in intensity of the tricuspid regurgitation murmur with inspiration, reflecting the general principle that right-sided murmurs augment with inspiration due to increased venous return.

Etiology

Signs — Pulmonary Stenosis

  • Harsh crescendo-decrescendo ejection murmur, similar in character to aortic stenosis but heard at a different location — left parasternal 2nd or 4th ICS (rather than the right upper sternal border), with the diaphragm of the stethoscope, patient leaning forward

FindingDetail
MurmurHigh-pitched, early diastolic, decrescendo — begins with P2 and ends before S1; radiates toward the mid-right sternal edge; heard best at the left upper sternal border, with the patient sitting upright and holding their breath at end-expiration; fixed split S2 with a loud P2 component

📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A patient who underwent surgical repair of Tetralogy of Fallot years ago now presents with a new murmur similar to aortic regurgitation but at a different location, along with a fixed split S2 and loud P2. What is the most likely diagnosis?

Explanation: Pulmonary regurgitation is a well-recognized complication of surgical repair of Tetralogy of Fallot (which often involves the pulmonary valve/outflow tract), presenting with a murmur resembling aortic regurgitation but at a different (pulmonic) location, along with a fixed split S2 and loud P2.

2. What echocardiographic finding is characteristic of pulmonary regurgitation, reflecting right ventricular volume overload?

Explanation: Paradoxical septal wall motion is seen on echo in pulmonary regurgitation, reflecting the right ventricular volume overload that alters the normal motion pattern of the interventricular septum.

3. What is the general approach to treatment for pulmonary valve disease, in contrast to the more aggressive surgical approach often used for left-sided valve lesions?

Explanation: Pulmonary valve disease is generally managed by treating the underlying cause (such as pulmonary hypertension) with diuretics, anti-arrhythmics, and anticoagulation, since valve commissurotomy or replacement is not a common approach for this valve, unlike the more frequent surgical interventions for left-sided valve disease.
