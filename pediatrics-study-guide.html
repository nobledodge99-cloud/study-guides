1. Pediatric History & Exam Approach

No single approach to the history applies to all pediatric patients. In younger children, parents/caregivers are the primary historians; as soon as a child can communicate, involve them directly. By around age 7, children may be considered reasonably reliable historians about their own health. By adolescence, much of the history is taken with the patient alone.

Pediatric family history should include not just adult-onset conditions but items specifically relevant to children: genetic disease, congenital abnormalities, miscarriages, and unexpected/early deaths in the family. For a newborn specifically, family history of genetic disease is the single most important item to elicit. For any pediatric patient, an unexplained sudden cardiac death in a young relative is highly relevant (possible inherited arrhythmia/cardiomyopathy risk).

A pediatric history should always include nutrition, elimination, and sleep, in addition to the standard adult-style history components (PMHx, birth history, meds, allergies, FMHx, social history including family structure, pets, daycare, smokers in the home).

General exam approach principles

  • Growth (height/weight, plotted on a standardized growth curve) should be measured and recorded at every visit — it can reveal failure to thrive or metabolic/endocrine disease.
  • Be flexible with exam sequence — do less-tolerated portions later, and examine the area of a specific complaint last.
  • Infants <9 months: exam on the table (with safety precautions against rolling off). >9 months: exam on parent's lap (stranger anxiety develops around this age).
  • Toddlers: undress to diaper, exam on parent's lap, use toys/distraction, let the child touch equipment first.
  • School-age children (4–10 yrs): can generally use head-to-toe order; explain each step; respect modesty.
  • Adolescents: gowned, on table; offer to have parent step out for part of the history/exam; always use a chaperone for breast/GU exams.

2. Delivery & APGAR Scoring

A pediatrician's job starts at delivery — roughly 1 in 500 newborns need some form of support in the first few minutes of life. For low-risk deliveries, the newborn is typically evaluated within 24 hours in the newborn nursery (NBN) rather than at the delivery itself; high-risk deliveries are attended directly.

APGAR mnemonic Appearance (color) · Pulse · Grimace (reflex irritability) · Activity (muscle tone) · Respiratory effort
APGAR Scoring (0–2 points per component)
Clinical sign0 points1 point2 points
AppearanceBlue, palePink body, blue extremitiesPink all over
PulseAbsent<100>100
Reflex irritability (Grimace)No responseGrimaceCrying vigorously, sneeze or cough
ActivityFlaccidSome flexion of arms/legsActive movement
Respiratory effortAbsentSlow & irregularGood, strong
Scores are assigned at 1 minute and 5 minutes (a third score at 10 minutes if needed). A common, expected score is 8–9 (a point is often lost on Appearance due to acrocyanosis of the extremities). Scores <8 at the 5-minute mark are a concern, especially <7 — if it hasn't improved by 5 minutes, further evaluation/intervention is warranted.

Shortly after delivery, nursing staff routinely provide: erythromycin ointment in the eyes (infection prevention), a vitamin K injection (bleeding prevention), and a full bath (typically delayed to encourage skin-to-skin contact with the mother first).

Gestational age and birth weight together help predict medical problems and morbidity. Gestational age is estimated from neuromuscular signs and physical characteristics using the Ballard scoring system when precise dating/prenatal records are unavailable.

Ballard Scoring System

Scores from two categories are added together, and the total correlates to a maturity rating (estimated gestational age):

  • Neuromuscular maturity: posture, square window (degree of wrist flexion), arm recoil, popliteal angle, scarf sign (ability to move the arm across the body), heel-to-ear
  • Physical maturity: skin, lanugo (fine body hair), plantar surface creases, breast, eye/ear (ear recoil), genitals

Growth tracking (beyond the newborn period)

  • Growth in the neonatal/infant period is faster than any other age: birth weight triples by 1 year, and height increases by ~50%.
  • Infant/toddler growth charts plot: length, head circumference (until 24–36 months), weight, and BMI after age 2.
  • School-age/adolescent growth rate slows until puberty; charts plot height, weight, and BMI.
  • Always plot measurements on a growth chart and review it before examining the patient.

It is normal for a newborn to lose 7–10% of birth weight in the first few days of life. The infant should be back to birth weight by 10–14 days, with expected weight gain thereafter of roughly 1 ounce per day. This pattern (loss, then steady regain by ~2 weeks) is checked specifically at the 2-week visit.

5. Neonatal Jaundice

The major concern with untreated severe jaundice is kernicterus — bilirubin-induced neurologic damage, particularly of concern once unconjugated bilirubin exceeds roughly 25 mg/dL.

Causes of Neonatal Jaundice
CategoryExamples
Decreased conjugation of bilirubinBreastmilk jaundice, cholestasis, CMV
Increased red blood cell turnoverBruising, ABO incompatibility, Rh incompatibility
Physiologic ("normal") jaundiceOccurs in about half of all newborns
Pathologic jaundice (other causes)Breastfeeding jaundice, cephalohematoma, infection, hemoglobinopathies (e.g., thalassemia), enzyme deficiencies (e.g., G6PD)
Physiologic jaundice appears on day 2–3, peaks around day 5, and usually resolves within a week. Any jaundice appearing within the first 24 hours of life is, by definition, pathologic and warrants prompt evaluation.

Evaluation: transcutaneous bilimeter, serum direct/indirect bilirubin levels; additional labs as indicated: neonatal blood type, direct antibody titer (Coombs test), complete blood count and smear.

Interpretation & treatment: compare the bilirubin level to a nomogram (risk-based on age in hours) and treat accordingly; repeat levels if concerning. Phototherapy is used for high levels or a rapid rate of rise (>5 mg/dL per day); exchange transfusion is reserved for severe cases.

7. Well-Child Visits & Developmental Screening

Screening at well-child visits engages parents in the child's development, sets expectations, and gives the provider a structured way to listen to parental concerns.

Routine Well-Child Visit Schedule
PeriodVisit timing
Newborn2–3 days, then 2 weeks, then 1 month
Infant2, 4, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 24 months
Beyond age 2Annually through middle childhood and adolescence into adulthood
Developmental surveillance vs. developmental screening — a frequently tested distinction: Surveillance is the ongoing use of parents' concerns, the provider's own observations, and prior knowledge of the child to judge developmental risk at every visit. Screening is the use of a structured, standardized tool (e.g., Denver II [direct provider observation], Ages & Stages Questionnaires/ASQ [parent-completed]) specifically to identify children at risk. Formal screening should occur at the 9-, 18-, and 30-month visits, and additionally at any visit where surveillance raises a concern — even if it isn't one of those three standard screening ages.

Developmental delay is common: up to 15% of children aged 3–17 have delayed development in at least one area. Risk factors include low socioeconomic status, poor nutrition, low parental education, and inadequate/unstable housing. Because delay is common and consequences of missing it can be significant, any concern should prompt referral to early intervention services for evaluation.

Autism screening red flags (M-CHAT-R)

Key M-CHAT-R type questions: Is your child interested in other children? Does your child respond when called by name? Does your child smile back when you smile at them?
Autism Red Flags by Age
AgeRed flag(s)
~6 monthsAvoiding eye contact (an early red flag to watch for)
~18 monthsNo single words; persistent rocking, hand flapping, head banging, or toe walking; any loss of previously acquired language or social skills; not playing interactively with other children

Note: pointing to indicate interest in objects is a typical (not concerning) behavior at 18 months — it's the absence of shared-interest behaviors, and the presence of repetitive/self-stimulating movements or skill loss, that are red flags.

8. Developmental Milestones by Age

Definitions of Pediatric Age Periods
PeriodAge range
Newborn0–28 days
Infancy0–12 months
Toddler/Early childhood1–4 years
School-aged/Middle childhood5–10 years
Adolescence11–20 years (early, middle, late)

Neonate/Infant milestones

  • Language: 2 months — social smile; 6 months — babbling (consonant sounds); 1 year — 1–3 words
  • Cognitive: 6 months — recognizes strangers; 9 months — stranger anxiety begins, seeks comfort from parent, learns cause/effect, develops object permanence and tool use
  • Social: bonding, attachment to caregivers; temperament varies widely between children; predictable schedules and consistent discipline help children feel secure

Toddler milestones

  • Physical/motor: growth rate slows to about half the infant rate; after age 2, gains ~2–3 kg and 5 cm per year; gross motor — walk, jump, hop, skip; fine motor — draw lines, make circles
  • Cognitive/language: sensorimotor learning, simple problem-solving, imitative play; progresses from 2–3 word phrases to full sentences
  • Social/emotional: progression from imitative to imaginative play; drive for independence; temper tantrums common
Speech development mnemonics Sentence length: 2-year-old → 2-word sentences; 3-year-old → 3-word sentences.
Intelligibility to a stranger: 2-year-old → ~50%; 3-year-old → ~75%; 4-year-old → ~100%.

School-aged child (5–10 yrs) milestones

  • Physical/motor: steady but slow growth; strength and coordination improve
  • Cognitive/language: concrete operational thinking (limited logic, applied mainly to physical objects), developing self-efficacy
  • Social/emotional: progressively more independent, developing self-esteem (heavily shaped by family and environment), evolving self-identity

9. Common Infant Issues & Anticipatory Guidance

The "3 C's" of early infancy Congestion · Constipation (often perceived rather than true) · Colic
Managing the 3 C's
IssueManagement
CongestionSaline rinse, suction; no effective medications for this
ConstipationMonitoring; pear juice, karo syrup, or Miralax as needed
ColicSwaddling, white noise, car rides; discuss keeping the infant safe when a parent is frustrated (take a break, leave infant safely in the crib, accept help)
Colic definition (paroxysms of fussiness/crying): 3 or more hours a day, 3 or more days a week, usually in the evenings, not explained by an underlying medical condition. Peaks around 6 weeks and resolves by 3–4 months.
Colic counseling mnemonic "Don't shake. Take a break." — change the diaper, feed, burp, swaddle, and examine the child for a physical cause before assuming it's simply colic.

Fever in the neonate

Fever in a neonate (within roughly the first 30 days) is a medical emergency — a full sepsis workup is indicated and the infant is considered septic until proven otherwise. Remember that neonatal sepsis can also present with hypothermia or temperature instability rather than fever.

Safety & anticipatory guidance highlights

  • Back to sleep; never shake a baby; no walkers.
  • Infants begin rolling around 3–4 months — discuss fall prevention at the 2-month visit.
  • Keep small objects/plastic bags away — discuss around 4–6 months.
  • Water safety — infants can drown in an inch of water; set the hot water heater to 120°F.
  • Rear-facing car seat until age 2, placed in the back seat.
  • Vitamin D supplementation for exclusively breastfed infants.
  • No honey for the first year of life (botulism risk).
  • Screen time: limit to about 1 hour/day for ages 2–5.
  • Booster car seat recommended until the child reaches 4'9".
  • 60 minutes of physical activity per day for school-aged children.

10. Physical Exam Findings (HEENT, Cardiac, Hip, Skin)

Head & fontanelles

Caput succedaneum crosses suture lines; cephalohematoma does not (it's confined by the periosteum of a single skull bone) — both are possible findings after a vaginal delivery.
Fontanelle Closure
FontanelleClosure timing
Anterior fontanelleLarger, closes later (roughly 4–26 months, commonly cited as 18 months–2 years)
Posterior fontanelleSmaller, closes earlier (by about 1–2 months of age)

Both fontanelles should be flat and soft ("AFS/F" or "PFS/F" in documentation shorthand); sunken or bulging is abnormal. Plagiocephaly is a positional skull deformity; craniosynostosis is early fusion of the cranial sutures.

Eyes, ears, mouth

  • Red reflex should be symmetric; an abnormal red reflex may indicate cataracts, glaucoma, retinoblastoma, or other eye pathology.
  • Cover/uncover testing is done between 9 months–3 years; formal visual acuity testing starts around age 3.
  • Infant ear canals point downward — pull the auricle down (not up, as in adults) to visualize the tympanic membrane.
  • Children have wider, shorter Eustachian tubes than adults, predisposing them to otitis media.
  • Preauricular pit: congenital, rarely associated with hearing loss, not strongly linked to genetic syndromes. Preauricular tag: congenital, increased risk of hearing loss and genetic syndrome association.
  • Epstein's pearls: benign mucus cysts on the hard palate. Ankyloglossia = tongue-tied.

Murmurs detected <6 hours of life are more likely valvular (e.g., tricuspid/mitral regurgitation, aortic/pulmonic stenosis); murmurs detected >6 hours of life are more likely shunt lesions (e.g., septal defects, PDA).

Abdomen & genitourinary

  • Liver edge palpable ~1–2 cm below the costal margin is normal; the spleen should not be palpable.
  • Umbilical hernias usually resolve as the umbilical ring closes; surgery isn't typically recommended (especially <4 years) unless there's concern for incarceration/strangulation.
  • Do not forcibly retract the foreskin in uncircumcised infants.
  • A sacral dimple with hair tufts can suggest an underlying neural tube defect (e.g., spina bifida) — though isolated sacral dimples are typically benign.

Hip exam: Ortolani vs. Barlow

Ortolani vs. Barlow Maneuver
ManeuverTests for
Ortolani testA posteriorly dislocated hip that can be reduced (a hip already out, being put back in)
Barlow testThe ability to sublux or dislocate an intact but unstable hip (a hip that's in, being pushed out)

Both maneuvers become less reliable after about 3 months, once the hip capsule begins to tighten. The Galeazzi sign (a difference in knee height with hips/knees flexed) is another sign of developmental hip dysplasia to look for.

Musculoskeletal & skin

  • Toddlers normally show increased lumbar concavity, decreased thoracic convexity, and a protuberant abdomen; bowlegged appearance is normal ages 1–3, knock-kneed appearance normal ages 3–5.
  • Flexible deformities generally reflect in-utero positioning and resolve; rigid deformities usually require intervention.
  • Common benign skin findings: Mongolian spots, nevi, hemangiomas; cradle cap (infant seborrheic dermatitis) is treated with infant shampoo and gentle combing.
  • Impetigo: honey-crusted lesions, commonly staphylococcal; treated with topical (or oral, for larger areas) antibiotics.
  • Tinea corporis (ringworm): elevated red rings with central clearing.

High-yield distinction: middle adolescence is specifically the stage where teens can intellectually recognize long-term consequences of their actions but those consequences don't yet meaningfully factor into real-time decision-making — and it's the stage where conforming to peers matters most.

HEADSS / HEEADSSS assessment

A structured tool for the adolescent social history, asked privately (without parents/guardians in the room), moving from least to most personal topics, using open-ended, non-judgmental questions:

HEADSS / HEEADSSS mnemonic Home · Education/Employment · (Eating) · Activities · Drugs · Sexuality · Suicide/depression · (Safety)

Example opening for the Drugs section (non-judgmental, indirect): "What kinds of drugs have you seen around school or at parties?"

Adolescent consent & confidentiality

In most states, adolescents may independently consent to and receive confidential care for: contraception, pregnancy-related care, sexually transmitted infection testing/treatment, substance abuse treatment, and mental health care. (Laws vary by state.)

Conversations with an adolescent patient may remain confidential unless there is a serious/immediate safety concern — e.g., suicidal ideation, homicidal ideation, or concern for physical abuse. If confidentiality must be broken for safety, inform the adolescent in advance whenever possible. Note: declining school performance, risky sexual behavior, and substance use disclosures can generally remain confidential on their own; it's specifically imminent harm to self or others that overrides confidentiality.
