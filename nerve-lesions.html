Brachial Plexus Structure

The brachial plexus (C5–T1) is organized, proximal to distal: Roots → Trunks → Divisions → Cords → Branches.

Mnemonic: "Real Texans Drink Cold Beer" (or "Randy Travis Drinks Cold Beer")

  • Roots (C5, C6, C7, C8, T1)
    • Trunks
      • Upper trunk (C5–C6) — injured in Erb-Duchenne palsy
      • Middle trunk (C7)
      • Lower trunk (C8–T1) — injured in Klumpke palsy

Each trunk splits into an anterior and posterior division, which recombine into three cords, named for their position relative to the axillary artery:

  • Lateral cord (anterior divisions of upper + middle trunks) → musculocutaneous n. + lateral head of median n.
  • Posterior cord (posterior divisions of all three trunks) → axillary n. + radial n.
  • Medial cord (anterior division of lower trunk) → ulnar n. + medial head of median n.

Terminal branches mnemonic: "Musty Aunt Rosy Uses Milk" for the 5 terminal nerves in order (roughly proximal-to-distal on a plexus diagram): Musculocutaneous, Axillary, Radial, Ulnar, Median.

Nerves that branch directly off the roots (before the trunks even form) include the long thoracic nerve (C5–C7) and the dorsal scapular nerve; the suprascapular nerve branches off the upper trunk itself.

Lumbosacral Plexus Structure

The lumbosacral plexus is formed from the anterior rami of L1–S4, conventionally divided into the lumbar plexus (L1–L4, mostly supplying the anterior/medial thigh) and the sacral plexus (L4–S4, mostly supplying the posterior thigh, leg, and foot), connected by the lumbosacral trunk.

  • Lumbar plexus (L1–L4)
    • Iliohypogastric n. (L1)
    • Ilioinguinal n. (L1)
    • Genitofemoral n. (L1–L2)
    • Lateral femoral cutaneous n. (L2–L3)
    • Obturator n. (L2–L4) — medial thigh (adductor compartment)
    • Femoral n. (L2–L4) — anterior thigh
  • Sacral plexus (L4–S4), via the lumbosacral trunk (L4–L5)
    • Superior gluteal n. (L4–S1)
    • Inferior gluteal n. (L5–S2)
    • Nerve to piriformis (L5–S2), nerve to obturator internus (L5–S2), nerve to quadratus femoris (L4–S1)
    • Posterior femoral cutaneous n. (S1–S3)
    • Pudendal n. (S2–S4)
    • Sciatic n. (L4–S3) — splits (usually at/near the popliteal fossa) into:
      • Tibial n. → posterior leg, sole of foot
      • Common fibular n. → splits into superficial fibular n. (lateral leg, eversion) and deep fibular n. (anterior leg, dorsiflexion, 1st web space)

Classic Named Deformities & Signs

Sign / DeformityNerveDescription
Wrist dropRadial n.Cannot extend the wrist or fingers; hand hangs flexed at the wrist
Claw handUlnar n.Hyperextension of the MCP joints with flexion of the DIP/PIP joints of the ring and little fingers (worse with a distal/wrist lesion — the "ulnar paradox")
Hand of Benediction / "Pope's blessing"Median n. (when trying to make a fist)Index and middle fingers fail to flex while ring/little fingers flex normally
Ape handMedian n. (chronic)Thenar eminence wasting with the thumb held adducted/extended in the same plane as the fingers, due to loss of opposition
OK sign weaknessAnterior interosseous n. (median n. branch)Cannot form a full circle pinching thumb to index finger — forms a triangle/pinch weakness instead
Froment's signUlnar n.Substitution of thumb flexion (via median-innervated FPL) for weak adduction (adductor pollicis) when pinching a piece of paper
Winged scapula (medial winging)Long thoracic n.Medial border of the scapula protrudes posteriorly, especially when pushing against a wall
Winged scapula (lateral winging)Spinal accessory n. (CN XI)Lateral scapular winging with shoulder droop and weak shrug (trapezius palsy)
Waiter's tip positionErb-Duchenne palsy (upper trunk, C5–C6)Arm adducted, internally rotated, extended at the elbow, forearm pronated, wrist flexed
Claw hand at birth ± Horner syndromeKlumpke palsy (lower trunk, C8–T1)Intrinsic hand muscle paralysis; Horner syndrome if the T1 root avulses (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis)
Foot dropCommon/deep fibular n.Cannot dorsiflex the foot; high-stepping ("steppage") gait to avoid dragging the toes
Trendelenburg signSuperior gluteal n.Pelvis drops on the side CONTRALATERAL to the lesion when standing on the affected leg
Gluteus maximus lurchInferior gluteal n.Thorax lurches posteriorly over the stance leg on the SAME side as the lesion, to substitute for weak hip extension
Meralgia parestheticaLateral femoral cutaneous n.Burning/tingling over the lateral thigh from entrapment under the inguinal ligament
