00 · General Concepts & Classification

What Is Vasculitis?

Inflammation of the blood vessel wall — the size and location of the affected vessel determines the clinical picture
  • Vascul = blood vessels (arteries and veins); -itis = inflammation
  • Arteries = arteritis or angiitis; veins = phlebitis
  • Vasculitis is a heterogeneous group of disorders characterized by inflammation of blood vessel walls, most often arteries
  • Characterized primarily by the size of the blood vessel affected — this is the single most important organizing principle for the entire topic
Study strategyEvery vasculitis in this guide can be filed first by vessel size (large / medium / small / variable), then by a handful of distinguishing features (organ tropism, serology, epidemiology). Section 14 builds this into a full pattern-recognition framework — use it as your final review tool.

Mechanism of Vessel Damage

  • Vasculitis is an autoimmune (immune-mediated) disease — molecular mimicry causes the body to confuse a part of itself as a foreign invader
  • Direct attack: immune cells attack the endothelial cells of the vessel directly — generally seen in large and medium vessel vasculitides
  • Indirect damage: immune cells attack tissue near the endothelial lining, causing secondary damage to the endothelium — generally seen in small vessel vasculitides
  • Exception: IgA vasculitis (HSP) is a small vessel disease that causes direct damage, via IgA antibodies targeting the endothelial cells themselves
Immune mechanisms of vessel damage
MechanismDescription
Autoantibodiese.g., ANCA (anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies)
Cell-mediatedMonocytes, macrophages, lymphocytes
Immune complexDeposit directly in vessel walls
Downstream consequences of endothelial damageExposed collagen and tissue factor trigger increased coagulation (blood cells clump onto exposed collagen → blood clots → limited flow → ischemia). The vessel wall itself is weakened → aneurysm formation. As the vessel heals, fibrin deposition makes it narrower and stiffer.
General (systemic) symptoms of the inflammatory responseFever, weight loss, fatigue, night sweats, myalgia/arthralgia — seen across essentially every vasculitis in this guide, on top of whichever organ-specific symptoms come from the vessel(s) actually involved.

01 · Giant Cell Arteritis (Temporal Arteritis)

Etiology, Epidemiology & Risk Factors

The most common systemic vasculitis
  • Mainly large vessels (some medium vessel involvement) — branches of the carotid artery
  • Temporal branch of the carotid → unilateral headache, temporal pain; ophthalmic artery → visual disturbances; facial artery → jaw claudication with chewing
  • Cause unknown — combination of genetic, environmental, and autoimmune factors
  • Risk factors: biologically female > male, Northern European descent, age > 50
  • Strong association with polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR) — ~15% of PMR patients develop GCA; ~50% of GCA patients develop (or have) PMR

Symptoms & Signs

  • Headache — new, severe, localized to the temporal region
  • Amaurosis fugax — transient unilateral vision loss, often preceded by episodes of blurred vision; visual loss can also be permanent and can occur very abruptly
  • Jaw claudication, especially with chewing; also scalp tenderness
  • Tender, nodular, or pulseless temporal arteries on exam
  • Decreased pulses or a bruit over the clavicle suggest subclavian/axillary involvement; aortic regurgitation may be present
  • Atypical presentations: fever of unknown origin, cough, mononeuritis multiplex, or other large-vessel symptoms (TIA, stroke, thoracic aortic aneurysm)
Visual loss — the number to knowVisual symptoms occur in about 1/3 of GCA patients, developing on average ~5 months after disease onset, secondary to occlusion of the posterior ciliary artery (a branch of the ophthalmic artery). Vision loss can be monocular or binocular, transient or permanent, and can occur abruptly.

Polymyalgia Rheumatica (PMR) — the Strongly Associated Syndrome

  • Affects the shoulders, hips, and neck; associated morning stiffness and muscle pain
  • Patients often describe difficulty getting out of bed, rising, or brushing their hair/teeth
  • No associated swelling, erythema, or heat — this distinguishes it from a true inflammatory arthritis on exam
PMR diagnostic criteriaPain persisting ≥1 month, involving 2 of: neck, shoulders, pelvic girdle · morning stiffness >1 hour · rapid response to prednisone 20 mg/day or less · absence of other diseases causing MSK symptoms · age >50 · ESR >40 mm/hr.

Diagnosis

  • ESR often very elevated (frequently >40, often >80) — nonspecific; if normal, consider other diagnoses but still pursue biopsy if suspicion remains
  • Mild anemia may be present
  • Temporal artery biopsy is the gold standard — shows granulomatous inflammation with multinucleated giant cells (monocytes packed together, embedded in the internal elastic lamina)
  • Disease has a segmental nature — biopsy needs to be a long section, and a false negative can occur if an unaffected segment is sampled
  • Color Doppler ultrasound can aid diagnosis but is operator-dependent; PET, CT, or MRI are other imaging options

Treatment

Treat immediately — do not wait for biopsy results
  • High-dose corticosteroids — 40–60 mg/day prednisone; IV methylprednisolone 1000 mg/day for 3–5 days may be used for visual impairment
  • Steroid tapering begins after symptom resolution and normalization of inflammatory markers (per ACR guidelines)
  • Biologic: tocilizumab (targets IL-6); methotrexate sometimes used
The single most important management point in this whole guideDelaying treatment while awaiting biopsy results can cause permanent vision loss — empirical high-dose steroids should be started immediately on clinical suspicion, before biopsy confirmation.

02 · Takayasu Arteritis

Etiology, Epidemiology & Pathophysiology

"Pulseless disease" — segmental, patchy granulomatous vasculitis of the aortic arch and its branches
  • Chronic granulomatous inflammation of the aorta and its major branches — similar to GCA, but affecting the aortic arch and branching large arteries rather than the temporal branch of the carotid
  • Causes: unknown; possibly bacterial (spirochetes, M. tuberculosis, strep) or genetic
  • Risk factors: Asian descent, biologically female, age < 40

Symptoms & Signs

  • Vascular manifestations: asymmetric blood pressures, bruits, and claudication of the upper extremities (subclavian involvement, often unilateral)
  • Systemic symptoms (fever, weight loss, arthralgia) common during the inflammatory phase
  • Carotid/vertebral involvement → neurologic symptoms (headache, syncope, stroke); ocular vessel involvement → retinal hemorrhages; coronary involvement → angina; renal involvement → hypertension
  • Aortic regurgitation may develop from dilation of the aortic root

Diagnosis

  • Labs: elevated ESR and CRP
  • CT angiography (CTA) or MRA: luminal narrowing, vessel wall thickening, aortic valve disease, aneurysms/pseudoaneurysms
  • Ultrasound: homogeneous, circumferential vessel wall thickening (in contrast to atherosclerotic plaque), vascular occlusion, loss of pulsatility
  • Biopsy (sometimes): giant cells in the internal elastic lamina
ACR classification criteriaOnset age <40 · limb claudication · decreased brachial artery pulse · unequal arm blood pressures (>10 mmHg difference) · subclavian or aortic bruits · angiographic evidence of narrowing/occlusion of the aorta, its primary branches, or large limb arteries. Meeting 3 of 6 criteria is 91% sensitive and 98% specific for Takayasu arteritis.

Treatment

  • High-dose corticosteroids are the mainstay, tapered gradually as inflammation subsides
  • Immunosuppressive agents (methotrexate, azathioprine) often required for steroid-sparing effect
  • Treat hypertension; surgery (angioplasty when no acute inflammation, or bypass if severe) sometimes necessary
GCA vs. Takayasu — the side-by-sideBoth are large-vessel granulomatous vasculitides with giant cells on biopsy and a strong female predominance, but they split cleanly by age and ethnicity: GCA = age >50, Northern European descent, temporal/carotid branches. Takayasu = age <40, Asian descent, aortic arch and its branches ("pulseless disease" from unequal pulses/BPs).

03 · Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis (GPA)

Etiology & Pathophysiology

Formerly Wegener's granulomatosis — a small-vessel, C-ANCA-associated, necrotizing granulomatous disease of 3 organ systems
  • Affects small vessels (arterioles, capillaries, venules) — granulomatous disease of the respiratory tract with systemic necrotizing vasculitis
  • C-ANCA: B cells release antibodies that bind proteinase 3 (PR3), causing neutrophils to release oxygen free radicals → indirect endothelial damage
  • Non-caseating granulomas
  • 3 organ systems classically involved: upper respiratory tract (nasopharynx), lower respiratory tract, kidneys
  • Typically presents in middle-aged adults; affects men and women equally

Symptoms & Signs

  • Upper respiratory (90% of patients): chronic sinusitis, nasal crusting, epistaxis; cartilaginous inflammation can lead to septal perforation and saddle nose deformity; oral ulcers and "strawberry gums" (painful)
  • Lower respiratory (80% of patients): cough, hemoptysis, dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain; cavitary lung nodules on imaging; subglottic stenosis can occur
  • Renal (80% of patients): oliguria, hypertension, hematuria/red cell casts, rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis
  • Eyes: scleritis, episcleritis, conjunctivitis, orbital pseudotumors; ears: conductive and sensorineural hearing loss
  • Neurologic: mononeuritis multiplex; joints: migratory arthritis
Don't confuse with Goodpasture syndromeGPA involves 3 organ systems (upper respiratory, lower respiratory, kidney) and is C-ANCA-positive with granulomas. Goodpasture syndrome involves only the lower respiratory tract and glomerulonephritis, driven by anti-GBM antibodies rather than ANCA.

Diagnosis & Treatment

  • C-ANCA positive in ~90%; thrombocytopenia; abnormal urinary sediment/hematuria
  • Chest x-ray: cavitary lung nodules, fixed infiltrates, nodules
  • Diagnosis confirmed by biopsy (lung, kidney, or upper respiratory tract) showing granulomatous inflammation, vasculitis, and necrosis
  • Treatment: corticosteroids, cyclophosphamide, rituximab; relapses are common because C-ANCA can persist and continue attacking neutrophils
Practice Question

04 · Microscopic Polyangiitis (MPA)

Etiology & Pathophysiology

GPA's close cousin — same organs, minus the nose, minus the granulomas, plus a different ANCA
  • Necrotizing vasculitis affecting small vessels, typically without granulomatous inflammation
  • P-ANCA (perinuclear), reacting to myeloperoxidase (MPO) — instead of C-ANCA/PR3 as in GPA
  • No nasopharyngeal involvement — this is the key differentiator from GPA
  • The most common cause of pulmonary-renal syndrome (alveolar hemorrhage + glomerulonephritis)
  • Affects men and women equally, mean age of diagnosis ~60; can be precipitated by medications, especially penicillin
  • Has a tropism for kidneys and lungs

Symptoms & Signs

  • Renal: hematuria, oliguria, red cell casts, proteinuria, rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis
  • Skin: purpura leading to focal necrosis and ulceration

Diagnosis & Treatment

  • P-ANCA positive; elevated ESR/CRP; anemia; increased creatinine; urine protein and red cell casts
  • Pauci-immune glomerulonephritis — minimal immunofluorescent staining for IgG (distinguishes it from immune-complex-mediated glomerulonephritides)
  • Treatment (same as GPA): rituximab with glucocorticoids, or cyclophosphamide plus glucocorticoids, or plasma exchange; relapse is common

05 · Eosinophilic Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis (EGPA)

Etiology & the Three Phases

Formerly Churg-Strauss syndrome — asthma-associated, eosinophil-driven, small-medium vessel vasculitis
  • P-ANCA positive in only 50% of patients (unlike MPA, where P-ANCA positivity is much higher)
The three phases of EGPA
PhaseFindings
ProdromeAsthma and allergic rhinitis, often lasting several years
Eosinophilic tissue infiltrationPeripheral eosinophilia and tissue infiltration of the heart, lung, GI tract, and other tissues
Vasculitic phaseTrue vasculitis of the heart, lungs, peripheral nerves, and skin

Symptoms & Signs by System

  • Lungs (asthma in 90%): reactive airway disease, transient/migratory pulmonary infiltrates, occasional pulmonary hemorrhage
  • Peripheral nerves (77%): mononeuritis multiplex, presenting with foot drop or wrist drop
  • Heart: congestive heart failure — accounts for about half of EGPA deaths
  • Kidneys: less likely than GPA/MPA to progress to end-stage renal disease
  • Skin: palpable purpura, papules, ulcers, vesiculobullous lesions, nodular skin lesions, splinter hemorrhages, digital ischemia and gangrene
  • Upper respiratory: allergic rhinitis and nasal polyposis (the "allergic" component that precedes the vasculitis)
ACR diagnostic criteria (4 of 6 required)Asthma · eosinophilia · mononeuropathy or polyneuropathy · pulmonary infiltrates · paranasal sinus abnormalities · extravascular eosinophilia.

Diagnosis, Treatment & Prognosis

  • Labs: leukocytosis with eosinophilia, normochromic normocytic anemia, elevated serum IgE, elevated ESR/CRP, positive rheumatoid factor or ANA, normal-to-elevated complement, P-ANCA in 50%
  • X-ray: transient patchy alveolar opacities, nodular infiltrates, or diffuse interstitial pattern with bilateral hilar adenopathy
  • Studies: chest CT, sinus or cardiac MRI; bronchoalveolar lavage may show eosinophilia; biopsy of the least invasive affected site (skin, kidney, peripheral nerve, lacrimal gland); PFTs often show an obstructive pattern
  • Treatment: glucocorticoids, cyclophosphamide, mycophenolate mofetil, methotrexate, or mepolizumab
Prognosis5-year survival is ~25% without treatment versus ~50% with treatment — a stark illustration of why timely recognition and treatment matter in this disease.

07 · Polyarteritis Nodosa (PAN)

Etiology & Pathophysiology

A necrotizing, transmural, medium-vessel vasculitis that famously spares the lungs
  • Necrotizing inflammation of muscular arterioles and medium-sized arteries; spares the capillaries and veins; no granuloma formation
  • Direct attack on endothelium, thought to be triggered by immune complex deposits in the vessel wall that attract polymorphonuclear leukocytes
  • Transmural inflammation (intima, media, and adventitia) with fibrinoid necrosis — layers die and are replaced by fibrin, weakening the wall and producing aneurysms/microaneurysms
  • Risk factors: biological males, age >40, active hepatitis B or hepatitis C, HIV, prescription/illicit drug exposure (e.g., amphetamines) — ~30% of PAN patients are HBsAg-positive
  • Spares the pulmonary arteries — reason unknown

Symptoms & Signs

  • Organ ischemia predominates: renal arteries → hypertension, renal failure, hematuria; mesenteric arteries → postprandial abdominal pain, GI bleeding, mesenteric infarction/aneurysm; coronary arteries → acute MI
  • Skin: livedo reticularis, palpable purpura, lower extremity nodules/ulcerations (a cutaneous-limited variant exists)
  • Peripheral nerve ischemia → mononeuritis multiplex, often presenting with wrist or foot drop
  • Testicular ischemia/pain, myalgias, arthritis
ACR criteria (10 total; 3 needed)Unexplained weight loss >4 kg · livedo reticularis · testicular pain/tenderness · myalgias/weakness · new-onset diastolic BP >90 · elevated BUN or creatinine · evidence of hepatitis B infection · characteristic arteriographic abnormalities · biopsy evidence of PMN infiltration in medium/small arteries. Meeting 3 of these criteria is 82% sensitive and 87% specific.

Diagnosis & Treatment

  • The classic "string of beads" appearance on angiogram results from alternating stenosis and aneurysm formation in medium-sized arteries — classically seen in the hepatic artery, superior mesenteric artery, and renal arteries
  • Diagnosis requires angiographic evidence or biopsy of an involved organ (microaneurysms commonly identified in kidneys and GI tract)
  • Treatment: glucocorticoids and cyclophosphamide, plus antivirals when hepatitis B-associated
Clinical differentiation from cutaneous PANA cutaneous PAN variant exists in which vasculitis is limited to the skin, presenting as nodules that break down into ulcers — subacute onset with constitutional symptoms, lower extremity nodules/ulcerations, mononeuritis multiplex, and intestinal angina are typical of the systemic form.

08 · Buerger's Disease (Thromboangiitis Obliterans)

Etiology & Pathophysiology

"Clot" + "vessel" + "inflammation" + "blockage" — a smoking-driven medium-vessel disease of the digits
  • Clots form in vessels supplying the fingers and toes (tibial and radial arteries)
  • Blood vessel wall integrity remains intact, even though inflammatory cell infiltration is present — the inflammatory process extends to adjacent arteries, veins, and nerves
  • Fibrosis encases these structures together, leading to ulcers, dead tissue, and autoamputation
  • Risk factors: men, age 20–40; higher incidence in Ashkenazi Jewish populations and in Israel, Japan, and India; tobacco use is the trigger — smoking cessation slows (but does not necessarily stop) progression

Symptoms, Diagnosis & Treatment

  • Claudication and pain in the forefoot; digit-predominant disease with painful digits, gangrene, tissue loss, and autoamputation
  • Raynaud phenomenon is common but nonspecific (also seen in scleroderma and lupus)
  • ~60% of patients have an abnormal Allen test
  • Skin: superficial thrombophlebitis, splinter hemorrhages, gangrene of distal tissue
  • Diagnosis: ankle-brachial index; angiography shows a "corkscrew" appearance from segmental thrombus-driven inflammation and abrupt vascular cutoffs to the digits
  • Treatment: smoking cessation — the only intervention shown to meaningfully alter disease course
Practice Question
A 32-year-old man with a 15-pack-year smoking history presents with painful, ischemic-appearing toes and a positive Allen test. Angiography shows corkscrew collaterals and abrupt vascular cutoffs to the digits. What is the single most important treatment intervention?

09 · Mixed Cryoglobulinemia

Etiology & Pathophysiology

Small/medium vessel vasculitis driven by abnormal immunoglobulins that precipitate in the cold
  • Cryoglobulins are abnormal immunoglobulins that stick together at low temperatures
  • Almost always associated with hepatitis C (~90%); other associations include lupus, RA, Sjögren's, Lyme disease, mononucleosis, and HIV/AIDS, plus conditions with increased lymphocytes (multiple myeloma, lymphoid leukemia, lymphoma)
  • More common in females (3:1) and most frequently presents at age >50

Symptoms, Diagnosis & Treatment

  • Cryoglobulins deposit in small blood vessels of the skin → palpable purpura, cyanosis of the nose/ears/digits, and Raynaud phenomenon
  • Kidneys: small arteries supplying the glomerulus are affected → glomerulonephritis
  • Peripheral nerves: peripheral neuropathy
  • Diagnosis: HCV serology, cryoglobulins, urinalysis; imaging depends on symptoms; biopsy or nerve conduction studies as needed
  • Treatment: treat the underlying HCV, corticosteroids, immunosuppression if vital organs are affected (azathioprine, cyclophosphamide), and biologics (rituximab) or plasmapheresis if very severe
Practice Question
A 58-year-old woman with chronic hepatitis C presents with palpable purpura, Raynaud phenomenon, and new peripheral neuropathy. Which underlying process links these findings?

10 · Cutaneous Small-Vessel Vasculitis (CSVV)

Etiology & Pathophysiology

Also called leukocytoclastic, hypersensitivity, or allergic vasculitis — a skin-limited diagnosis of exclusion
  • Typically triggered by medications (penicillin, cephalosporin, phenytoin, allopurinol, NSAIDs, loop and thiazide diuretics) roughly 7–10 days after exposure, or by infections (strep pyogenes, hepatitis C, HIV)
  • Any age, generally >16; affects all genders equally
  • Usually confined to the skin, but systemic involvement (when present) affects joints, GI tract, and kidneys

Diagnosis & Treatment

  • Palpable purpura typically on the lower extremities and buttocks, appearing over days to weeks and resolving as the trigger clears; can become relapsing/remitting
  • It is essential to exclude other causes of vasculitis before making this diagnosis — CBC, electrolytes, LFTs, urinalysis, RF, ANCA, C3/C4, hepatitis serologies, cryoglobulins, and chest x-ray are typically normal
  • Skin biopsy: perivascular inflammation of small vessels, disruption/destruction of the vessel wall, fibrinoid necrosis, extravasation of RBCs, and leukocytoclasis (predominance of neutrophils and nuclear debris)
ACR diagnostic criteria (3 of 5 required)Age at onset >16 · medication at disease onset · palpable purpura · maculopapular rash · biopsy showing granulocytes in a perivascular or extravascular location.
Treatment mirrors the "remove the trigger" themeMild disease: conservative measures (leg elevation, NSAIDs, antihistamines). Persistent disease: colchicine, hydroxychloroquine, or dapsone. Refractory cases: glucocorticoids. Overall, treatment is generally supportive, and the disease usually clears spontaneously once the trigger is addressed.
Practice Question
A 24-year-old man develops palpable purpura on his lower legs one week after starting a new antibiotic. Basic labs (CBC, LFTs, UA, ANCA, complement) are all normal, and skin biopsy shows leukocytoclasis. What is the most appropriate first step in management?

11 · Behçet's Disease

Etiology & Pathophysiology

The only vasculitis in this guide affecting vessels of all sizes, both arterial and venous
  • Rare; named for Hulusi Behçet, a Turkish pathologist; strong predilection for Turkish and Mediterranean populations
  • Unique among vasculitides in affecting blood vessels of all sizes — both arterial and venous
  • Cause unknown; HLA-B51 is strongly implicated; may follow HSV or parvovirus infection; involves altered T-helper cell and cytokine levels, with lymphocytes infiltrating vessel walls

Symptoms & Signs

  • Can affect every organ system; oral ulcers (aphthous, painful sores) are the most typical initial symptom — new ulcers can appear before previous ones heal, giving a continuously-present pattern
  • Genital ulcers — painful, involving the anus, vulva, or scrotum
  • Eyes: anterior uveitis (painful, with conjunctivitis; hypopyon = sterile pus in the anterior chamber) or posterior uveitis (painless, with visual field floaters, sometimes optic nerve involvement)
  • Skin: erythema nodosum (inflammation of subcutaneous fat)
  • Other: joint stiffness, abdominal pain, meningitis/meningoencephalitis, kidney disease, and the most severe complications — CNS involvement and large-vessel vasculitis

Diagnosis & Treatment

  • Primarily a clinical diagnosis — no specific laboratory findings for the disease
  • Pathergy test: a pin-prick fails to heal normally within 1–2 days, instead causing an ulcer, papule, or pustule
  • Recurrent oral ulcerations are defined as occurring 3 times over 12 months, plus 2 or more of: recurrent genital ulcerations, uveitis/retinal vasculitis, skin lesions, or a positive pathergy test
  • Treatment: topical steroids or dapsone for recurrent oral ulcers; high-dose systemic steroids and anti-TNF inhibitors for vision-threatening uveitis or meningoencephalitis; corticosteroids, biologic TNF blockers, and IV immunoglobulins more broadly
Practice Question
A young woman of Turkish descent has had recurrent painful oral ulcers 4 times in the past year, along with two episodes of genital ulceration and one episode of painful anterior uveitis with hypopyon. A pin-prick test produces a pustule within 24 hours. What is the diagnosis, and what test supported it?

12 · IgA Vasculitis (Henoch-Schönlein Purpura)

Etiology & Pathophysiology

The most common systemic vasculitis of childhood, and the exception to the "small vessel = indirect damage" rule
  • IgA deposition in small vessel walls, typically following an upper respiratory infection (viral URI or group A strep) by about 10 days in roughly two-thirds of patients
  • 90% of cases occur in children — the most common systemic vasculitis of childhood
  • No ANCA — instead, increased IgA antibodies (from mucosal cells exposed to the outside environment) directly target the endothelial cells, likely via molecular mimicry — direct damage, unlike most other small-vessel vasculitides

Symptoms, Diagnosis & Treatment

The classic tetradPalpable purpura (skin blood vessels of the buttocks and legs; petechiae progressing to palpable purpura from fibrosis) · arthritis (migratory arthralgia, periarticular disease of the knees and ankles) · abdominal pain (colicky, with GI bleeding in ~33%; hematoma in the intestinal wall can cause intussusception) · glomerulonephritis (hematuria; can progress to IgA nephropathy, aka Berger's disease — not to be confused with Buerger's disease).
  • Skin pathology: leukocytoclastic vasculitis of small vessels in the superficial dermis, with necrosis but no granulomas; immunofluorescence shows coarse granular IgA deposition
  • Classic presentation: acute-onset fever, palpable purpura on the lower extremities/buttocks, abdominal pain with GI bleeding, arthritis, and hematuria
  • Diagnosis can be confirmed with direct immunofluorescence and conventional biopsy staining, but in children, treatment is often based on clinical presentation alone
  • Treatment: largely self-limited, usually resolving within 6–8 weeks in children; glucocorticoids and immunosuppressive therapy for severe cases
Berger's vs. Buerger's — a classic name mix-upIgA nephropathy is also called Berger's disease — completely unrelated to Buerger's disease (thromboangiitis obliterans, Section 08). Same-sounding names, entirely different pathology; know which spelling goes with which disease.
Practice Question
A 6-year-old boy develops palpable purpura on his buttocks and legs, colicky abdominal pain, and hematuria about 10 days after an upper respiratory infection. His ANCA is negative. What is the diagnosis, and what is the expected disease course?

13 · Primary CNS Vasculitis & Infectious Vasculitides

Primary CNS Vasculitis

A rare, single-organ vasculitis that requires tissue for diagnosis
  • Primarily presents with headaches and stroke-like symptoms
  • Diagnosis requires brain biopsy since imaging alone is nonspecific
  • Angiography demonstrates alternating stenosis and ectasia
  • Treatment: high-dose corticosteroids
Secondary CNS vasculitis — rule these out firstEtiologies for secondary vasculitis of the CNS include HIV, herpes zoster, hepatitis C, Behçet's disease, polyarteritis nodosa, cocaine use, and the ANCA-associated vasculitides — primary CNS vasculitis is a diagnosis of exclusion within the broader CNS vasculitis differential.

Infectious Vasculitides

Several infections can cause vasculitis or a vasculitis-like presentation — the primary management approach is always treating the underlying infection

14 · Clinical Reasoning & Pattern Recognition

Step 1: Start With Vessel Size

The organizing framework for the entire clinical reasoning process
  • Large vessel → Giant Cell Arteritis (GCA), Takayasu
  • Medium vessel → Polyarteritis Nodosa (PAN), Buerger Disease
  • Small vessel → GPA, MPA, EGPA, IgA Vasculitis, Cryoglobulinemia, CSVV
  • Variable vessel → Behçet Disease

Clinical Reasoning Algorithm

The sequenceInflammatory symptoms → determine vessel size → identify involved organs → order targeted labs (ESR/CRP, ANCA, UA, hepatitis testing, cryoglobulins as indicated) → confirm with imaging or biopsy → start disease-specific treatment.

High-Yield Pearls

  • GCA: treat immediately — do NOT delay corticosteroids for biopsy
  • GPA = ENT + lungs + kidneys + C-ANCA
  • MPA resembles GPA but has NO upper airway disease and NO granulomas
  • EGPA = adult-onset asthma + eosinophilia
  • PAN spares the lungs
  • Cryoglobulinemia is strongly associated with hepatitis C
  • Buerger disease: smoking cessation is the cornerstone of treatment

Common Student Mistakes

  • Memorizing diseases instead of recognizing patterns
  • Forgetting to classify by vessel size first
  • Confusing GPA and MPA
  • Missing GCA as an ophthalmologic emergency
  • Ordering broad testing instead of hypothesis-driven testing
Take-home messageThink in patterns, not lists. Vessel size, organ involvement, characteristic clinical features, and a few high-yield laboratory findings will lead you to the correct diagnosis in most patients.
