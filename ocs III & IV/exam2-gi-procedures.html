3. Nasogastric (NG) Intubation (slides 9–15)

NG intubation is the passage of a tube through the nares and esophagus into the stomach. He assigns an NG tube insertion video.

ItemContent
IndicationsEvacuate blood; decompress the stomach and upper GI tract; remove toxic substances; provide enteral feedings
EquipmentNG tube, lubricant, cup of water with a straw, tape, large syringe
ContraindicationsFacial trauma, basilar skull fracture, bilateral nasal obstruction, recent nasal, pharyngeal, esophageal or gastric surgery, bleeding diathesis
ComplicationsBleeding, aspiration, tracheal intubation (tube in the airway), erosion of the naris (long term), pharyngeal or nasal perforation
Why basilar skull fracture and facial trauma?A blind nasal tube could enter the cranium or injure facial structures, so they are contraindications. beyond source

Types of tubes

Levin tube: one lumen, usually PVC, several drainage holes near the gastric end, graduated markings to see how far it has been inserted.
Levin tube (slide 14)
Salem sump tube: two lumens: a drainage lumen and a smaller second lumen open to the atmosphere. Allows continuous suction and prevents gastric mucosa from being sucked into the tube (less gastric bleeding).
Salem sump tube (slide 15)
Levin vs SalemLevin = one lumen. Salem sump = two lumens, second one vented to air, protects the mucosa.

4. Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) (slides 16–19)

A flexible endoscope goes through the mouth (or, with smaller-caliber scopes, the nose) and passes the pharynx, esophagus, stomach and duodenum. EGD is both diagnostic and therapeutic. Instruments passed through the scope allow a biopsy (small tissue sample for microscopy) or removal of a polyp or tumor with a thin wire snare and electrocautery.

ItemContent
IndicationsDiagnostic: signs or symptoms of upper GI disease (dyspepsia, dysphagia, noncardiac chest pain, recurrent emesis). Surveillance for upper GI cancer in high-risk settings (Barrett’s esophagus, polyposis syndromes). Biopsy for known or suspected disease (malabsorption, neoplasms, infections). Therapeutic: foreign body retrieval, control of hemorrhage, dilation or stenting of stricture, excision of neoplasms, gastrostomy tube placement
Absolute contraindicationsKnown or suspected perforation, medically unstable patients, obstruction
Relative contraindicationsAnticoagulation, pharyngeal diverticulum, recent head or neck surgery, esophageal stricture
ComplicationsBleeding, infection, perforation, vocal cord injury, pharyngeal irritation, cardiopulmonary events
High-yield numberCardiopulmonary events are 50% of all major EGD complications, usually caused by the medications used for conscious sedation.

Esophagus

Normal esophagus (slide 20)
Esophageal varices (slide 21)
FindingKey points
Esophageal varicesCirrhosis raises intrahepatic vascular resistance (architectural distortion, deficient nitric oxide). Portal hypertension = increased intrahepatic resistance + splanchnic arteriolar vasodilation. Varices form in the esophagus and stomach by dilation of existing vessels and angiogenesis, enlarge with severity, and rupture and bleed past a maximal pressure. Look tortuous.
EsophagitisRisk factors: alcohol, smoking, chest surgery or radiation, certain medications (tetracycline, doxycycline, vitamin C, aspirin), prolonged vomiting, weakened immunity (HIV, corticosteroids), fungi or viruses
Barrett’s esophagusAdaptation to chronic acid reflux: columnar epithelium replaces squamous. Premalignant. Highest malignant transformation in Caucasian men >50 with >5 years of symptoms
Esophageal cancerSquamous cell: proximal and mid esophagus. Adenocarcinoma: distal esophagus. 20–30 times higher rate in China. Risk factors: smoking, ethanol, high-fat/low-protein diet, GERD or Barrett’s. Metastasis: lungs, pleura, liver, stomach, peritoneum, kidneys, adrenal gland

6. Flexible Sigmoidoscopy (slides 31–35)

Examines the rectum and lower (sigmoid) colon under direct visualization. The scope is a flexible tube 60 cm long, about 1.25 cm in diameter, inserted through the anus and advanced slowly.

ItemContent
IndicationsColorectal cancer screening; preoperative evaluation before anorectal surgery; surveillance of previously diagnosed malignancy (or high-grade dysplasia polyp) in the rectum or sigmoid; local treatment such as radiation proctitis; removal of rectal foreign bodies; therapeutic procedures (endoluminal stent for strictures, balloon dilation, decompression tube); hematochezia requiring hemostasis
Absolute contraindicationsBowel perforation, acute diverticulitis, active peritonitis, fulminant colitis, cardiopulmonary instability
Relative contraindicationsLack of informed consent (except in emergencies), lack of patient cooperation, lack of good bowel preparation
ComplicationsPerforation, infection, bleeding, pain

7. Colonoscopy (slides 36–45)

Sigmoidoscope vs colonoscopeSigmoidoscope = 60 cm. Colonoscope = 130–170 cm. The advantage is finding and removing polyps beyond the reach of the sigmoidoscope.

The patient is mildly sedated, the scope is inserted through the anus and advanced around the bends. A polyp is removed with a thin wire snare; electrocautery painlessly controls bleeding; biopsy can also be done.

ItemContent
IndicationsColorectal cancer screening in average-risk adults; evaluation and removal of polyps; current or previous bowel resection for colon cancer; family history of cancer; management of inflammatory bowel disease; identification of acute bleeding sites; decompression of the colon
Absolute contraindicationsFulminant colitis, known or suspected perforation, early post-colectomy period
Relative contraindicationsHistory of radiation therapy for abdominal or pelvic cancer, history of abdominal or pelvic malignancy, extensive adhesions from prior abdominal surgery, bleeding dyscrasias, anticoagulant therapy
ComplicationsPerforation, infection, bleeding, respiratory depression, cardiac arrhythmias or ischemia, transient bacteremia, nausea/vomiting, ileus
PreparationThe rectum and lower colon must be completely clean of stool: dietary modification and osmotic laxatives (applies to sigmoidoscopy too)

Imaging of the small bowel (slides 50–51)

TestPoints
Magnetic resonance enterography (MRE)Small bowel and extra-intestinal findings (fistula, abscess, liver, renal, bone). Avoids radiation from CT and UGI/small bowel follow-through. High performance for active inflammation
CT enterography (CTE)Same concept, used in more urgent situations or when MRE is unavailable. Not first line because of radiation
Video capsule endoscopy (VCE)Increasingly used in adults to diagnose and assess Crohn’s severity; identifies small bowel involvement (affects treatment); in children evolving, FDA-approved over age 10

9. Virtual Colonoscopy (CT Colonography) (slides 52–54)

  • Imaging with computers to produce two- and three-dimensional images of the colon from the rectum to the distal ileum.
  • Used to diagnose polyps, diverticulosis and cancer.
  • Performed with x-rays: computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

10. Colorectal Cancer Screening Tests: Advantages and Disadvantages (slides 55–69)

Seven tests: fecal occult blood test (FOBT), stool DNA, sigmoidoscopy, colonoscopy, colonography (virtual colonoscopy), double contrast barium enema (DCBE), digital rectal exam (DRE).

TestAdvantagesDisadvantages
FOBTNo bowel prep; samples at home; low cost; no risk of bleeding or perforationMisses most polyps and some cancers; false positives common: very sensitive but not specific (guaiac, from Guaiacum tree resin, reacts with iron); dietary restrictions (meat, some vegetables, vitamin C, iron, aspirin); repeat 3–4 times; colonoscopy if abnormal
Stool DNA (Cologuard)No special prep, diet or medication change; at home; no time off workColonoscopy still needed if abnormal
Flexible sigmoidoscopyQuick, few complications, minimal discomfort; biopsy and polyp removal possible; less cleansing than colonoscopyViews only the rectum and lower colon; polyps in the upper colon are missed; very small risk of bleeding or perforation; colonoscopy if abnormal
ColonoscopyEntire colon; biopsy and polyp removalMay miss small polyps, nonpolypoid lesions and cancers, but one of the most sensitive tests; thorough prep; sedation; uncommon bleeding, perforation
Virtual colonoscopyEntire colon; 10–15 minutes; no anesthesia; non-invasive, little bleeding or perforation risk; every 5 years (average risk, 45+)May miss small lesions; thorough prep; polyp or lesion 6–9 mm or larger needs immediate standard colonoscopy; rectal gas insufflation: very uncomfortable, takes days to resolve
Double contrast barium enema (DCBE)Usually views the entire colon; rare complications; no sedation; every 5 yearsMay miss small polyps and cancers; thorough prep; false positives; no biopsy or polypectomy; additional procedures if abnormal
Digital rectal exam (DRE)Part of a routine exam; no cleansing; quick, painlessDetects abnormalities only in the lower rectum

Cologuard (slide 59)FDA-approved 2014; ages 45+ at average risk; repeat every 3 years.
IntervalsVirtual colonoscopy and DCBE: every 5 years.
