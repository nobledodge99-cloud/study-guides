00. Overview & Learning Objectives

  • Define adiposity-based chronic disease (ABCD) and differentiate it from "obesity" as classically described
  • Examine markers that help in diagnosing this disease entity
  • Examine the health consequences of ABCD
  • Enable physicians to implement changes that might effectively treat this problem
Why this matters: An estimated 43% of the population is currently overweight or obese, with a projected rise to 50% by the year 2030. Finding more effective diagnostic and treatment frameworks is essential to addressing this epidemic.
Practice Question

01. Key Definitions

  • Adiposity: Refers to all sites of intracellular fat deposition, involving both healthy and unhealthy pathophysiologic mechanisms. Adipose cells themselves are not inherently pathologic.
  • Adiposity-Based Chronic Disease (ABCD): A chronic disease state that is causally associated with abnormalities in adiposity. This occurs when adipose cells become dysfunctional and secrete substances that cause a chronic disease state, primarily through inflammation.
  • Adipokines: Molecules produced by fat cells to maintain homeostasis. These can be normal or pathologic — pathologic adipokines include pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1 (IL-1), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α).

04. Why a New Diagnosis? Limits of BMI & "Obesity"

  • The term "obesity" carries significant negative connotation — when discussed with patients, it often generates resentment and can undermine the physician-patient relationship, even though it is medically an accurate diagnosis
  • BMI alone is limited in predicting poor outcomes — some patients in the overweight or obese BMI category remain healthy with no apparent poor outcomes (sometimes described in the literature as "healthy obesity" versus "unhealthy obesity," though this distinction is controversial and lacks general consensus)
  • Changing social norms may cast doubt on the urgency and severity of health risks — patients told they are "obese" may feel normal or happy and not understand the urgency, causing them to shy away from treatment
  • ABCD emphasizes the unhealthy nature of adiposity beyond a simple BMI-based category, which may help patients engage more effectively with treatment

05. Pathophysiology: Functional → Dysfunctional Adipose Tissue

  • Functional adipose tissue can become dysfunctional due to chronic overfeeding, intestinal dysbiosis, and genetic susceptibilities
  • Dysfunctional adipose tissue develops an altered adipokine secretion profile: decreased adiponectin, increased leptin, increased pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as TNF-α and IL-6), and increased free fatty acid (FFA) release
  • This leads to ectopic fat deposition and lipotoxicity
  • Downstream consequences include insulin resistance, hyperinsulinemia, endothelial dysfunction, and a wide range of obesity-related diseases
Board tip: Adipose cells are not pathologic by default — it is the transition to a dysfunctional, pro-inflammatory secretory state (driven by overfeeding, dysbiosis, and genetics) that drives disease.

07. Treatment Framework: Domains of Intervention

Because ABCD is a total-body disease affecting nearly every organ system, effective treatment requires addressing multiple domains simultaneously — not just diet and exercise:

  1. Intensive lifestyle modification (diet + physical activity)
  2. Sleep hygiene
  3. Stress reduction
  4. Antibiotic use (minimizing unnecessary prescribing)
  5. Alcohol moderation
  6. Treatment of mood disorders
  7. Community engagement
  8. Recognizing cultural differences
Practice Question

08. Lifestyle Modification: Diet

Historical context: Fifty years ago, diets consisted largely of home-cooked meals prepared from scratch, and activity levels were higher (more walking, fewer automobiles, less suburban sprawl). Today, convenience foods and dual-income households have shifted eating patterns considerably.

Recommended dietary pattern (closely resembling the Mediterranean diet):

  • High in fruits, vegetables, nuts, and complex carbohydrates
  • Controlled amounts of fish and meat
  • Negligible processed foods and sugars

Common barriers include the convenience of pre-prepared or frozen foods (e.g., from big-box retailers) and high sugar intake from soft drinks, candy, and sweets — both of which should be regulated, though not necessarily eliminated entirely.

Practice Question

09. Lifestyle Modification: Physical Activity

  • Minimum recommended activity: 150 minutes per week of vigorous activity; ideally 150–300 minutes per week
  • A mix of both aerobic and anaerobic exercise is recommended:
    • Aerobic exercise promotes cardiovascular fitness
    • Anaerobic exercise promotes buildup of lean muscle mass

10. Sleep & Metabolic Regulation

  • Decreased sleep is associated with increased BMI and increased caloric intake
  • Sleep affects metabolic regulation through multiple pathways: hypothalamic function, cortisol release, thyroid function, hepatic glucose production, brown fat activation, and insulin resistance
  • Quality of sleep matters as much as quantity — multiple studies indicate people are getting less sleep today than 20 years ago, and insomnia/nighttime awakening further impairs sleep quality even when time in bed is adequate
PopulationRecommended Sleep
Adults7–9 hours per night
Adolescents8–10 hours per night
Counseling tip: Most adults do not currently meet the 7-hour minimum — giving patients a concrete sleep-duration goal can be a useful and achievable target.

11. Stress: Physiologic Effects & Treatment

Human Response to Stress

  • Hypothalamic dysfunction
  • Increased consumption of palatable, calorie-dense food
  • Reduction in physical activity
Both increased caloric-dense food intake and reduced physical activity are directly counterproductive to a healthy lifestyle — making stress management an important treatment target in its own right.

Treatment of Stress

  • Avoid stressful situations when practical — easier said than done, but anticipating predictable stressors can allow patients to plan alternate approaches
  • Stress-reduction techniques — mindfulness training has been studied and shown to improve both serum markers and weight
  • Regular low-intensity exercise — such as yoga — has also been shown to improve stress markers and weight, without requiring high-intensity activity

12. Antibiotics: Use, Risks & the GI Microbiome

  • Antibiotic prescriptions are extremely common in the United States — many patients request antibiotics even for viral illnesses where they are not indicated
  • Antibiotics affect GI microflora, which plays an important role in the metabolism of sugar and fiber, and influences insulin resistance, systemic inflammation, and metabolic rate
  • Destroying these microflora can increase insulin resistance, increase systemic inflammation, and change metabolic rate
Evidence caveat: Studies linking antibiotic use (particularly chronic childhood antibiotic exposure, often studied in European pediatric populations) to later obesity are not definitive due to confounding factors. The data is suggestive but not yet conclusive — avoid presenting this as an equally well-established factor alongside core lifestyle interventions like diet and exercise.

Takeaway for physicians: Reduce unnecessary antibiotic prescribing and educate patients on when antibiotics are helpful, when they are not, and how they can be harmful.

13. Alcohol Moderation

Risks of Alcohol Use

  • Dependence (alcohol is an addictive drug)
  • Cognitive impairment (falls, impaired driving)
  • Fatty liver, cirrhosis
  • Weight and vitamin deficiencies

Potential Benefits of Moderate Consumption

Studies (primarily involving red wine) suggest moderate consumption may:

  • Improve markers of insulin resistance
  • Improve cholesterol levels
  • Decrease systemic inflammation
SexModerate Consumption
Men14 or fewer drinks per week
Women9 or fewer drinks per week
Anything above these thresholds is considered excess/high consumption and should be discouraged. Patients often encounter conflicting information online, so being prepared to discuss both the risks and the moderate-consumption data is important.
Practice Questions

14. Mood Disorders: Bidirectional Relationship & Treatment

  • Mood disorders can alter hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis signaling, increase oxidative stress, and increase systemic inflammation — tying directly back to the adipose dysfunction pathway described in Section 05

Treatment Options

  • Medication — a double-edged sword: many antidepressants, antipsychotics, and anti-anxiety medications can treat the mood disorder but also increase appetite and cause weight gain, potentially exacerbating the overweight/obesity issue
  • Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) — has been shown to be very effective, and is worth considering before reaching for a prescription pad
Practice Questions

15. Community Engagement

Family Engagement

Getting the entire family involved is important, since obesity/overweight problems are often familial — when parents are overweight or obese, children frequently become so at a young age as well. Getting the whole family to buy into a lifestyle change is often the most effective approach.

Barriers to family engagement:

  • Parental concerns for the child's psychosocial well-being
  • Parental denial or lack of recognition that a problem exists (often because the pattern is familial and normalized)

Faith-Based / Church-Based Engagement

When family engagement is unsuccessful, church-based or faith-based group engagement has been shown to be a successful alternative — worth considering as an option depending on what is available in the local community.

Practice Questions

16. Cultural Differences

  • Cultural background has a significant impact on lifestyle choices and acceptance of medical advice
  • Some cultures place less trust in formal medical advice, preferring guidance from family elders or traditional sources
  • In some cultures, a larger body size may be viewed as a sign of health and success rather than a health risk
  • Traditional measures of obesity used in one population (e.g., Caucasian populations) may not apply equally to others (e.g., Asian or Indian populations) — cultural and population-specific context should inform how measures are interpreted
Takeaway: Physicians must consider a patient's cultural background when discussing lifestyle change — failing to do so risks losing engagement with many patients.
Practice Question

17. Conclusion & Osteopathic Perspective

  • As osteopathic physicians, it is extremely important to look at the total patient and the total picture when treating ABCD
  • Effective treatment requires intensive lifestyle changes addressing every domain discussed: diet, physical activity, sleep, stress, antibiotic use, alcohol, mood disorders, community engagement, and cultural context
  • This lecture defined a new diagnostic category within the overweight/obesity epidemic, summarized the associated health risks, and examined treatment modalities — with an emphasis on ABCD rather than BMI alone
