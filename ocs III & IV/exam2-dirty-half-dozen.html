1. Overview: Why the Dirty Half Dozen?

Dr. Langevin’s framingUntil now you have learned to find a somatic dysfunction and treat it. The Dirty Half Dozen (DHD) is “your first introduction” to what to do when a patient comes in with a complaint (low back pain): a format to approach and treat the patient with OMT, and a lesson in the biomechanics behind it. Source: Greenman’s Principles of Manual Medicine, 5th ed., ch. 20, Low Back Pain Syndromes, pp. 472–493; Greenman, Phys Med Rehab Clin 1996.

Learning objectives (slide 2)

  1. Describe the 6 primary areas of somatic dysfunction in persistent mechanical low back pain.
  2. Describe the most common musculoskeletal patterns for each and their prevalence.
  3. Describe form and force closure of the pelvis.
  4. Describe how these dysfunctions contribute to instability and pain.
  5. Describe the evaluation and diagnostic criteria for each.
  6. Describe the suggested treatment sequence.
The six (slide 7), in treatment order
  1. Pubic symphysis dysfunction
  2. Innominate shear dysfunction
  3. Restriction of anterior nutation of the sacral base (posterior torsion or posteriorly nutated/extended sacrum)
  4. Non-neutral (type II) lumbar dysfunction, mainly FRS in the lower lumbar and thoracolumbar spine
  5. Short-leg, pelvic-tilt syndrome
  6. Muscle imbalance of the trunk and lower extremities

Why low back pain matters (slides 4–5)

  • Low back pain is in the top three reasons people see a doctor (primary, acute and emergency care) and is a huge occupational medicine problem.
  • Cost: low back plus neck pain topped US health spending at $134.5 billion (2016 data: private 57%, public 34%, out of pocket 9%); low back pain alone was $52.9 billion (2019 data). Including lost productivity, the total burden is estimated at $100–300 billion or more.
  • Mean back pain–related cost is often $7,000+ in the first year after diagnosis and rises with more procedures (injections, ablations, imaging) and for high-impact chronic pain (pain that substantially restricts work, social life or self-care on most days), which drives disproportionate cost.
  • Lumbar fusion costs about $80–90k or more. Its goal is to stabilize the spine and prevent further neurological compromise (radiculopathy, urinary or rectal incontinence), not to cure low back pain.

2. The Universal Compensatory Pattern (slide 6)

Not the Zink patternGreenman’s universal (common) compensatory pattern is NOT the common compensatory pattern of Zink (L/R/L/R). Living on this ball under gravity, most people show these findings, so finding one does not mean it is pathological.
RegionTypical findings
FootPronated right foot
InnominatesAnteriorly rotated right innominate; posteriorly rotated left innominate
PubisRight inferior pubis; left superior pubis
SacrumLeft-on-left sacral torsion
SpineRight lower thoracic scoliosis; left lumbar scoliosis; left cervical scoliosis
Shoulder girdleAnterior and inferior right shoulder girdle

Normal pattern vs pathology

  • In the universal pattern the segments are springy and mobile: they just favor the position. A finding that moves through its range like the other segments is most likely not pathological.
  • A finding that is restricted or “rock hard” is most likely pathological.
  • When somatic dysfunction does not follow the pattern (for example a posteriorly rotated right innominate, or a left inferior pubis), it is more clinically significant: patients have pain, an abnormal gait and hip or low back problems.

4. Form and Force Closure of the Pelvis (slides 15–16)

Dr. Langevin“This is a very important concept”: a phylogenetic event in us bipeds, and it is essential that we have normal form and force closure.
Form closure (A) and force closure (B) (slide 15)
Sacrum: wedge shape and articular surface (slide 15)
Form closureForce closure
What it isThe anatomical structure: the sacrum’s shape keeps the pelvic ring stable (static stability)The interaction of ligaments and muscles across the SI joint that absorbs forces (dynamic stability)
HowSI joint surfaces are flat and prone to shear, but the wedge shape protects from shearing, like a keystone in an arch: wider superiorly than inferiorly (coronal plane) and wider anteriorly than posteriorly (horizontal plane)Ground force is absorbed by soft tissue, not bone: the sacrum nutates forward and the SI joint closes the ring

The force closure chain (heel strike)

MemorizePeroneus longus → biceps femoris → sacrotuberous ligament → ipsilateral SI joint → lumbosacral (thoracolumbar) fascia → contralateral latissimus dorsi, with the gluteus maximus firing to complete the loop. Force crosses to the opposite side through soft tissue.
Posterior fascial sling: gluteus maximus, thoracolumbar fascia, latissimus (slide 16)
Nutation and counternutation (slide 16)
  • Closure needs only about 4–6 mm of motion, even in a sprint, but it protects the ring.
  • Anterior nutation of the sacral base is also a trigger for the gluteus maximus: as the sacrum comes forward, the glute is stretched and fires maximally, which lets you extend the hip properly.
  • If the SI joint is held posteriorly (counternutation): the ring stays open, the glute does not fire fully, force does not cross over, and forces stay on one side and load bones and joints that were not meant to absorb them, causing pain and instability over years.

5. DHD #1: Pubic Symphysis Dysfunction (slide 11)

Treat this firstA dysfunctional pubic symphysis alters mechanics and can give a false diagnosis of the innominates, sacrum or lumbar spine, but the reverse is not true (treating the sacrum does not fix the pubic symphysis). Treating it first gives a “good clean” exam of the rest. Address it early in any low back, pelvic or SI complaint.

75% had pubic dysfunction: 32% right inferior and 25% left superior. The symphysis moves only millimeters, but the motion is essential. Dysfunction does not have to be a shear. Criteria: restricted, lacking spring, tender, or asymmetric; if it is restricted and tender it is dysfunctional.

TypeFindingAssociations
Superior pubic shear (for example left superior)Ipsilateral pubic tubercle higherIpsilateral posterior innominate rotation or upslip, psoas hypertonicity, lumbar type II
Inferior pubic shear (for example right inferior)Ipsilateral tubercle lowerOften with anterior innominate rotation or other shears
CompressionTender, reduced spring and mobility; “not moving well”Pregnancy, tight adductors or pelvic floor, trauma; Dr. Langevin sees it often
Diastasis (gapping)Excessive separation, palpable gap, instabilityPostpartum, especially prolonged vaginal labor, traumatic
Anterior/posterior pubic shearHorizontal displacement; subtle asymmetryRarely isolated; usually part of an innominate dysfunction

Treatment: restore symmetry and normal motion

  • Counterstrain is Dr. Langevin’s choice: “regardless of your diagnosis, you treat the tender spot and it will go away.”
  • Muscle energy: superior shear: patient supine with the leg extended off the table, isometric contraction into hip flexion plus slight adduction; inferior shear: hip and knee flexed, contact on the ischial tuberosity, isometric contraction into hip extension.
  • Others: myofascial release, HVLA if appropriate, articulatory; compression: frog-leg isometric abduction (decompression).
  • Always recheck landmarks and treat associated dysfunctions (innominate, sacrum, lumbar type II, psoas); add home exercises (pelvic tilts, adductor/abductor isometrics, core).

6. DHD #2: Innominate Shear Dysfunction (slides 12–13)

24% had innominate shear: 19% superior, 4% inferior (and they are very symptomatic). In a shear the entire innominate translates superiorly or inferiorly along the plane of the SI joint (a vertical force) with no rotation. This disrupts the pelvic ring’s coupled motion, alters load distribution, and produces compensation in the lumbar spine and lower extremity. SI shear reflexively inhibits the gluteus maximus, impairing force closure.

Shear vs pubic shear vs rotationInnominate shear: ASIS, PSIS and iliac crest all move the same direction. Pubic shear: only the pubic tubercle/ramus is displaced. Innominate rotation: ASIS and PSIS move in opposite directions.
FeatureSuperior shear (upslip)Inferior shear (downslip)Rotation (anterior/posterior)
LandmarksASIS + PSIS + crest all superiorASIS + PSIS + crest all inferiorASIS and PSIS move opposite ways
Leg lengthOften shorter on that sideOften longer on that sideVariable
ASIS compression testPositive ipsilaterally (the restricted side)Positive ipsilaterallyMay be positive
PrevalenceMore common (~19%)Less common (~4%)Separate category
Typical muscle energy vectorInferior traction on the legSuperior force or adduction positioningRotational isometric
Most specific findingsThe standing flexion test lateralizes to the side of the shear, plus ASIS/PSIS or iliac crest height asymmetry that persists from standing to supine, which strongly suggests shear rather than a simple rotation or short leg. Dr. Langevin uses the ASIS compression test (more restricted side = the side of the shear). Treatment: counterstrain, HVLA, myofascial, or muscle energy.

7. DHD #3: Sacral Dysfunction (slide 14)

Restriction of anterior nutation (the sacral base does not move forward: it is stuck in neutral or stuck posterior, in extension) was found in 48.6–48.8% (60% of post-operative patients).

Sacral findingPrevalence
Left-on-right torsion17.5%
Right-on-left torsion15.8%
Bilateral extension (extended base)15.3%
Unilateral extensionNot reported (probably too few)
Why it mattersA counternutated (extended) sacral base destabilizes pelvic force closure, and SI dysfunction reflexively inhibits the gluteus maximus, destabilizing the pelvis and lumbar spine. Sacral extension also keeps the lumbar spine in flexion. If the sacral nutation keeps returning, you have not treated everything (the lumbar spine and pelvic shears maintain it).

Evaluation: standing and seated flexion tests, sacral sulcus depth and inferior lateral angle (ILA) position, and the spring test of the sacral base (restricted anterior nutation). Treat after the pubic symphysis and the shears.

8. DHD #4: Non-Neutral (Type II) Lumbar Dysfunction (slide 17)

84% had type II (non-neutral) dysfunction at L4 or L5; most commonly a single segment, often with sacral nutation restriction (a lumbopelvic chain reaction).

PatternSlide valueMeaning
ERS (extended)34.9%Physiological compensation (the normal lordotic lower curve): these patients are less symptomatic from it
FRS (flexed)83.6%Non-physiological: this is the pathological one, and is what you treat
Fryette’s laws (slide 17)
Greenman: “recalcitrant drivers”These dysfunctions perpetuate abnormal loading through the SI joints and pubic symphysis, contribute to muscle imbalance (inhibited gluteals, hypertonic psoas and erectors), and maintain or worsen sacral nutation failure and pelvic shears. Together with the other five they form a self-perpetuating dysfunctional unit, which is why you treat all of them. Address after the pelvic ring is stabilized. Evaluation: Fryette’s laws, TART changes, spring testing.

9. DHD #5: Short-Leg, Pelvic-Tilt Syndrome (slides 18–21)

63% had it. A lateral pelvic tilt that unlevels the sacral base, caused by a leg-length discrepancy. Even small discrepancies (5–15 mm) can cause symptoms when combined with other pelvic or lumbar dysfunctions, which explains persistent pain after “normal” imaging or earlier treatment that ignored the pelvic foundation.

True (anatomic) short legFunctional (apparent) short leg
CauseActual bony or joint difference: congenital, post hip replacement, fracture, growth (epiphyseal) injurySomatic dysfunction: hypertonic psoas or quadratus lumborum, innominate upslip/shear, lumbar scoliosis, pelvic rotation
FrequencyClinically relevant around 15–20 mm (¾ inch); some tolerate 20–25 mm; >20 mm affects about 1 in 1,000Most common in OMM practice
CheckASIS to medial malleolus; best confirmed by imaging (scanogram or full-leg films)Apparent length: umbilicus or xiphoid to medial malleolus; iliac crest heights
TreatmentLift in the shoe (inside or custom); wear it consistently (no day-long barefoot walks)Treat the dysfunction
Dr. Langevin’s practiceBefore sending for imaging, remove all somatic dysfunction in the low back, pelvis and hips and re-measure clinically. If a difference of about 20 mm (¾ inch) is still obvious after everything moves symmetrically, then measure and consider a lift. Look at shoe wear (he mainly looks for asymmetry).

Relative long and short leg (the innominate axis)

  • Anterior rotation of an innominate gives a relatively longer-appearing leg; posterior rotation gives a relatively shorter leg.
  • Axis of rotation is the mid-SI joint. Anterior rotation: the ASIS moves inferior (PSIS superior), which lowers the femur relative to the other side (long leg). Posterior rotation: the opposite (short leg).
  • The tight (restricted) side tells you which side is abnormal, short or long.
  • Greenman: the body compensates to lengthen the short side, often by anterior rotation of the innominate on the short-leg side, causing lordosis and internal rotation of the femur, tibia and foot: a cascade.
Short-leg compensation through the body (slide 18)
Short-leg / pelvic-tilt cascade (slide 21)

History: chronic or recurrent, often unilateral low back pain; buttock or SI pain; hip pain; perceived leg-length difference; uneven shoe wear; worse with standing or walking. Compensations: lower extremity dysfunction, innominate rotations (compensatory, not part of the core DHD), type I spinal sidebending, muscle imbalance, shoulder asymmetry. Especially relevant in postpartum patients, asymmetric athletes, prior hip/knee surgery and chronic postural collapse.

10. DHD #6: Muscle Imbalance of the Trunk and Lower Extremities (slides 22–23)

Nearly all (>95%). Greenman: asymmetric hypertonicity and inhibition of key trunk and lower extremity muscles that disrupt pelvic and lumbar stability. It is both a cause and a consequence of the other five, a self-reinforcing cycle (“self-licking ice cream cone”).

Muscles
Tight / hypertonic (especially on the short-leg side)Hip flexors (psoas), quadratus lumborum, erector spinae/iliocostalis, piriformis, adductors
Inhibited / weakGluteus maximus and medius, transversus abdominis, multifidus, sometimes contralateral muscles

Why it matters

  • It maintains pelvic tilt and lumbar compensation even after the other dysfunctions are partly or completely corrected; it explains pain that returns despite treatment or normal imaging. Hypertonic psoas/QL on the short side drives the functional short leg.
  • Transversus abdominis is a trigger muscle: it fires about 40–60 ms before an arm or leg moves, signaling the erector spinae and QL to stabilize the spine and keep the pelvis level (elite athletes fire it faster and harder). When it is weak, pelvic and spinal integrity is lost, and proprioception from an injured QL changes how muscles fire, overloading joints such as the knee.

Treatment options (high yield)

StepWhat to do
1. OMT firstRelease psoas (counterstrain, muscle energy, myofascial); release QL; wake up the glutes (muscle energy, facilitated positional release, Still technique, Dr. Langevin’s choice); core facilitation (gentle techniques for transversus abdominis and multifidus); integrate with corrections for innominate and pubic shear
2. Home exercisePsoas/hip flexor stretches (lunge, Thomas), QL stretches, glute activation (clamshells, bridges, single-leg bridges, bird-dog), core stabilization (dead bugs, side planks, pelvic tilts with TA engagement), gait retraining and single-leg balance
3. Sequencing tipCorrect the hypertonic muscles (psoas, QL, TFL) before aggressively strengthening the inhibited ones (glutes, core)
The closing message“What we do with OMM is not cure them. We are removing restrictions and normalizing motion and function. Their strengthening and retraining maintains that treatment. They’re curing themselves.” The patient must be compliant or the pain returns.

11. The Treatment Sequence and Clinical Vignette (slides 24–31)

Greenman’s treatment sequence (slide 24)
  1. Pubic symphysis dysfunction
  2. Innominate shears
  3. Sacral dysfunction
  4. Lumbar type II dysfunction
  5. Correct short leg, if present
  6. Correct muscle imbalance: exercises and retraining
OMM re-establishes normal motion and function; strengthening and retraining maintain that treatment.

The pelvic ring is addressed before the lumbar spine. Treating these in order also resolves the relative leg-length differences. “I can’t stress this enough.”

Clinical vignette

Patient52-year-old construction worker, 22 months of persistent mechanical low back pain; failed 6 months of PT, two epidural steroid injections and NSAIDs; worse with prolonged standing, bending, lifting; no red flags (no incontinence, radiculopathy, muscle atrophy or weakness). Exam: standing flexion test lateralizes right; pubic symphysis tender with right inferior pubic bone; positive right ASIS compression test, right iliac crest high in standing; left sacral sulcus deeper, spring test shows restricted anterior nutation on the left; FRS right at L5; apparent right short leg, true lengths equal; tight right hip flexors and piriformis; weak right transversus abdominis and gluteus medius.
QuestionAnswer and reasoning
Which DHD components are present?A: all six: pubic (right inferior, 75%), innominate shear (right, 24%), sacral (left nutation restriction, 48.6%), lumbar type II (FRS right L5, 84%), short-leg (apparent, 63%), muscle imbalance (>95%)
Which should be addressed first, and why?C: the inferior right pubic dysfunction, because it can confound the diagnosis of the sacral and innominate findings
Which finding is most specific for the right innominate shear?B: standing flexion lateralizing right plus persistent right iliac crest elevation, standing vs supine. A = lumbar FRS; C = pubic; D = muscle imbalance; E = short-leg syndrome
Teaching pointCorrelate multiple findings; no single test is 100% diagnostic. His take-home: “when you have a relative with back pain, follow this… you’ll be surprised, it works quite well.”

Treatment sequence (Greenman)

1. Pubic symphysis → 2. Innominate shears → 3. Sacral dysfunction → 4. Lumbar Type II → 5. Short leg (if present) → 6. Muscle imbalance (exercises and retraining)

Form vs force closureForm closure: bony anatomy (the wedge-shaped sacrum protects against shear). Force closure: ligaments plus muscles, via the heel-strike chain: peroneus longus → biceps femoris → sacrotuberous ligament → ipsilateral SI joint → lumbosacral fascia → contralateral latissimus dorsi.
