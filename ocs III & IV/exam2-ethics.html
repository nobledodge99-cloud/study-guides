2. Models of the Doctor–Patient Relationship

Emanuel & Emanuel (JAMA 1992) describe four models that differ in the goal of the interaction, the physician’s obligations, the role of patient values, and the conception of patient autonomy. Emanuel & Emanuel

ModelPhysician’s rolePatient’s role / autonomyDecision-making
Paternalistic (parental, priestly)Guardian: decides what is best, with limited input; emphasizes health over choiceAssents to the physician’s determinationPhysician holds most power
Informative (scientific, consumer)Purveyor of technical expertise: gives all facts, then executes the patient’s choiceControl over decision-making: autonomy = controlPatient holds most power
InterpretiveCounselor/advisor: gives information, then helps the patient clarify and interpret their own valuesAutonomy = self-understandingPatient decides, with a more active physician than in the informative model
DeliberativeTeacher or friend: engages the patient in dialogue about which course is best, and what the patient should do, while respecting the patientAutonomy = moral self-development through discussionShared, through dialogue
Key contrastsPaternalistic vs informative are the two poles (physician controls vs patient controls). Interpretive vs deliberative: interpretive helps the patient discover their own values; deliberative also lets the physician argue for health-related values. Emanuel & Emanuel favor the deliberative model as the ideal; a fifth “instrumental” model (patient as a means) was discarded on moral grounds. Paternalism has a role mainly in rare emergencies when time to obtain consent would harm the patient.

All of this sits under the AMA Principles of Medical Ethics: the patient’s welfare comes first, and the relationship is built on trust. AMA Code

3. Conflict of Interest and How It Is Addressed

DefinitionA conflict of interest exists when a physician’s primary interest (the patient’s welfare) may be unduly influenced by a secondary interest (financial gain, career advancement, loyalty to an organization, personal relationships). A conflict can be actual or perceived, and the concern is not the secondary interest itself but its potential to subvert patient care.

The AMA Code: “The primary objective of the medical profession is to render service to humanity; reward or financial gain is a subordinate consideration. Under no circumstances may physicians place their own financial interests above the welfare of their patients.” Where an organization’s economic interests conflict with patient welfare, patient welfare takes priority. AMA Code

StrategyHow it works
RecognizeIdentify the secondary interest and whether a reasonable person could see it as influencing your judgment
DiscloseTell patients about financial incentives or interests that could affect their care (and about limits on referrals or treatment a health plan imposes)
Avoid or limitDecline gifts and arrangements that carry the risk of bias; limit the size and proximity of financial incentives to individual treatment decisions
Manage / separate dutiesPut structures (policies, oversight, ethics committees) between the interest and the decision; the duty to the patient stays paramount
AdvocatePhysicians should promote payment and delivery models that support access to high-quality care and professionalism

Disclosure alone is not enough; the obligation is to ensure that the patient’s interests are actually protected. AMA Code

4. Social Determinants of Health; Outcomes, Access and Cost in the US

Social determinants of health (SDOH)

The conditions in the places where people live, learn, work and play that affect health outcomes and quality of life, largely beyond an individual’s control. Healthy People 2030 groups them into five domains: Healthy People 2030

DomainGoal / examples
Economic stabilitySteady income to meet health needs; poverty, employment, food and housing security
Education access and qualityEducational opportunity, early childhood education, high school graduation, health literacy
Health care access and qualityComprehensive, high-quality care: insurance coverage, preventive services, a usual source of care
Neighborhood and built environmentSafe, health-promoting neighborhoods: clean water, housing quality, transportation, crime, broadband
Social and community contextCommunity and social support, discrimination, incarceration of family, civic participation
Why this matters to the physicianPatients with the same diagnosis can have very different outcomes because of these factors (for example, a patient who cannot afford medication or transport). Screening for SDOH and connecting patients to resources is part of good care, and links to health equity. Healthy People 2030

Health outcomes, access and cost in the United States

  • The US spends more on health care than any other high-income country (about $12,700 per person in 2022; Switzerland, second, about $9,000) but has among the worst outcomes: lowest life expectancy at birth among peer countries (77 years in 2020, about 3 years below the OECD average), the highest rates of maternal and infant mortality, and the highest rate of people with multiple chronic conditions. Commonwealth Fund
  • The Commonwealth Fund’s 2024 comparison of 10 high-income countries ranked the US last overall and last in access, equity and health outcomes. About 26 million Americans are uninsured; the US is the only country in the comparison without universal coverage. Commonwealth Fund
  • Care process is not the main driver: SDOH, access and cost barriers contribute heavily. Commonwealth Fund

5. Ethical Risks of the Payment Systems

Every payment arrangement creates incentives that can influence clinical decisions. The AMA’s guidance: monetary incentives should be judged in part by their size (large incentives compromise objectivity) and their proximity to individual treatment decisions. AMA Code

SystemHow the physician is paidEthical riskSafeguard
Fee for servicePer service or procedureIncentive to overuse: unnecessary tests, procedures, visits; also self-referral and fee splittingProhibitions on fee splitting, unnecessary services, unreasonable fees and self-referral; disclose financial interests in referral facilities
CapitationA fixed amount per patient per month, regardless of services usedIncentive to underuse: withholding or delaying beneficial care, avoiding sick patientsEvaluate the plan’s capitation rate before contracting so quality is not threatened; rates should reflect patients’ medical needs; larger plans spread risk better
SalaryFixed payIncentive to do less work or see fewer patientsQuality monitoring and professionalism
Bonuses / pay-for-performance / utilization targetsRewards or penalties tied to utilization or measuresLarge or tiered rewards and penalties near a cutoff create strong pressure; group incentives can pit patients against each otherIncentives should be calculated on a continuum of utilization, applied across broad physician groups, promote efficiency but not savings beyond what efficiency allows
What physicians must doPatients must be informed of financial incentives that could affect the level or type of care they receive. Physicians should evaluate a plan’s incentives before contracting, avoid arrangements that would harm the patient’s trust if disclosed, and provide care based on evidence, sound judgment and guidelines. When a plan does not cover a service the physician believes the patient needs, the physician should tell the patient so the patient can decide. AMA Code

Potential benefits of incentives: reducing waste, encouraging preventive and ambulatory care, attending to patient satisfaction. The risk is that they put the physician’s personal financial interest into the therapeutic relationship, especially where there is no clear clinical course. AMA Code

6. Bedside Rationing of Health Care

DefinitionBedside rationing is the withholding by a physician of a medically beneficial service because of that service’s cost to someone other than the patient. Ubel & Goold

Recognizing it

  • The physician withholds, withdraws or fails to recommend a service that, in their best judgment, would benefit the patient, because of cost to a payer, hospital or society. Ubel & Goold
  • Not rationing: if the patient chooses a cheaper option because of the cost to themselves; if the options are equally effective (consider cost only); or applying legitimate eligibility criteria (for example, transplant list criteria). Ubel & Goold
  • Bedside rationing is troubling because it can undermine trust and the physician’s role as the patient’s advocate; critics also worry that physicians will make inconsistent or unfair decisions. Ubel & Goold

The physician’s approach AMA Code

PrincipleWhat to do
Primary obligationPromote the well-being of the individual patient; also be a responsible steward of shared resources (stewardship, parsimonious care)
Avoid waste firstUse evidence and clinical guidelines; do not provide interventions with no benefit (“Choosing Wisely”); this is stewardship, not rationing
Follow fair policiesRationing decisions should rest on explicit, consistent criteria (guidelines, benefit, cost-effectiveness) rather than the individual physician’s private judgment; ethical acceptability depends on guideline-consistency and fairness
Be transparentExplain to patients who are denied a scarce resource why, and what the allocation policy is; tell them a service is unavailable, not “useless”
AdvocateAdvocate for the patient within the system (appeals, alternatives, exceptions); seek ethics consultation for hard cases; work to change unjust policies

7. Gifts from Drug Companies

The concernGifts from industry create conditions that carry the risk of subtly biasing, or being perceived to bias, professional judgment, and can erode public trust. Even small gifts create a sense of obligation (reciprocity). AMA Code

Current AMA guidance (Opinion 9.6.2) AMA Code

RuleContent
(a)Decline cash gifts of any amount from an entity with a direct interest in treatment recommendations
(b)Decline any gift for which reciprocity is expected or implied
(c)Accept an in-kind gift for the practice only if it directly benefits patients (including patient education) and is of minimal value
(d)Academic institutions and residency programs may accept funding for trainees to attend professional meetings only if recipients are chosen by independent institutional criteria and funds go to recipients without attribution to sponsors

Older, more detailed guidance (former Opinion 8.061) AMA Code

  • Gifts should primarily benefit patients and not be of substantial value (textbooks and modest meals tied to a genuine educational function).
  • No strings attached: do not accept gifts tied to prescribing.
  • A legitimate meeting is primarily dedicated to objective scientific and educational content, with disclosure of financial support.
  • Subsidies for continuing medical education should go through the conference organizers, not directly to attendees; do not accept payment for travel, lodging or personal expenses.
  • Drug samples may be used for patients; they should not be used for personal or family use in a way that interferes with patient access.
For traineesMany medical schools now prohibit industry gifts entirely; the scholarly consensus has moved toward declining gifts of any size or nature. AMA Code

8. Disclosing Errors to Patients

Definition and duty (Opinion 8.6)An error is “an unintended act or omission or a flawed system or plan that harms or has the potential to harm a patient.” Patients have a right to know their past and present medical status, including conditions that may have resulted from error. Concern about legal liability should not affect the physician’s honesty with the patient. AMA Code

Even when the new information will not change the patient’s treatment, a physician involved in a possible error should: AMA Code

StepContent
DiscloseTell the patient that the error occurred; explain the nature of the (potential) harm
InformGive the information the patient needs to make informed decisions about future care
Acknowledge and express concernAcknowledge the error and express professional and compassionate concern toward the patient (an apology)
Support the patientOffer to help, including transfer of care if the patient has lost trust in the physician

Other responsibilities AMA Code

  • If you believe another professional may have erred: encourage them to disclose; report impaired or incompetent colleagues as the ethics guidance requires.
  • Promote a culture of patient safety, including compassion for colleagues who have been involved in an error.
  • Study the circumstances of errors; a legally protected review process is essential; ensure root cause analysis leads to prevention and that measures are communicated to stakeholders.
Why discloseHonesty is fundamental to the trust that underlies the patient’s relationship with the physician, and it respects patient autonomy: patients cannot make informed choices without knowing what happened.
