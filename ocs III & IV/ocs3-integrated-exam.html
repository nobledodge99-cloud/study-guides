00. Overview & Learning Objectives

The student will:

  1. Recognize different methods of organizing the integrated physical examination and osteopathic structural exam (OSE):
    • Screening systems-based
    • Positional
    • Focused
  2. Appreciate the purpose of performing an integrated osteopathic examination
  3. Develop applications of both a full integrated osteopathic examination and focused integrated examinations depending on patient presentation and clinical situation
Practice Question

01. Where Did We Start? The Screening Exam

  • The initial screening examination (learned in first year, OCS I/II) already included some integration of the osteopathic structural examination (OSE)
  • That integration was mainly focused on incorporating the OSE within the musculoskeletal and peripheral nervous system (PNS) exam section of a systems-based examination
  • This forms a solid foundation for applying a systems-based approach to the integrated osteopathic examination
  • It enables components of the physical exam (PE) and OSE to be applied to focused examinations based on patient presentation
Practice Question

02. Screening Checklist I: Vitals, General, Derm & HEENT

The comprehensive screening physical exam checklist begins as follows:

  • Wash hands (or sanitize); obtain consent to examine
  • Vitals: Blood pressure (palpatory and auscultatory), heart rate, respiratory rate
  • General Assessment: Appearance/behavior, affect/mood, language
  • Dermatological Exam: Inspection and palpation of skin, hair, and nails
  • HEENT Exam: Inspection and palpation of head/face and neck; lymph nodes; sinuses; eye exam (pupil light reflex, extraocular movements); otoscopic ear exam; otoscopic nose exam; oral exam; thyroid
Time-management tip: Orthostatic blood pressure (supine, seated, standing) takes several minutes and would consume a significant portion of a typical 30-minute exam window. In a time-limited screening exam, perform palpatory and auscultatory blood pressure instead, and reserve orthostatics for when they are clinically indicated.

03. Screening Checklist II: Cranial Nerves, Pulmonary, CV & Abdominal

  • Cranial Nerves (additional): Trigeminal (V), Facial (VII), Vestibulocochlear (VIII), Spinal Accessory (XI), Hypoglossal (XII) — the remaining nerves not already assessed during the HEENT/eye exam (II, III, IV, VI)
  • Pulmonary Exam: Inspection, palpation, auscultation
  • Cardiovascular Exam: Inspection and palpation; jugular venous pressure; pulses (carotid, radial, tibialis posterior, dorsalis pedis); cardiac auscultation; carotid auscultation
  • Abdominal Exam: Inspection; auscultation (quadrants, aorta); palpation (quadrants, liver, spleen, aorta); costovertebral angle tenderness (Lloyd's sign)
Sequence logic: Every system exam in the checklist begins the same way — inspection, then palpation, then auscultation — followed by special tests specific to that system (e.g., JVP and carotid auscultation for cardiovascular; CVA tenderness for abdominal).
Practice Questions

04. Screening Checklist III: MSK/PNS — Neck & Upper Extremity

  • Postural landmarks
  • Neck: Inspection and palpation; range of motion
  • Upper Extremity: Inspection and palpation; range of motion (shoulder, elbow, wrist/hand)
  • Strength: Biceps, triceps, forearm flexors, forearm extensors, hands
  • Sensation: Dermatomes or peripheral cutaneous nerve distribution; 2-point discrimination
  • Reflexes: Biceps (C5), Brachioradialis (C6), Triceps (C7)
Practice Questions

05. Screening Checklist IV: Back, Lower Extremity & CNS

  • Back (standing): Inspection and palpation; standing flexion test; range of motion (active, passive); assess for Area of Greatest Restriction (AGR)
  • Back (seated): Inspection and palpation; seated flexion test; range of motion; assess for AGR
  • Lower Extremity (supine): Inspection and palpation; range of motion (hip, knees, ankles)
  • Lower Extremity (seated) — Strength: Hip flexors, hip extensors, knee flexors, knee extensors, ankle dorsiflexors, ankle plantarflexors
  • Lower Extremity (seated) — Sensation: Dermatomes or peripheral cutaneous nerve distribution; vibration; toe proprioception
  • Lower Extremity (seated) — Reflexes: Patellar (L4), Achilles (S1)
  • CNS Exam — Cerebellar: Rapid alternating movement; finger-to-nose test; heel-to-shin test; Romberg with pronator drift; gait testing (regular, tandem, toes, heels)
Testing order matters: Romberg is a separate test from pronator drift — perform the Romberg test first, then assess for pronator drift.
Practice Questions

06. Where Are We Going? Building on the Foundation

  • Continued use of the entire systems-based physical examination remains valuable when useful for the clinical situation
  • Reviewing the examination as learned in OCS I/II reinforces all of its elements
  • This is particularly useful during peer-to-peer teaching activities — performing the exam in a familiar, consistent manner allows second-year students to provide examples and coaching for first-year students
  • It is entirely appropriate to adapt the exam reasonably from the checklist based on other experiences and growth as a second-year student advancing toward clinical rotations
  • This provides a basis upon which to build additional physical examinations suited for different settings and patient populations
Practice Question

07. What Is the Integrated Osteopathic Examination?

  • Consists of elements of the typical physical examination (PE) combined with elements of the osteopathic structural examination (OSE), integrated into a smooth sequence of physical exam maneuvers
  • This does not mean performing all of the PE followed by a separate OSE block, or alternating PE-then-OSE within each section — it means the OSE is woven within each portion of the exam, whether that integration is almost entirely within the musculoskeletal/PNS exam (as in the screening exam) or spread more broadly (as in the positional exam)
  • Purpose: build the ability to apply appropriate PE and OSE elements into an adaptable examination suited to all circumstances encountered in clinical medicine
Practice Question

08. Purpose of Adaptable Examinations

Flexibility Across Clinical Settings

Not every patient can tolerate the same examination sequence:

  • A hospitalized or ICU patient may be unable to sit or stand → the exam must be adapted to a supine or semi-recumbent position. This can also occur with a patient in significant pain who gets onto the treatment table and has little ability to move without pain, requiring assessment (and treatment) before being repositioned.
  • A patient in respiratory distress (e.g., tripodding) may need to remain upright → the sequence must be adjusted to minimize exertion

Efficiency in Practice

Having more than one "mental model" for the exam allows rapid adaptation. At the same time, there is value in an exam performed frequently and repetitively enough to build muscle memory — so that if paged to the ER, ICU, or Labor & Delivery in the middle of the night, the exam sequence is already second nature.

Options

  • System-based sequencing (traditional checklists, organ systems)
  • Position-based sequencing (standing → seated → supine → prone) — more natural for an integrated osteopathic assessment
Clinical realism: Actual patient encounters rarely follow the "ideal" exam flow learned in class — patients don't read the textbook. Developing flexible versions of the exam prepares you for real practice.
Practice Questions

09. Benefits of the Integrated Osteopathic Examination

Efficiency & Flow

  • Example: when assessing a patient for back pain, it's natural to check cardiorespiratory exams alongside AGR and TART findings in the same sequence
  • This prevents duplication — you don't have to examine the same body region twice in separate sections of the exam

Diagnostic Power

  • Integration makes it easier to correlate viscerosomatic findings with system exam findings — for example, a viscerosomatic reflex finding at T6–T9 (warm tissue, positive red reflex) may correlate with abdominal pain, or a restriction in rib motion may correlate with pulmonary findings
  • This helps you learn to notice patterns across systems rather than isolating musculoskeletal findings into their own separate bucket
Practice Questions

10. Integrated Exam & Osteopathic Philosophy

Holistic Patient View

  • If the OSE is kept "tacked on" at the end of a typical physical exam, it becomes all too easy to let it fall away or think of it as separate or optional
  • The musculoskeletal exam and somatic dysfunction findings are part of the patient's health story — the body is telling you what is going on. Integrating the structural exam throughout, rather than appending it, helps weave the physical exam findings and somatic dysfunction into one coherent narrative for patient care.

Identity as an Osteopathic Physician

  • Embedding the OSE throughout the physical exam models the osteopathic philosophy of the unity of the person and unity of structure and function
  • This encourages thinking "osteopathically" from early lab experiences and early patient contacts — not as an afterthought to the process

11. Positional Exam: Overview

The integrated osteopathic examination can be organized by patient position — standing, seated, supine, and prone — with all elements of the systems-based exam integrated within each position.

PositionComponents
StandingGeneral assessment · Musculoskeletal exam · CNS exam (cerebellar/gait testing)
SeatedVitals · HEENT exam · Cranial nerves · Dermatological exam · Pulmonary exam · Cardiovascular exam · Musculoskeletal & PNS exams
SupineAbdominal exam · Musculoskeletal exam
ProneMusculoskeletal exam
Practical flow: It is easier to break out the seated (and standing) exam into anterior and posterior components, moving smoothly as you examine — for example, postural assessment naturally breaks into anterior, posterior, and lateral views.
Practice Question

12. Positional Exam: Standing

  • General: Appearance & behavior — this can be assessed as the patient walks into the room (reducing self-consciousness), though often the patient is already seated, in which case the sequence may begin seated and move to standing/gait later
  • OSE: Postural landmarks; assess Area of Greatest Restriction (AGR) across cervical, thoracic, lumbar, sacral, and rib regions; standing flexion test (screens for a possible pelvic/innominate problem — note the finding to correlate later)
  • MSK: Trunk range of motion (active/passive)
  • MSK/CNS: Gait (regular, tandem, toes, heels)
  • CNS: Rapid alternating movement; finger-to-nose; heel-to-shin; Romberg with pronator drift (test Romberg first, then pronator drift)
Practice Questions

13. Positional Exam: Seated (Anterior)

  • Vitals: Blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate — ideally after letting the patient sit for about five minutes (temperature is often omitted from a student screening exam since a thermometer typically isn't on hand, unless the full vital sign set is being obtained)
  • HEENT/CNS: Head/neck mobility, palpation, and observation; lymph nodes; sinuses; eye exam (pupils, extraocular movements); ears, nose, oral exam
  • CNS: Remaining cranial nerves — V, VII, VIII, XI, XII
  • Derm: Skin, hair, nails — hair can be incorporated while examining the head and neck; skin and nails can be further assessed during the musculoskeletal component
  • Cardiovascular: Inspection, palpation, auscultation (don't forget the carotid arteries)
  • Pulmonary: Inspection, palpation, auscultation
  • MSK/PNS: Upper extremities and lower extremities — range of motion, strength, sensation, reflexes
Practice Questions

14. Positional Exam: Seated (Posterior)

With the patient still seated, the exam continues posteriorly:

  • Pulmonary: Continue inspecting, palpating, and auscultating the posterior chest
  • HEENT: Palpate the thyroid, completing the HEENT exam
  • OSE: Assess AGR; TART findings; scapular motion test and upper extremity diagnosis if appropriate; seated flexion test (indicates possible sacral somatic dysfunction — investigate further if positive); make a vertebral diagnosis if indicated
  • Abdomen: Complete the abdominal exam with costovertebral angle tenderness (CVA) / Lloyd's sign
Practice Questions

15. Positional Exam: Supine

  • Cardiovascular: Complete the exam with jugular venous pressure (JVP) — observe for jugular venous distension, and measure the pressure if indicated
  • Abdominal Exam: Inspect, auscultate, and palpate
  • OSE: ASIS compression test — used to further affirm or refute the standing and seated flexion test findings; assess for pelvic findings and diagnose pelvic somatic dysfunction if indicated
Practice Questions

16. Positional Exam: Prone

The prone position wraps up the integrated osteopathic examination, focusing entirely on the osteopathic structural exam:

  • Thoracic and lumbar TART, if not already assessed while seated, and vertebral diagnosis if indicated — some clinicians find this position easier for these findings, though not every patient can tolerate prone positioning well
  • Sacral findings and somatic dysfunction diagnosis, if indicated
Adaptability reminder: If a patient cannot tolerate the prone position, thoracic, lumbar, and sacral findings can be obtained using an alternative position the patient can tolerate (e.g., seated or side-lying).
Practice Questions

17. Further Adaptations & Focused Exams

  • Adapt the full integrated exam to the patient in front of you — select the relevant systems and OSE findings for the presenting problem
  • Maintain flow by grouping related maneuvers — for example, lung auscultation, thoracic cage assessment, rib excursion/compliance, and seated AGR/TART findings can all be performed together
  • Ensure coverage of the general assessment and OSE — this may include more than just AGR and TART findings if a diagnosable (and treatable) somatic dysfunction is present
  • Document what you evaluated — even without permission to treat with OMT on rotations, examining is always within your scope. Always examine, incorporate findings into your clinical decision-making, and document what was evaluated.

18. Take-Aways & Master Summary Table

  • Combine the physical exam and osteopathic structural exam into a seamless sequence that flows well
  • Utilize systems-based, positional, or focused examination formats for flexibility
  • Avoid duplication of examination in the same area, and use osteopathic diagnostic value for efficiency in clinical decision-making
  • Think osteopathically at all times, and craft examinations that are adaptable, efficient, and patient-centered
