01. What Is Telemedicine?

Telemedicine is the delivery of health care from a distance using electronic information and technology — such as computers, cameras, videoconferencing, wireless communications, satellites, and the internet. It is also called telehealth.

02. History of Telemedicine

EraDevelopment
1879The concept of telemedicine was first introduced with the invention of the telephone
1920s–1930sRadiology images were sent via telephone to be interpreted by specialists at a distance
1950s–1960sClosed-circuit television began to be used for medical consultations
1970s–1980sNASA developed telemedicine technology to monitor astronauts' health in space, advancing remote healthcare
1990sWidespread internet availability led to video conferencing technology
2000–presentContinued evolution with mobile health apps, remote monitoring devices, and virtual care platforms — now an integral part of modern healthcare delivery

04. Types of Telemedicine Services

TypeDescription
Real-time telemedicineUses real-time video, allowing medical professionals to assess, consult, diagnose, and prescribe from hundreds or thousands of miles away — virtual visits between patients and providers from any location
Store-and-forward telemedicineCollecting clinical information and sending it electronically to another site for evaluation (e.g., the radiology images sent via telephone in the 1920s–30s). Typically includes demographic data, medical history, lab reports, video or sound files, and images.
Remote patient monitoringA type of telehealth often not thought of as "telehealth," in which providers monitor patients outside the traditional office setting — e.g., remote blood pressure monitors, glucose monitors, weight scales, and pulse oximeters

06. Benefits for Medical Professionals

  • Increased reach and patient base: patients don't have to come into the office, take time off work, or find childcare — they can take a call from their office or their car
  • Time-saving and efficient: one of the most efficient ways to see patients, deliver lab results, and have conversations — most practitioners can see more patients in the same amount of time
  • Improved work-life balance: physicians can see the same (or more) patients in less time, mixing in-office and out-of-office visits to maximize their day and make sure all patients are seen and cared for
Practice Questions

07. Benefits for Patients

  • Access to care from remote locations: in a state like Montana, where most of the state is considered remote, telemedicine can replace a three-hour, one-way drive to see a specialist — a real improvement in quality of care
  • Reduced travel time and costs: less time off work, less spent on gas, less need for babysitting, and so on
  • Continuity of care: it's often easier to see your provider through a telemedicine service than to wait for an in-person clinic visit

09. Regulations & Legal Considerations

  • Licensure: telemedicine allows you to see patients in other states — a physician living in California could see a patient in New York, or one in Florida could see a patient in Texas. Telemedicine licensure requirements differ across states, so it's essential to hold appropriate licensure everywhere you practice, and to follow all licensing, CME, and telemedicine-specific requirements.
  • Reimbursement: different payers and policies reimburse telemedicine differently — proper coding is crucial to obtaining appropriate reimbursement for the service.
  • Patient privacy and security: HIPAA compliance must remain front and center. Telemedicine visits should be conducted in a space considered HIPAA-compliant — losing sight of privacy and security can be catastrophic.

10. Telemedicine Workflow

Workflow varies across different platforms, but a typical sequence looks like this:

  1. Patient calls or otherwise requests an appointment (scheduling)
  2. Payment is typically made prior to the video consultation
  3. Video consultation occurs with the physician
  4. Labs are ordered or viewed, chart notes are written, and prescriptions are sent as needed
  5. A visit summary is created and posted to the patient portal

11. Success & Growth of Telemedicine

  • Telemedicine has seen huge success, especially over the last five years, driven substantially by a massive surge during COVID-19
  • Patients and providers report greater satisfaction with telemedicine compared to before
  • Growth has been supported by successful implementations, new programs, better workflows, and improved patient outcomes and satisfaction

12. Challenges of Telemedicine

Commonly reported challenges, in roughly descending order of frequency:

  1. Effectiveness
  2. No knowledge of the patient's history
  3. Limited physical exam
  4. Uncertainty about the accuracy of treatment
  5. Fewer in-person consultations (balancing in-person vs. telemedicine care)
  6. Outdated equipment
  7. Security and privacy of patients
  8. Patient technology literacy/education
  9. Misdiagnosis
  10. General lack of confidence using telemedicine, from both physicians and patients

13. Telemedicine Ethics

  • Security, HIPAA compliance, and privacy
  • Quality of care
  • The patient-physician relationship
  • Accessibility
  • Upholding the standard of care
  • Handling informed consent
  • Managing emergencies remotely (particularly risky and difficult)
  • Risk of fraud and abuse
As many benefits as telemedicine offers, there is also "the other side of the coin" — navigating these ethical issues while continuing to provide the standard of care patients deserve.
Practice Questions

14. Overcoming Challenges

  • Improved infrastructure — one of the biggest factors in improving effectiveness
  • Enhanced staff training — staff familiarity with equipment helps physicians deliver better care
  • Recognizing telehealth is here to stay — it's being incorporated by the millions every year. Providers who have practiced for 30–40 years and are resistant to it often benefit most from understanding how it can help their own practice.
  • Awareness of and advocacy for policy changes — at the local, state, and federal level, regarding both ethical and structural issues
Practice Questions

15. Telemedicine & COVID-19

There was rapid, massive growth in telemedicine utilization during COVID-19. With that rapid growth came both good and bad: changes had to happen quickly, and some companies or structures were slower to adapt, less prepared, or did not fully adhere to privacy requirements while everyone was scrambling to figure out how to see patients safely during an emergency.

The lessons learned during the pandemic have allowed the field to be better prepared, putting in place structures that continue to support the growth of the telehealth platform today.
Practice Question

16. Telemedicine & Health Equity

  • Telemedicine helps make healthcare more equitable by addressing disparities in access to care
  • It helps reach underserved populations — patients no longer need to drive hours, find childcare, or take time off work just to be seen
  • Patients can see their provider from wherever they are, on their own time, regardless of how far they live from that provider
  • Telemedicine also supports cultural competency in care delivery — an important piece of bridging gaps and making medicine more equitable

17. Future Trends in Telemedicine

  • Artificial intelligence integration — increasingly prevalent across many fields, including telemedicine
  • Virtual reality applications
  • Wearable technology integration — already emerging, and increasingly accessible across various telemedicine platforms and practices

18. Best Practices

Three core areas support the continued growth of telemedicine in the best possible way:

  1. Patient-centric care
  2. Proper organization and staffing
  3. Quality and continual evaluation — identifying what needs to improve and where the issues are

Supporting practices include establishing and following clear protocols and guidelines, continuous staff training and education, and consistently gathering feedback to drive quality improvement.
