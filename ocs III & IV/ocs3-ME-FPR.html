TJ, 22F — right ankle discomfort after an overseas flight

TJ presents with a week of right ankle discomfort after a long overseas trip. Both ankles have full dorsiflexion, but her right ankle is more limited in plantarflexion than her left. Keep her case in mind as you work through the muscle energy techniques below — we'll revisit her at the end.

What Are the Activating Forces That Drive Muscle Energy Treatment?

Muscle energy technique (MET) is a broad category of osteopathic manipulative techniques in which the patient actively contracts a muscle in a specific direction against a controlled counterforce applied by the physician. Because the patient must cooperate and respond to instructions, MET is an active technique, and because the initial setup is at the feather-edge of the restrictive barrier, it is usually a direct technique.

The activating forces — the patient's active muscle contraction plus the clinician's stabilizing counterforce — are what make MET effective. These forces engage specific neurophysiologic reflexes to produce the therapeutic effect. The physician tailors the amount and duration of force, and the type of muscle contraction, to the treatment goal: contractions can either shorten or lengthen a muscle, which allows a taut muscle to relax or a weak muscle to strengthen.

Types of Muscle Contraction

Four types of muscle contraction are used in MET: isometric, concentric isotonic, eccentric isotonic, and isolytic. The treatment goal determines which type is used.

Quick check — Activating force

An activating force is the effort the patient applies against a controlled counterforce provided by the physician — it's the combination of patient effort and clinician resistance that drives the therapeutic reflex.

Quick check — Most common contraction type

Isometric contraction is the most commonly used type of muscle contraction in MET.

Q1. A patient holds a wall-sit position — knees bent, back against the wall, no movement at the joint despite active muscle effort. Which type of contraction is occurring in the quadriceps?

  • Eccentric isotonic contraction
  • Concentric isotonic contraction
  • Isometric contraction
  • Isolytic contraction

Isometric contraction produces force without a change in muscle length or joint movement — exactly what happens during a static wall-sit hold. The patient's counterforce equals the opposing force, so no motion occurs.

Post-Isometric Relaxation Direct

Post-isometric relaxation (PIR) is the prototype MET technique and the most commonly used direct method. It restores muscle tone through a specific sequence: an isometric contraction is followed by passive stretching of the same muscle.

How It Works

Immediately after an isometric contraction, the muscle enters a brief refractory state during which it can be passively stretched with minimal resistance — analogous to the refractory period after an action potential. MET takes advantage of this window to reposition the patient into a new restrictive barrier.

Indications

Subacute or chronic somatic dysfunctions.

Technique

  1. Place the patient at the feather-edge of the restrictive barrier.
  2. Patient contracts against an equal, opposing force for 3–5 seconds, in the direction of ease.
  3. Instruct the patient to relax for about 2 seconds.
  4. Reposition the patient into the feather-edge of the new restrictive barrier and repeat.

Q2. A college student presents with neck pain after hours of studying at her computer. Her cervical spine is treated with post-isometric relaxation. Which best describes this technique?

  • Indirect and passive
  • Direct and active
  • Indirect and active
  • Direct and passive

PIR is a direct technique (set up at the feather-edge of the restrictive barrier) and active (the patient must voluntarily contract against the physician's counterforce).

Respiratory Assistance Technique Direct

Respiratory assistance uses the natural motion generated by the diaphragm and other respiratory muscles during breathing to influence structures throughout the body.

How It Works

This direct technique uses the muscle forces of breathing to influence the spine, pelvis, and extremities. Respiratory motion radiates through the body via tensegrity (fascial) structures. The physician creates a fulcrum within the affected tissue, resists motion away from the barrier during one phase of respiration (usually inhalation), and encourages motion into the barrier during the opposite phase (usually exhalation).

Indications

Easing tension in hypertonic muscles and their associated structures.

Example — Treating an Inhalation Rib Dysfunction

For a rib stuck in an expanded (inhalation) position: the physician applies inferior pressure on the anterior-superior surface of the rib throughout the respiratory cycle. On inhalation, the downward force resists further rib expansion. On exhalation, the physician applies inferior force to exaggerate the rib's exhalation motion, moving it toward a new barrier. Repeating this encourages the rib back toward its correct position.

Q3. What force does the physician use to enhance treatment during the respiratory assistance technique?

  • The mechanical forces generated by the patient's own inhalation and exhalation
  • A rapid, high-velocity thrust delivered at end-exhalation
  • Isometric contraction of an antagonist muscle group
  • Sustained traction held for 90 seconds or more

Respiratory assistance capitalizes on the patient's own voluntary breathing motion — transmitted through fascial tensegrity structures — as the activating force, rather than an external thrust or sustained hold.

Reciprocal Inhibition Technique Direct or Indirect

Reciprocal inhibition relies on voluntary contraction of an agonist muscle to reflexively relax its antagonist. Muscle tone is restored when one muscle contracts while its functional opposite is stretched.

How It Works

Activating the agonist muscle through contraction produces a reflex relaxation of the antagonist muscle.

Indications

Acute somatic dysfunctions — especially helpful for relieving muscle cramping.

Technique

  1. Move the dysfunctional agonist muscle to the feather-edge of the restrictive barrier.
  2. Instruct the patient to gently contract the functional antagonist toward the restrictive barrier while the physician applies counterforce for 3–5 seconds, then relax for about 2 seconds.
  3. Reposition into the feather-edge of the new restrictive barrier and repeat.

Example — Treating Biceps Tension

To relax a tight biceps, the physician flexes the elbow ~45°, then has the patient gently push the forearm toward the table — activating the triceps (antagonist) — while the physician palpates the biceps to confirm relaxation. After 3–5 seconds, the patient relaxes for 2 seconds, and the physician extends the elbow slightly to a new barrier. This is repeated 3–5 times.

Q4. What is the basic mechanism by which reciprocal inhibition works?

  • Passive stretch of a muscle immediately following an isometric hold
  • Respiratory motion transmitted through fascial tensegrity
  • Loss of support in one limb driving compensatory contraction in the opposite limb
  • Contracting and activating an agonist muscle leads to reflex inhibition of its dysfunctional antagonist

Reciprocal inhibition is a myotatic (stretch) reflex: voluntary contraction of the agonist produces reflex relaxation (inhibition) of the antagonist.

Oculocephalogyric Reflex Technique Direct & Indirect

This technique uses the vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR), which keeps gaze steady during head movement. VOR-driven eye movements produce slight contractions in muscle groups connected to the cervical and upper thoracic spine, both from head rotation and from eye movement itself.

How It Works

Also called the doll's eyes maneuver or oculocervical reflex: when the head rotates, the eyes reflexively move in the opposite direction to keep gaze fixed forward. This drives extraocular muscle contraction, which produces corresponding subtle contractions in muscles attached to the cervical and upper thoracic spine.

Indications

Dysfunctions of intrinsic spinal muscles, particularly cervical segments (including OA and AA) and some upper thoracic segments.

Technique

If a patient has full left rotation but limited right rotation: rotate the head to the right until the restrictive barrier is engaged, then either (a) instruct the patient to look right — a direct technique inducing post-isometric relaxation — or (b) instruct the patient to look left, inducing the reciprocal inhibitory reflex.

Example — Treating Infant Torticollis

A hypertonic left sternocleidomastoid (SCM) sidebends the neck left and rotates it right. Encouraging the infant to look up and to the left drives reflexive contraction of the right (non-dysfunctional) SCM, which — via reciprocal inhibition — relaxes the left SCM, allowing more left rotation and right sidebending.

Q5. How does the oculocephalogyric reflex technique treat intrinsic spinal muscle dysfunction?

  • By generating a crossed extensor response in the contralateral limb
  • By using inhalation/exhalation forces transmitted through tensegrity structures
  • By using voluntary eye movements to drive extraocular contractions that produce corresponding contractions in cervical/upper thoracic muscles
  • By forcibly stretching a chronically shortened muscle beyond its resting length

Directed eye movement engages the vestibulo-ocular reflex, producing extraocular muscle contraction that translates into subtle, corresponding contractions in the muscles attached to the cervical and upper thoracic spine.

Crossed Extensor Technique Indirect

The crossed extensor technique uses the crossed extensor reflex: a response in which the contralateral limb compensates for a loss of support in the ipsilateral limb. When flexors contract and extensors relax in one limb, the opposite happens in the contralateral limb — flexors relax, extensors contract. Because the dysfunctional limb is never treated directly, this is considered an indirect MET.

How It Works

This CNS-coordinated reflex causes the contralateral muscle to relax when the corresponding muscle is voluntarily contracted. Classic example: stepping on broken glass with the right foot causes the right quadriceps to contract and right hamstrings to relax (flexing the knee away from the stimulus); the CNS simultaneously relaxes the left quadriceps and contracts the left hamstrings to maintain balance.

Indications

Useful when direct manipulation of a severely injured muscle is inadvisable — fractures, wounds, or other conditions limiting treatment options (e.g., IV lines, cellulitis, painful injuries).

Technique

The physician moves the contralateral, healthy limb to its restrictive barrier and has the patient contract the healthy agonist muscle against resistance. This causes the corresponding dysfunctional agonist muscle on the injured side to relax — treating the injured muscle indirectly.

Q6. According to the crossed extensor reflex, a voluntary contraction of the right biceps brachii will cause relaxation of which muscle?

  • Right triceps brachii
  • Left biceps brachii
  • Left triceps brachii
  • Right brachialis

The crossed extensor reflex relaxes the same muscle on the contralateral side — not the antagonist on either side. Voluntarily contracting the right biceps reflexively relaxes the left biceps, which is exactly how the technique is used to treat an injured, unmanipulable limb indirectly through its healthy contralateral counterpart.

Facilitated Positional Release (FPR) Indirect

CASE CONNECTION — JK

JK, 62F — achy low back pain after an MVA 2 weeks ago

Tenderness and bilateral hypertonicity of the lumbar paraspinals, negative straight leg raise, unremarkable MRI. Never had OMT before. Keep her case in mind as you read about FPR.

What Is FPR?

FPR is an indirect osteopathic technique designed to reduce tissue hypertonicity and improve joint mobility, similar to counterstrain — but faster, since it uses an added activating force rather than counterstrain's sustained hold. Patients are placed in a position of comfort, and a facilitating force (usually traction or compression) is applied along the direction of the joint or tissue.

FPR was created by Dr. Stanley Schiowitz. The facilitating force is controlled, precise pressure that encourages relaxation in the restricted tissue; the activating force is the "release-enhancing" dynamic force that triggers the actual physiologic release.

How Does FPR Work?

FPR targets abnormal muscle hypertonicity underlying TART changes and somatic dysfunction. Dr. Schiowitz's theory holds that most vertebral motion restrictions arise from hypertonic small, deep intervertebral muscles influenced by gamma motor neurons and muscle spindles.

Skeletal muscle contains extrafusal fibers (the main contractile cells, innervated by alpha motor neurons) and intrafusal fibers/muscle spindles (which detect changes in muscle length and set resting tone, innervated by gamma motor neurons). Normally, alpha-gamma co-activation keeps muscle spindle length matched to extrafusal fiber length during voluntary contraction — this is the "gamma gain" reflex, and it preserves proprioception. When a muscle stays contracted for a long time (e.g., postural spinal muscles), the muscle spindles recalibrate to treat the shortened state as the new normal, locking the muscle into hypertonicity and somatic dysfunction.

FPR reduces gamma gain by recalibrating the spindles: placing the body part in a position of ease reduces stretch/tension on the muscle spindle, decreasing 1a sensory afferent signaling to the brain. This reduces output from the descending corticospinal and reticulospinal tracts, which reduces gamma motor neuron drive to the intrafusal fibers — alpha motor drive to the extrafusal fibers falls in parallel, and the muscle relaxes.

Quick check — What differentiates FPR from counterstrain?

Both are indirect techniques that use a position of comfort, but FPR adds a facilitating/activating force (traction, compression, or torsion) that speeds resolution — counterstrain instead relies on a longer sustained hold (~90 seconds) without an added activating force.

Quick check — Muscle spindle function

Muscle spindles are sensory receptors in the muscle belly that detect changes in muscle length and relay that information to the CNS via 1a sensory afferents — central to the stretch reflex and to resting muscle tone.

Q7. Which of the following best explains why FPR resolves somatic dysfunction more quickly than counterstrain?

  • FPR uses a direct approach into the restrictive barrier, while counterstrain is indirect
  • FPR requires the patient to actively contract muscles, while counterstrain is entirely passive
  • FPR relies on the crossed extensor reflex to bypass the dysfunctional segment
  • FPR adds a facilitating/activating force (e.g., compression, traction, or torsion) on top of the position of ease, rather than relying on a sustained hold alone

FPR and counterstrain are both indirect, passive techniques built around a position of ease. FPR's distinguishing feature is the added facilitating/activating force, which speeds the release compared to counterstrain's longer sustained hold.

When Is FPR Indicated?

FPR treats both acute and chronic somatic dysfunction. Because it is indirect, it is especially useful for patients with acute somatic dysfunction (e.g., trauma) or for elderly/fragile patients.

  • Muscle relaxation — reduces hypertonicity, tension, pain, and discomfort.
  • Joint mobility — placing dysfunctional segments in a neutral position with a facilitating force improves mobility and addresses restrictions from soft tissue/muscle imbalance.
  • Myofascial dysfunction — targets trigger points and soft tissue restriction, improving tissue texture.
  • Pain management — reduces muscle tension, improves circulation, and promotes tissue healing.

Contraindications & Safety

FPR is generally very safe, but the following should be kept in mind.

Absolute Contraindications

  • Unstable fracture affected by treatment positioning
  • Neurological symptoms brought on by treatment positioning
  • Exacerbation of potentially life-threatening signs (EKG changes, drop in O2 saturation) in a monitored patient

Relative Contraindications

  • Treatment not well tolerated, or significant symptoms/signs occur during the process
  • Comorbidities that raise fracture risk (e.g., severe osteoporosis, malignancy)
  • Moderate-to-severe joint instability
  • Spinal stenosis / nerve root impingement where positioning could worsen symptoms

FPR is also contraindicated if the patient cannot tolerate the required positioning — if the position can't be maintained, treatment is unlikely to succeed, and an alternate technique or positioning should be chosen.

Q8. Which of the following is a relative (not absolute) contraindication to FPR?

  • An unstable fracture in the region being positioned
  • Neurological symptoms provoked by the treatment position
  • Severe osteoporosis
  • EKG changes triggered by the treatment position in a monitored patient

Severe osteoporosis is a relative contraindication (it raises fracture risk but doesn't categorically rule out FPR). Unstable fracture, position-provoked neurological symptoms, and position-triggered life-threatening changes are all absolute contraindications.

How Is FPR Performed?

FPR has two applications: treating tissue texture changes from muscular/myofascial/ligamentous dysfunction, and treating intervertebral joint restriction. The general steps:

  1. Diagnose. Assess for TART changes / somatic dysfunction.
  2. Flatten the AP curve. Mobilize the patient to flatten the anterior-posterior spinal curve at the dysfunctional region, reducing lordotic/kyphotic curvature.
  3. Add facilitating force. Apply a facilitating force axially through the spine/musculature — compression, distraction, torsion, or a combination.
  4. Disengage the barrier. Passively move the dysfunction away from the restrictive barrier, into a position of ease that allows freedom in all planes.
  5. Hold. Hold 3–5 seconds, then release the facilitating force.
  6. Reposition. Gently and passively return the patient to neutral.
  7. Reassess. Reassess the involved region for improvement.
  8. Repeat steps 2–7 as needed.

Q9. While treating T3 FRSR with FPR, the physician moves T3 into a position of ease, then gently distracts the neck while adding torsion to enhance tissue relaxation. What is this combination of distraction and torsion called?

  • Repositioning
  • Activating force
  • Distraction force
  • Torsional force

The "release-enhancing" force applied on top of the position of ease — here, distraction combined with torsion — is the activating force (closely related to the facilitating force applied axially through the tissue).

R6. Which of the following statements correctly defines a muscle spindle?

  • Band of connective tissue that anchors muscle to bone
  • Contractile fibers responsible for the primary force generation in a muscle
  • Neuron that tells a muscle when to contract
  • Sensory receptor in a muscle belly that detects changes in muscle length and conveys this information to the central nervous system

Muscle spindles (intrafusal fibers) are sensory receptors that detect length changes and relay them to the CNS via 1a afferents — they don't generate the muscle's primary contractile force (that's the job of extrafusal fibers).
