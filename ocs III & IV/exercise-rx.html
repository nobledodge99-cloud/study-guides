1. Overview: Why Exercise Prescription Needs More Than FITT

Dr. Wilde’s framingPhysicians often fall short at prescribing exercise (vague advice, veiled threats, negotiation). He wants you to leave with a clear understanding of what the patient needs and how to prescribe it. FITT (Frequency, Intensity, Time, Type) is “a good start” but not customized to the patient’s physical exam; the goal is a truly customized prescription. The material comes from Dr. Vladimir Janda and Dr. Philip Greenman (Principles of Manual Medicine, 3rd ed., ch. 20); it is normally taught over several days.

Objectives (slide 2)

  • Describe upper and lower cross syndromes and how they contribute to pathology.
  • Describe the role of osteopathic manipulative treatment (OMT) and exercise prescription in treating them.
  • Safely and accurately perform, on yourself and a patient: balance training; psoas, piriformis, hamstring, latissimus, pectoral and cervical stretches; abdominal, gluteal and rhomboid/trapezius retraining.
The two quotes to rememberGreenman: “Good exercise is defined not by its volume, but rather by how appropriate.” (Fit athletes can still have pain, so “exercise more” is not enough: assess the quality of exercise.) Janda: “A muscle can be as strong as possible, but if it doesn’t fire at the right time, it’s useless.” Muscles work together: hip extension needs several muscles, and if one is “checked out,” the patient is set up for pain and pathology.
How this guide is builtRed marks what Dr. Wilde stresses (the framework, the firing order, the stage-by-stage prescriptions). Photos are the Greenman/slide figures.

2. Foundational Concepts: Neuro and Muscle (slides 6–9)

Neuro concepts

Core ideaThe spinal cord learns both good and bad behavior.
  1. A musculoskeletal function disturbance (injury, painful stimulus) stimulates mechanoreceptors and nociceptors.
  2. Afferent activity goes to the cord; some signals go up, and reflexes start at cord, brainstem and cortical levels.
  3. Alpha motor neuron stimulation causes muscle fiber contraction.
  4. If prolonged, muscle spindles adapt and the resting muscle tone changes.
  5. If the stimulus persists, you get chronic dysfunction: ongoing muscle tension or spasm, pain and abnormal tone.

The same learning capacity lets the cord be reprogrammed back to normal function through appropriate manual medicine and exercise.

Reciprocal inhibitionThe afferent signal that excites one muscle also sends inhibitory signals to others, ipsilaterally and contralaterally. Ipsilateral example: firing the biceps inhibits the triceps. Contralateral example: standing on two legs then one, the firing hamstring on the standing side inhibits the opposite hamstring. This is why stretching with the opposing muscle active works (the hamstring and pectoral stretches below).

Muscle concepts

  • Muscles don’t think; they respond. Janda saw muscles as a window into the sensorimotor system: they respond to the CNS and react to joints and the peripheral nervous system.
  • Patterns do not differ among individuals, only the degree of imbalance; low back pain patients show the same tightness/weakness pattern as upper motor neuron lesions (for example, cerebral palsy), just smaller.
Tonic (postural) fibersPhasic (dynamic) fibers
Speed / metabolismSlow-twitch, glycolytic (red muscle; contracts for a long time without fatigue)Fast-twitch, oxidative (white; fatigues quickly)
Response to dysfunctionShorten and tighten (also called facilitated)Weaken and become inhibited

“Weak” usually means inhibitedThese imbalances are functional impairments manifesting a reflexive change (pseudoparesis). The phasic muscle is usually not truly weak: something is inhibiting it, and if you remove the inhibitor its strength returns (stretch the tight opposing muscle or treat the segmental dysfunction).

4. Goals, Assessment and the Treatment Framework (slides 15–22)

The goal (both cases)Maximal pain-free movement of the musculoskeletal system in postural balance. Even with degenerative disease on x-ray, OMT will not correct the underlying pathology, but you can still maximize functional capacity. Focus on function, not pain, and place the responsibility back on the patient.
ToolRole
Manipulation (OMT)Restore movement; the patient is passive (most of the course)
ExerciseMaintain the symmetry the manipulation restored; the patient is active and participates

Assessment: the sequence you already know

  • Postural symmetry, range of motion.
  • Special tests for red flags (for example straight leg raise, deep tendon reflexes, peripheral vascular exam): “no musculoskeletal exam is complete without a neurovascular exam.”
  • Regional assessment of the complaint area for somatic dysfunction, tender/counterstrain points and resting muscle length.
  • Greenman adds tests of active control, because static findings do not show how muscles work together.

Treatment: length, strength, control

Restore length (stretching) and strength (retraining), but the bigger goal is control: better muscle balance around joints and across regions protects the osteoarticular system and reduces strain on joints, capsules and ligaments.

The sequence (slide 22)
  1. OMT first (restore symmetry and motion).
  2. Sensorimotor balance training.
  3. Stretch short, tight, hypertonic muscles to symmetry.
  4. Retrain (strengthen) weak, inhibited muscles to balance.
  5. Aerobic conditioning (and integrative exercises).

Hip extension firing pattern (slide 18)
The common abnormal patternThe contralateral erector spinae fires almost immediately, then the hamstring, and the glutes last. It is not the lumbar erector spinae’s job to extend the hip. Pathology increases as asymmetry in the pattern and delay of gluteus maximus firing increase.

Case 1 findings (slide 19)

  • L2 FRS left, L5 FRS right (flexed, rotated and side-bent segmental dysfunctions).
  • Right anterior innominate, left-on-right sacral torsion.
  • Late gluteal firing.
  • Hypertonic iliopsoas, TFL and rectus femoris.
  • Left piriformis counterstrain point.

That is a lower cross picture: tight hip flexors, weak glutes.

6. Sensorimotor Balance Training (slide 23)

  • The sensory and motor systems must function together. Janda: “The sensory system is the key to proper function of the motor system.”
  • A proprioceptive deficit causes local and global dysfunction: the body recruits muscles across other joints to keep balance.
  • Three key areas for balance: the foot, the SI joint and the cervical spine.

Stork stance testing (slide 23)
How he prescribes itStork stance 60 seconds, twice a day, alternating feet: “you brush your teeth twice a day”, so stand on the left foot in the morning and the right in the evening. Attach it to a habit. narration

Stretch rules (all stretches)Stretch to symmetry. Sustained stretch 30 seconds × 3 repetitions on each side, or use post-isometric relaxation (muscle energy) to speed it. Each muscle has a clinician-facilitated stretch and a self-stretch.

8. Retraining Weak Muscles and Aerobic Conditioning (slides 29–34)

Retraining principles (slide 29)

  • Most muscle weakness is due to inhibition. Often you don’t need retraining at all: after stretching the tight muscle, the strength returns.
  • Take care not to overexercise an inhibited muscle. It improves after stretching the opposing muscle or treating the underlying segmental dysfunction (for example, before retraining the gluteus maximus, first stretch the hip capsule, rectus femoris and psoas).
  • If the patient is asymmetrically tight, he will fall into aberrant firing patterns when retraining.
  • Retraining starts at the cortical level (the patient must turn the muscle on on command), with the goal of a subcortical reflex that is less fatigable.

Gluteus maximus

Three exercises: prone straight-leg raise, prone with knee bent off the table, supine with pelvis lifted. Pelvis at 12 o’clock. Tap the glute to cue it (“fire that muscle”); if they cannot turn it on, do not move on. If the glutes go soft and the erector spinae tighten, the patient is extending the hip with the back: reset, and do not reinforce substitution (the patient can put a hand on the glute to feel it stay engaged). narration

Sit backs (slide 31)

Aerobic conditioning (slide 33)

  • Conditioning determines the muscles’ ability to respond to challenge and reduces injury.
  • Choose an activity the patient enjoys: walking, cycling, swimming, elliptical (for most patients, getting them doing anything matters; athletes are sport-specific).
  • Caution with jogging and running: they may perpetuate hip flexor tightness. Rotary exercises should always be followed by elongation exercises.

Follow-up logicExercises take under 20 minutes; conditioning adds 20–30 (all under an hour). See him about 1 week later, then space out; do a structural exam and OMT at each visit; advance stages by readiness. If he is not improving: he is not doing the exercises, the diagnosis is wrong or pathology was missed, or the prescription needs modification.

9. Case 2: Headache (slides 35–43)

The patient42-year-old woman with 10 years of chronic headache, neck stiffness and right hand paresthesia. X-ray: cervical spondylosis (degenerative uncovertebral and facet joints, osteophytes, disc space narrowing, endplate sclerosis); Dr. Wilde would be concerned at her age. Same goal as Case 1: maximal pain-free movement in postural balance, manipulation to restore and exercise to maintain.

Assessment

Postural symmetry, range of motion, and red-flag tests: axial cervical compression / Spurling’s, deep tendon reflexes, peripheral vascular exam; regional C-spine, T-spine and upper extremity for somatic dysfunction, tender points and resting muscle length.

Shoulder abduction firing pattern (slide 40)
AbnormalThe most common variation: levator scapulae, then upper trapezius, with early firing of the (contralateral) quadratus lumborum; the lower trapezius is last. Weak supraspinatus/deltoid → substitution by levator scapulae and upper trapezius → excessive shoulder elevation and loss of scapular stabilization by lower trapezius and serratus anterior → shoulder impingement and more strain on the cervical spine.

Case 2 findings (slide 41)

  • Forward head carriage.
  • C2 ERS right, C3 ERS right, C5 ERS right (stacked extended dysfunctions).
  • Right scapular asymmetry and lift-off in active abduction/adduction (the right scapula moves early and far, then drops and lifts off).
  • Hypertonic right trapezius, latissimus dorsi, pectoralis major and levator scapulae.
  • Bilateral rhomboid counterstrain points.

That is an upper cross picture. Dr. Wilde’s point: the headache’s link to these findings is a reasonable argument even though the treatment is not “directly attached to the head.”

Retraining: lower trapezius and rhomboids

  • Lower trapezius needs retraining: tap the muscle; if they cannot turn it on, have them lift the hand, wrist and forearm off the table first, then the elbow, and hold 5–7 s while extending the arm. T8–12 FRS dysfunctions are major inhibitors of the lower trapezius.
  • Rhomboids and lower trapezius self-strengthen: pull the elbow back to a wall or down toward the floor (great for desk jobs); gripped hands pulled apart (rhomboids) and arms overhead pulled down (lower trapezius, shoulder blades medial and inferior).
