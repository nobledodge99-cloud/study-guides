1. Overview: A High-Level Map of the US System

Dr. Zavala’s framingThe goal is “a high-level overview of the US health care system.” You will not be an expert afterward, but you should have a basic understanding on which you may grow your knowledge over time. He covers payment systems and their history and the structure of the system.

Objectives (slide 2)

  • Understanding health care delivery systems: fee for service (FFS) and value-based care (VBC).
  • Understanding the health care structure: the continuum of care.
  • Processes in health care and team-based care.
The one idea to hold ontoThe system is shifting from paying for volume (fee for service) to paying for value (value-based care), with care delivered by teams that center on the patient.
How this guide is builtRed marks the points he stresses: the definitions, numbers and acronyms he states aloud. A reference list of sources is on his last slide (CMS Innovation Center, Bodenheimer & Sinsky, Institute of Medicine, Nundy et al., and the AMA Health Systems Science textbook).

2. Payment Models: Fee for Service vs Value-Based Care (slides 3, 6)

Fee for service (FFS)Value-based care (VBC)
How it paysThe provider or facility is paid by the visit and by the procedure; a transactional payment for each serviceRewards quality, safety and patient engagement; many models blend FFS with a value component
StrengthSimple, transactionalDrives quality, safety and a better patient experience
WeaknessMay encourage unnecessary care: you are paid more for doing moreHard to do: sophisticated metrics are needed to report quality, safety and patient experience
Who uses itHistorically all insurers, private and government (traditional insurance, Medicaid, Medicare)All payers are now trying to move to value-based models

Bundled payment (example)

The cost of a procedure such as a hip replacement is bundled into one payment covering pre-operative care, the surgery and post-operative care, to drive quality, safety and patient experience. In blended models, the visit or procedure is paid like FFS and there is a bonus for quality, safety and patient engagement (the value component).

5. The ACA, MACRA and the Value-Based Payment Models (slides 9–11)

Affordable Care Act (ACA), March 2010

The Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act, a comprehensive reform law. Three goals:

  1. Make affordable insurance available to more people: subsidies (premium tax credits) for households with income between 100% and 400% of the federal poverty level (FPL).
  2. Expand Medicaid to all adults with income below 138% of the FPL (not all states have expanded).
  3. Support innovative care delivery methods designed to lower costs, which pushed the system toward value-based care.

MACRA (Medicare Access and CHIP Reauthorization Act of 2015)

  • Changes how Medicare rewards clinicians: value over volume.
  • Streamlines multiple quality programs under the Merit-based Incentive Payment System (MIPS).
  • Gives bonus payments for participation in eligible alternative payment models (APMs).

Value-based payment models (slide 11)

ModelWhat it is
Accountable care organizations (ACOs)Led by health care organizations to manage the entire continuum of care, quality and overall cost. Can be run by primary care groups, multispecialty groups, hospital groups, or government systems (Department of Defense, VA). 6. The Value Formula and the Quality Aims (slides 12–17)

The value formulaValue = outcomes and patient experience ÷ cost. A major change from the transactional FFS model; it is challenging because it needs sophisticated metrics. Programs such as patient-centered medical homes (PCMH) and bundled payments (BPCI) aim to advance the triple aim.
Benefits of value-based health care by stakeholder (slide 13)
  • Patients: lower costs and better outcomes; providers: higher satisfaction, fewer inefficiencies; payers: cost control and risk reduction; suppliers: prices aligned with outcomes; society: lower spending and better health.

8. Transitions of Care, Shared Decision Making and Care Coordination (slides 19–23)

Transitions of care

Moving a patient from one setting of care or one clinician to another: floor to floor in a hospital, acute hospital to post-acute setting or home, or specialty to specialty. Transitions are one of the riskiest areas for patients (many errors have been found).

Goals of a successful transition
Prevent adverse events: falls, readmissions, medication errors
Improve the patient experience
Improve quality
Reduce costs

Shared decision making

Communicating with the patient, family or surrogate caregivers about the best available evidence for the recommended care, so they can make informed decisions about treatments, future care plans and the site where care will be delivered.

Care coordination

Care coordinators, social workers and patient navigators are part of the team. They: follow up after discharge (medications filled, appointments made), coordinate post-acute care with other team members, assist with community resources, work with family, and schedule follow-up appointments. Their work is crucial as the system becomes more complex and integrated.

The health care continuum (slide 23)

The health care continuum: pre-acute, acute and post-acute settings (slide 23)
His worked exampleA patient with COPD, cough and shortness of breath goes to urgent care, is sent to the emergency department (physician to physician, nurse to nurse, care team to ambulance; do not duplicate the chest x-ray, which the electronic health record lets you see), is admitted (hospitalist, floor nurses, respiratory and physical therapists), then discharged. Length of stay is a metric hospitals are held accountable for. Discharge options: outpatient rehab, skilled nursing facility, a critical access hospital with swing beds (rural), or home with home health. Not every organization has access to the same record, so information and medications must be transferred. Follow-up returns to the primary care physician (often in a medical home with care coordinators).

9. The Patient-Centered Health Care Delivery System (slide 24)

The goal is to move from the old model with the physician at the center to one with the patient at the center (Skochelak, Health Systems Science).

Patient-centered care delivery system: patient, micro-, meso- and macrosystem (slide 24)
LevelWhat it isExamples
Patient and family (center)Helped with self-care and family and community connectionsSelf-referenced information, schools, pharmacies
MicrosystemThe frontline teams that care for the patient: physicians and other professionals in the clinic or hospital, care coordinatorsVisits, phone calls, portal
MesosystemA collection of microsystems: clinics and programs, often part of a large groupPulmonology clinic, respiratory therapy, physical therapy, primary care office
MacrosystemLarge collections of mesosystems: integrated health systemsHospital, outpatient facilities and clinics under one system, such as Intermountain Health or the Billings Clinic health system

The goal of value-based care is for the patient to move through every level seamlessly, which takes continuous improvement, physician leadership and a focus on the patient’s health.

10. Strategies to Promote High-Value Care (slide 25)

The American Medical Association (AMA) strategies; physicians need to lead in promoting patient-centered, high-value, evidence-based care.

StepStrategyHis example
1Understand the benefits, harms and relative costs of interventionsKnow them for the patient’s care, transitions and specialty care
2Decrease or eliminate interventions that provide no benefit and/or cause harmUnnecessary procedures, labs and radiology; unnecessary CT scans mean unnecessary radiation (but if needed, do it properly)
3Choose interventions and care settings that maximize benefits, minimize harms and reduce costsThe transitions-of-care example
4Customize care plans with patients that incorporate their values and address their concernsShared decision making, evidence-based treatment
5Identify system-level opportunities to improve outcomes, minimize harms and reduce wasteWorking with the system so the process is smooth, safe and appropriate
Closing messageThis is accomplished in a patient-centered system working toward the triple aim: improve the health of the population, improve the patient experience, and reduce the cost of care.
