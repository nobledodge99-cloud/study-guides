02. Congenital/Genetic Syndromes I: Treacher Collins & Hurler

Treacher Collins Syndrome

  • Autosomal dominant genetic condition with strong craniofacial features and normal intelligence
  • Incidence: 1 in 50,000
  • Facial bone hypoplasia — most often a small jaw (micrognathia)
  • Small or absent external ears with stenosis of the auditory canal; poorly developed ossicles → conductive hearing loss
  • Eyelid coloboma with downward-slanting palpebral fissures and missing eyelashes
  • Coloboma (a hole in an eye structure, e.g. iris or optic disc) sometimes seen
  • Cleft palate and dental abnormalities are common

Hurler Syndrome

  • Genetic disorder causing accumulation of intracellular glycosaminoglycans due to an enzyme deficiency — specifically alpha-L-iduronidase
  • Progressive — enlarging head, frontal bossing, and macroglossia develop over time
  • Coarse facial features and short stature
  • Corneal clouding, hirsutism, hearing loss, and often noisy breathing (due to a low nasal bridge)
  • Hepatosplenomegaly; murmurs from mitral or aortic regurgitation
  • Placid demeanor in more severe cases, or aggression in less severe cases
  • If caught before age 2, enzyme replacement therapy or stem cell transplant may help
  • Most patients die within the first decade of life

03. Congenital/Genetic Syndromes II: Fetal Alcohol & Fragile X

Fetal Alcohol Syndrome

  • Key facial features: short palpebral fissure length, smooth philtrum, and a thin upper (vermilion) lip
  • Growth deficits and CNS problems accompany the facial abnormalities
  • Occurs in approximately 8 per 1,000 live births worldwide
  • Risk factors: older maternal age, being unmarried, lack of employment or prenatal care, smoking during pregnancy, and alcohol use during pregnancy
  • Associated CNS problems: seizures, ADHD, and impaired visual acuity
  • Mean intake of less than one drink daily — especially during weeks 6–12 of gestation — is the number one cause
  • Microcephaly often accompanies the short palpebral fissure length, thin vermilion border, and smooth philtrum; reduced height and weight are also common
  • This condition is irreversible; treatment is supportive care only

Fragile X Syndrome

  • Genetic disorder caused by expansion of the FMR1 gene on the X chromosome
  • Variable presentation: intellectual disability, behavior problems, macroorchidism, and characteristic facial features (long face, long ears, prominent jaw)
  • Presentation depends on the degree of dominance of the mutation
  • Developmental delay with facial features should trigger suspicion; differential includes autism and ADHD
  • Treatment is supportive — SSRIs and Ritalin for behavioral issues; anticonvulsants for associated seizures

04. Endocrine Disorders: Acromegaly, Cushing's & Hypothyroidism

Acromegaly

  • Due to excess growth hormone secretion from the pituitary gland or an ectopic source — most often a benign, hormone-secreting pituitary adenoma
  • Usually occurs over age 50; no demographic predominance by gender, age, or ethnicity
  • Can be associated with Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia type 1 and secondary hyperparathyroidism (elevated serum calcium, elevated PTH)
  • Maxillofacial changes are the most prominent finding as tissues enlarge
  • Elevated PTH/calcium and hyperparathyroidism cause bone pain, osteoporosis, pathological fractures, and vascular/soft tissue calcification
  • Also associated with joint pain, hypertension, heart failure, and obstructive sleep apnea

Cushing's Disease

  • Due to excess ACTH — usually from a pituitary source — causing overstimulation of the adrenal glands and increased tissue exposure to glucocorticoids
  • About a 4:1 female-to-male ratio, most common in reproductive-age women; affects 2–3 people per million each year
  • Usually treatable with surgery and/or medical treatment
  • "Cushingoid appearance": moon-shaped facies, hirsutism, buffalo hump, dowager's hump between the scapulae, abdominal obesity, elevated blood pressure
  • Fragile skin that heals poorly; chronic adrenal stimulation can also lead to fatigue

Hypothyroidism

  • Due to low thyroid hormone
  • Facial findings: dull expression, facial puffiness, sometimes macroglossia
  • Systemic findings: weight gain, cold intolerance, slowed mentation, fatigue, dry skin, coarse/brittle hair, bradycardia
  • Women may have menstrual irregularities, usually with heavy bleeding
  • Thyroid gland size is variable (large, small, or normal) — not a reliable indicator of hypothyroidism
  • If untreated, can result in myxedema coma — a loss of brain function resulting from severe, long-standing, untreated hypothyroidism

05. Ear Exam: Inspection, Palpation & Otoscopic Technique

  • Inspection: Size, shape, symmetry, landmarks, position, color, deformities/lesions
  • Palpation: Auricles, mastoid, and tragus
  • Otoscopic exam — canal: Color, swelling, foreign body/debris
  • Otoscopic exam — tympanic membrane: Landmarks, color, contour, perforation, and mobility (assessed via pneumatic otoscopy)
  • Hearing: Gross testing, followed by Weber and Rinne testing (see Section 09)

06. Ear Position & Auricle Abnormalities

Ear Position

Low-set ears can be found in numerous syndromes — including Down, Turner, Noonan, Patau, Edwards, and Fragile X syndromes, to name a few. Recognizing low ear position is an important clue to underlying syndromic diagnoses.

Normal Congenital Variants

  • Preauricular skin tag — a very common, normal variant
  • Auricular sinus/pit — a developmental defect of the branchial arches; can become infected
  • Darwinian tubercle — a thickening along the upper ridge of the helix
Preauricular pits: These anomalies can indicate risk for hearing impairment — patients should be screened with a hearing test. There is also an inconclusive association between preauricular skin lesions and renal tract abnormalities in infants; a renal ultrasound should be considered, though evidence is not strong enough to mandate it in every case.

Pathologic Auricle Findings

  • Cauliflower ear: Usually due to blunt trauma — a hematoma separates the cartilage from the perichondrium, then scar tissue fills the gap between the two layers, causing the tissue to fold in on itself and appear pale, giving it a cauliflower-like appearance
  • Tophi: Usually caused by gout; other etiologies include actinic keratosis, basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, amyloidosis, and rheumatoid nodules
  • Sebaceous/epidermal inclusion cysts: Fluid-filled cysts that occur within the epidermis

07. Ear Canal Abnormalities & Otitis Externa

Why the otoscopic exam matters: Untreated otitis media can progress to a generalized, severe facial infection — underscoring the importance of a careful otoscopic exam to identify normal versus abnormal findings early.

Otitis Externa

  • Etiology: Usually Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Staphylococcus aureus; consider fungal or allergic causes if duration is longer than 3 months
  • Associations: Prolonged swimming, humidity, earplugs/hearing aids, aggressive ear cleaning, foreign body/impacted cerumen, or dermatologic conditions such as eczema and psoriasis
  • Most common in children and immunocompromised patients
  • Treatment: Topical antibiotic/steroid drops (e.g., polymyxin B/neomycin/hydrocortisone) are the mainstay — oral antibiotics are usually not effective
  • Fluoroquinolones are preferred, especially if the tympanic membrane is ruptured, due to their antimicrobial spectrum, anti-inflammatory effects, lack of ototoxicity, and low risk of allergic reaction
  • First, gently clean the ear canal; a solution of 1 part white vinegar to 3 parts water can help cleanse the area alongside prescribed drops
  • Tools such as an ear wick, ear curette, and tray/ear flush are commonly used for cerumen impaction removal

Otitis Media

  • Etiology: Most commonly viral; bacterial causes include Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, and Moraxella catarrhalis
  • Most common in children
  • If left untreated, 50% of all cases will clear without antibiotics, but 1 in 400 will progress to acute mastoiditis — a life-threatening ear infection; very rarely, meningitis can occur from untreated otitis media

Treatment

  • Pain control first — oral or topical agents; decongestants may be considered depending on the patient
  • OMT can help with symptoms: Galbreath maneuver, auricular drainage technique, and lymphatic pump
  • Antibiotics are indicated for severe infection, duration >48 hours, fever >102°F, or age <2 years
  • Amoxicillin given twice daily is standard; cefdinir or another cephalosporin if penicillin-allergic
  • Duration is usually 5–10 days based on age and severity

09. Hearing Loss Exam: Weber & Rinne Tests

Normally, air conduction transmits sound about twice as long as bone conduction, because the middle ear apparatus is much more sensitive to sound transmitted through air than through bone to the cochlea.

ResultRinne TestWeber Test
NormalAir conducted > bone conducted, bilaterallyMidline
Conductive hearing lossBC > AC in affected ear; AC > BC in unaffected earLateralizes to the affected ear
Sensorineural hearing lossAC > BC in both earsLateralizes to the unaffected ear, away from the affected ear
Mixed hearing lossBC > AC in affected ear; AC > BC in unaffected earLateralizes to affected or unaffected ear (variable)
Review tip: If the Weber/Rinne relationship is hard to recall, it can help to revisit the general physical exam material from first year — the underlying air conduction vs. bone conduction physiology hasn't changed.

10. Nose Exam: Inspection & Palpation

  • Inspection: Size, shape, symmetry, position, color, deformities/lesions, and patency of the nares
  • Palpation: Tenderness, masses, and any cartilage or bone displacement
  • Otoscopic nose exam: Septum and mucosa — assess color, alignment, discharge, turbinate swelling, and perforation
  • Sinuses (frontal and maxillary): Inspect for swelling; palpate for tenderness
Endoscopic pearl: On nasal endoscopy following decongestant administration, the middle turbinate becomes visible — normally it would not be seen without this step.

FrequencyMost common type — accounts for 90% of nosebleedsLess common

Typical patientAny ageOlder patients with fragile vessels (hypertension, atherosclerosis, coagulopathy, weakened tissue)
Common causesArid climates, inhaled irritants, hypertension, coagulopathy, inhaled drug use (e.g., cocaine)Same vascular risk factors, in more fragile/older vasculature
BleedingUsually visible on inspectionProfuse, due to larger vessels

Persisting bleed? If anterior treatment doesn't stop the bleeding, it is likely a posterior bleed instead. Also monitor patients with nasal packing for toxic shock syndrome — though given its rarity, prophylactic antibiotics are not frequently used.

12. Rhinitis & Acute Rhinosinusitis

  • Acute rhinosinusitis is symptomatic inflammation of the nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses lasting less than 4 weeks; it is often a secondary infection following a viral upper respiratory infection
  • Etiology: Most cases are viral, though S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, and M. catarrhalis are common bacterial causes
  • Anything that causes swelling in the sinuses or impairs ciliary movement of mucus can trigger sinusitis — changes in temperature or air pressure, and activities like swimming or diving, are common triggers
  • Assessing tenderness by percussion or palpation over the sinuses is crucial in determining a viral versus bacterial cause
  • When mucous membranes fail to clear secretions and cilia are impaired, stagnant mucus in the sinus can become secondarily infected with bacteria

Treatment

  • Mild to moderate: Analgesics, saline irrigation, intranasal steroids, and possibly decongestants and antihistamines — notably, no antibiotics
  • Suspect bacterial etiology when: Symptoms last longer than 10 days, temperature is over 102°F, symptoms worsen after an initial mild upper respiratory infection improved, or purulent discharge is present
  • Bacterial treatment: Amoxicillin-clavulanate is preferred; doxycycline can be used if the patient is allergic to penicillins

13. Mouth & Throat Exam: Inspection & Palpation

  • Inspection: Lips, buccal mucosa, gums, teeth, all surfaces of the tongue, hard palate, and posterior oropharynx (soft palate, uvula, and tonsils or lack thereof)
  • Palpation: Lips (symmetry, color, swelling); teeth (dental cavities, missing teeth, surface abnormalities); gingiva and buccal mucosa (color, lesions, tenderness)
  • Tongue: Inspect for color, symmetry, swelling, and ulcerations; this also assesses the function of cranial nerve XII
  • Palpate the tongue; inspect the palate and uvula; elicit the gag reflex to test cranial nerves IX and X; inspect the oropharyngeal characteristics of the tonsils and posterior pharyngeal wall
Practice Question

14. Cleft Lip/Palate & Dental Abnormalities

  • Cleft lip and palate: Varying degrees of severity — ranging from involvement of solely the lip to the entire hard and soft palate. These are repaired surgically.
  • Dental abnormalities: Breakdown of enamel, usually due to acidity, leading to pain and sensitivity
  • Acute gingivitis: Erythema of the gum tissue, usually secondary to poor hygiene; the tissue is often easily friable

15. Oral Mucosal Abnormalities: Thrush & Herpangina

Thrush

  • An oral infection caused by an overgrowth of Candida albicans
  • Most commonly seen in infants, immunosuppressed patients, and patients on antibiotics or chemotherapy
  • Treated with nystatin, plus a systemic antifungal if the patient is over 6 months of age and it is needed
  • Consider evaluating for HIV or another immunosuppressive condition, especially if recurrent
  • In infants, replace bottles and pacifiers to prevent reinfection

Herpangina

  • Small, blister-like ulcers and vesicles on the palate and posterior pharynx
  • Most commonly caused by Coxsackievirus A
  • Very painful, often associated with fever, sore throat, headache, and neck pain
  • Differs from hand-foot-mouth disease because the ulcers are located in the posterior pharynx
  • Self-limiting; care is supportive

16. Tongue Abnormalities

  • Geographic tongue: Appearance caused by a loss of papillae; can be linked to a vitamin B deficiency. No treatment is necessary, it is not contagious, and can be a little tender — usually more prominent when the patient also has a fever or upper respiratory infection.
  • Fissured tongue: Usually genetic and usually asymptomatic; sometimes associated with a vitamin A deficiency
  • Hairy leukoplakia: One of the earliest signs of HIV infection; can also be associated with pipe smoking and chewing tobacco or snuff. Resembles thrush and can sometimes be misdiagnosed as such. Usually painless and rarely undergoes malignant transformation.
  • Oral cancers: Generally aggressive; main risk factors are chronic alcohol use and smoking. Detection is often delayed because of inadequate examinations, or because findings are assumed to be thrush or hairy leukoplakia — getting a thorough medical and social history is essential to identify risk factors.
Practice Questions

17. Acute Tonsillitis: Diagnosis & Treatment

  • Usually caused by gram-positive bacteria
  • First, evaluate the airway and ensure the absence of an abscess or epiglottitis
  • If a rapid Group A beta-hemolytic strep test is positive, penicillin is the first choice for treatment — oral penicillin or amoxicillin in adults (an IM option exists but is not commonly used)
  • Alternatives: first-generation cephalosporins, clindamycin, and macrolides
  • If the patient is very swollen, an intramuscular steroid may be used instead of an oral one
  • Supportive care: warm drinks, gargling, and antipyretic analgesics
  • Consider tonsillectomy (plus or minus adenoidectomy) if tonsillitis becomes a recurrent problem, occurring 3–4 times within a year
