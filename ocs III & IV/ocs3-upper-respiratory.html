1. Introduction & the ABCS Framework

The upper respiratory system is the ENT of HEENT — ears, nose, mouth/throat — along with the sinuses as they connect into the nasal and oral pharynx, extending into the neck and trachea. The lower respiratory system is the rest of the trachea, the bronchi, and the lungs.

Every system can be approached using the same four-part framework:

  • A — Autonomics: sympathetic and parasympathetic innervation to the system and their actions, plus relevant Chapman's points
  • B — Biomechanics: related myofascial and skeletal structures, and how restrictions in them affect the system
  • C — Circulatory: arteries, veins, and lymphatics — ideal circulation is vital for the system's function
  • S — Screening: additional areas to screen that may affect (or be affected by) the system
Required reading: this lecture plus Chapter 4 of the MCOM-OMM III & IV Manual on Upper Respiratory/ENT.
Section 1

Q1. Which structures are included in the "upper respiratory system" as defined in this lecture?

The upper respiratory system is the ENT of HEENT (ears, nose, mouth/throat) along with the sinuses, extending into the neck and trachea. The lower respiratory system picks up from there with the rest of the trachea, bronchi, and lungs.

2. A — Autonomics

Sympathetic & Parasympathetic Innervation

Pearl: sympathetics go back up through the cervical ganglia, mainly the superior cervical ganglion, before reaching their targets.
Section 2.1

Q3. A patient has increased sympathetic tone. What effect would this have on nasal/sinus secretions and the trachea?

Sympathetic innervation decreases the quantity of secretions but thickens them, while dilating the trachea (making it easier to take in air) — the classic "fight or flight" pattern.
Section 2.1

Q4. Which cranial nerve provides parasympathetic innervation specifically to the trachea?

Parasympathetic innervation to the upper respiratory system is mainly via CN VII and CN IX, but the trachea specifically is innervated by CN X (vagus).
Section 2.1

Q5. A patient has increased parasympathetic tone, producing a runny nose. What would be the expected effect on the trachea?

Parasympathetic influence increases and thins secretions (runny nose) while simultaneously constricting the trachea, making it more difficult to breathe deeply — the opposite pattern from sympathetic tone.

3. B — Biomechanics

Cranium & Cranial Nerve Foramina

All cranial nerves originate from the cranium and must pass through the skull to reach their targets — meaning dysfunction/restriction in any cranial bone can directly affect the upper respiratory system.

  • The pterygoid artery and nerve pass through the foramen lacerum, composed of the sphenoid, occiput, and temporal bone.
  • CN IX (glossopharyngeal) passes through the jugular foramen, composed of the temporal and occipital bones.
  • The pterygopalatine ganglion is close to the maxilla, sphenoid, and palatine bones. It is mostly parasympathetic and can be stimulated to thin sinus secretions and increase their outflow from the head.
Section 3.1

Q9. Which bones make up the foramen lacerum, through which the pterygoid artery and nerve pass?

The foramen lacerum, through which the pterygoid artery and nerve pass, is composed of the sphenoid, occiput, and temporal bones.
Section 3.1

Q10. Stimulation of the pterygopalatine ganglion would be expected to have what effect on sinus secretions?

The pterygopalatine ganglion is mostly parasympathetic and can be stimulated to thin sinus secretions and increase their flow out of the head — useful therapeutically for sinus congestion.

Ear, Nose, Mouth & Throat

The Ear

Ear Anatomy & Common Pathology
RegionStructuresPathology
Outer earExternal auditory canal (where cerumen forms and can trap fluid), auricle, surrounding fasciaOtitis externa ("swimmer's ear") — painful, often with purulent exudate from the canal
Inner earCochlea (hearing); semicircular ducts (balance); CN VII passes through, CN VIII terminates here (hearing)Vertigo — often from displaced otoliths sending invalid signals to the brain
Middle earRuns from the tympanic membrane to the oval window (behind the stapes); Eustachian tubeOtitis media — erythema of the TM, bubbles/air pockets behind the TM, bulging or retracted TM
Pearl: less intense vertigo can sometimes be relieved by mobilizing the temporal bones using cranial techniques.

The Eustachian tube drains fluid from the middle ear to the nasopharynx (preventing fluid accumulation and infection) and equalizes pressure between the middle ear and nasopharynx. Maneuvers like yawning, chewing gum, or pinching the nose while blowing out help open it. Two small muscles facilitate opening/closing: the tensor veli palatini (connects at the base of the tube) and the levator veli palatini (connects more distally along the cartilaginous portion). If these muscles get tight, the tube can kink. The petrosphenoid junction can also affect air/fluid flow if restricted.

Pediatric pearl: otitis media is a bigger problem in young children because their Eustachian tubes are less sharply angled and more convoluted than in adults.

The Nose & Sinuses

The frontal, ethmoid, and maxillary sinuses all drain into the nose. The frontal and maxillary sinuses are the most common sites of inflammation/infection with pain; the ethmoid and sphenoid sinuses are less commonly infected.

Turbinates act as air filters, trapping dust and particles. They swell when inflamed (from infection or allergens) — protective, but this restricts airflow and mucosal drainage, setting the stage for bacterial infection.

The nasopharynx connects the nose to the oropharynx and rest of the respiratory system, and is where the Eustachian tubes open.

The Mouth

The mouth is the opening for food and air, connecting to the rest of the respiratory system at the oropharynx. The tongue is essential for mastication and swallowing. Small muscles like the genioglossus and mylohyoid can affect the upper airway via their attachments to the hyoid bone. The tongue is also subject to edema (secondary to inflammation), which can narrow the airway and restrict breathing.

The Throat

The throat is where everything comes together biomechanically. The tonsils and adenoids (lymphoid tissue at the back of the throat, near the nasopharynx) sit at a common site of microbe/allergen entry for protection; repeated use or need can lead to edema/inflammation, causing snoring and airway obstruction (removal is no longer common unless problems are severe). The epiglottis protects the trachea from food by directing it to the esophagus — epiglottitis is rare but is a medical emergency because of its position, where it can quickly occlude the airway.

Section 3.2

Q11. A patient presents with ear pain and purulent drainage from the external auditory canal after a week of swimming. Which condition is most likely, and where does it occur?

Otitis externa (swimmer's ear) occurs in the outer ear (external auditory canal) and can be quite painful, often accompanied by purulent exudate leaking from the canal.
Section 3.2

Q12. Which two muscles facilitate opening and closing of the Eustachian tube, and what happens if they become tight?

The tensor veli palatini (connecting at the base of the tube) and levator veli palatini (connecting more distally along the cartilaginous portion) facilitate opening and closing of the Eustachian tube — if these muscles get tight, the tube can kink.
Section 3.2

Q13. A young child has more frequent episodes of otitis media than adults typically do. What anatomic feature of the pediatric Eustachian tube explains this predisposition?

Otitis media is a bigger problem in young children because their Eustachian tubes are less sharply angled and more convoluted than in adults (who have a flatter, more angled tube), predisposing to poor drainage and infection.
Section 3.2

Q14. Which two paranasal sinuses are the most common sites of infection, inflammation, and pain?

The frontal and maxillary sinuses are the common sites of inflammation and infection with resultant pain; it is less common to get infections in the ethmoid and sphenoid sinuses.
Section 3.2

Q15. A child presents with drooling, stridor, and appears anxious and unwilling to lie flat, with a rapid onset of symptoms. Which upper respiratory structure's dysfunction should be immediately suspected as a possible emergency?

Epiglottitis, though rare, is a medical emergency because the epiglottis is positioned where it can quickly occlude the airway — drooling, stridor, and an unwillingness to lie flat are classic red-flag features.

The Neck

  • The superior cervical ganglion is found near the C2-C3 and C3-C4 cervical segments.
  • The anterior cervical fascia can get tight and restrict lymphatic drainage.
  • The hyoid bone connects several muscles from the neck and mouth — if restricted, it can lead to problems with breathing and swallowing.
  • The thoracic inlet is key to lymphatic flow and needs to be addressed whenever lymphatic drainage is a consideration.
Clinical pearl: practically any congestion in the head or neck can be decreased by opening the thoracic inlet with MFR or BLT. Improved lymphatic drainage enables medications to work more effectively and shortens the duration of symptoms for many ENT problems — from sinusitis, to otitis media, to pharyngitis.
Section 3.3

Q16. A patient with chronic sinus congestion is treated with myofascial release or BLT to the thoracic inlet. What is the rationale for this intervention?

The thoracic inlet is key to lymphatic flow, and practically any congestion in the head or neck can be decreased by opening it with MFR or BLT — improving lymphatic drainage, helping medications work more effectively, and shortening symptom duration for many ENT problems.
Section 3.3

Q17. A patient has restriction of the hyoid bone. Which two functions are most likely to be affected?

The hyoid bone connects several muscles from the neck and mouth — if it's restricted, this can lead to problems with breathing and swallowing.

4. C — Circulation

Arterial Supply

The carotid artery provides most of the blood supply to upper respiratory structures. In the upper cervical spine, it branches into the internal and external carotid arteries. Further branches supply the rest of the ENT structures, including the maxillary, facial, temporal, auricular, and ophthalmic arteries.

Important distinction: the vertebral artery is important to consider when performing cervical HVLA (given its relationship to the C1 transverse foramen), but it does not supply the upper respiratory structures — it supplies the brain instead.
Section 4.1

Q18. Which artery provides most of the blood supply to the upper respiratory structures discussed in this lecture?

The carotid artery provides most of the blood supply to the upper respiratory structures, branching into internal and external carotid arteries in the upper cervical spine, with further branches (maxillary, facial, temporal, auricular, ophthalmic) supplying the rest.
Section 4.1

Q19. Why is the vertebral artery specifically mentioned as important when performing cervical HVLA, even though it doesn't supply the upper respiratory structures?

The vertebral artery runs through the transverse foramina of the cervical vertebrae (including C1) and supplies the brain — its anatomic relationship there makes it an important safety consideration during cervical HVLA, even though it plays no role in upper respiratory blood supply.

Q20. Fluid from the middle ear and maxillary sinuses drains via which venous pathway?

The external jugular pathway (maxillary vein → retromandibular vein → external jugular) specifically drains the middle ear and maxillary sinuses, while the internal jugular pathway drains just about everything else in the head and neck.

Lymphatic drainage from the tonsils/adenoids and submental region flows down the anterior/posterior, deep/superficial lymphatic chains of the neck into the right lymphatic duct and/or the subclavian lymphatic trunk.

5. S — Screening

Beyond the head and neck themselves, several other regions and systems should be screened when evaluating upper respiratory complaints:

Head & Neck

  • OA (occipitoatlantal joint), cervical spine, anterior cervical fascia, hyoid bone
  • Anterior and posterior cervical lymphatic chains
  • The face — looking for tension/fascial restriction that could decrease fluid flow
  • Tenderness around the sinuses, ears, and the submandibular region of the jaw

Other Regions

  • Thorax — for sympathetic reflexes
  • Thoracic inlet — a major site of constriction, important for vascular drainage
  • Ribs — especially the 1st and 2nd (attachments of the scalenes and relationship to the thoracic inlet), and the rest of the rib cage for restrictions limiting rib motion/airflow (the rib cage acts as a pump for lymphatic and venous drainage)
  • Abdominal diaphragm — another important influence on lymphatic and venous drainage
  • Upper extremities — particularly muscles attaching to the clavicle and scapula, which can influence the thoracic inlet and/or limit effective respiration

Other Systems

  • Lungs — listen for lung sounds
  • Signs of general inflammation — fever, erythema, edema
  • Neuro system
  • Psych — upper respiratory issues can be frustrating and lead to depression, anxiety, and other mood disorders
Section 5

Q22. Why are the 1st and 2nd ribs specifically highlighted as important screening areas for upper respiratory dysfunction?

The 1st and 2nd ribs are highlighted because of the scalene muscle attachments and their relationship with the thoracic inlet — restriction here can limit thoracic inlet mobility and impair lymphatic/venous drainage from the head and neck.
Section 5

Q23. A patient with chronic sinusitis reports increasing frustration and low mood related to their ongoing symptoms. According to this lecture's screening framework, which additional system should be considered?

The screening framework specifically includes psychological/mood considerations, since upper respiratory issues can be frustrating and contribute to depression, anxiety, and other mood disorders.
Section 5

Q24. Why is the abdominal diaphragm included as a screening area for upper respiratory complaints?

The abdominal diaphragm is an important influence on lymphatic and venous drainage generally (via its pumping action), which is why it's included as a screening area even for complaints centered on the head and neck.

6. Case 1: Acute Sinusitis

Building the Differential

CC / HPI KC is a 25-year-old female presenting with a headache. She's had a bad headache (7/10) for 3 days, progressively worsening. Pain is worse when leaning forward; she feels like her head is going to explode. Slight subjective fever, cough, and sore throat. Denies dizziness, vision changes, and difficulty swallowing or breathing. PMH: no chronic illness, seasonal allergies. Meds: none besides an IUD; recently took Tylenol. Social: PhD student, drinks multiple cups of coffee daily, admits to a poor diet, sexually active/monogamous. Her boyfriend has had cold symptoms recently.

Reasoning through the HPI:

  • Headache alone → migraine jumps onto the differential
  • 3 days, progressively worsening, worse leaning forward, "head about to explode" → sinus headache/sinusitis rises above migraine
  • Slight fever → infectious process (viral or bacterial) becomes more likely
  • Cough + sore throat → consider a combination of sinusitis and pharyngitis
  • Denies dizziness, vision changes, or trouble swallowing/breathing → makes vertigo-related causes and epiglottitis/severe pharyngitis much less likely
  • No chest findings/vitals yet to rule pneumonia in or out
  • Seasonal allergies → allergic sinusitis/rhinitis goes on the differential
  • Boyfriend's recent cold symptoms → supports an infectious (likely viral) cause
Section 6.1

Q25. KC's headache worsens when she leans forward, and she describes a sensation like her head is going to explode. How should this specific detail shift her differential diagnosis?

Headache pain that worsens with leaning forward, along with a sense of pressure ("head about to explode"), is characteristic of sinus pressure/sinusitis, and should raise that possibility above a simple migraine.
Section 6.1

Q26. KC denies dizziness, vision changes, and difficulty swallowing or breathing. What does this help rule out or deprioritize on her differential?

Denying dizziness argues against significant inner ear/vertiginous pathology, and denying trouble swallowing or breathing argues against a severe airway threat like epiglottitis or a severe pharyngeal process — both become much less likely.

Physical & Osteopathic Exam

Physical Exam Vitals: Temp 99.8°F, Pulse 88, Resp 16, SpO2 97% RA (not a true fever, but she may have taken Tylenol). General: uncomfortable but not in respiratory distress. HEENT: eyes/ears normal (no conjunctivitis or otitis); nose red and swollen, tender over the frontal sinuses to percussion; scattered cervical lymphadenopathy. CV: RRR, no murmurs. Lungs: clear.
Osteopathic Structural Exam OA restricted; C3 somatic dysfunction (superior cervical ganglion is very near C3, consistent with a viscerosomatic reflex); anterior cervical fascia and thoracic inlet tight (likely decreasing venous/lymphatic outflow from the head — "blood gets in, but not out," building pressure and worsening the headache); T2–T4 somatic dysfunction (another viscerosomatic reflex — tissues here feel "rubbery" rather than solid/bony); abdominal diaphragm not moving well (likely exhaled, from coughing — further decreasing lymphatic flow and slowing the immune system); tenderness over the frontal sinuses.
Section 6.2

Q27. KC's clear lungs, normal respiratory rate, and lack of respiratory distress on exam help rule out which diagnosis from her differential?

Clear lungs, a normal respiratory rate, and no respiratory distress argue strongly against pneumonia or any significant lower respiratory process, allowing the physician to focus the differential on the upper respiratory tract.
Section 6.2

Q28. KC's anterior cervical fascia and thoracic inlet are found to be tight on osteopathic exam. How does this relate to her headache, according to the reasoning in this case?

Tight anterior cervical fascia and thoracic inlet likely decrease venous and lymphatic outflow from the head — blood/fluid gets in but has trouble getting out, building up pressure and contributing to the headache.
Section 6.2

Q29. Somatic dysfunction is found at C3 in this patient. Why does this location make particular physiologic sense given her presumed diagnosis?

The superior cervical ganglion sits very near C3, so finding somatic dysfunction there is consistent with a viscerosomatic reflex arising from the sympathetic chain's relationship to her upper respiratory pathology.

Diagnosis & Plan

Working Diagnosis Acute frontal sinusitis — most likely viral at this point (sinusitis needs to persist 10–14 days before being considered bacterial), especially given her boyfriend's recent cold. Allergic sinusitis is less likely given the inflammatory picture (including fever). Migraine is possible but unlikely, and would be secondary to the sinusitis/rhinitis if present. Somatic dysfunction of the head, cervical spine, thoracic spine, and abdomen/ribs is also definitively present.
Plan
  • No imaging at this time (acute presentation); consider later only if it becomes chronic
  • Switch to ibuprofen (helps with inflammation); nasal saline for drainage; avoid Sudafed (can cause sleepiness or anxiety depending on the patient, and can increase congestion/sympathetic tone)
  • OMT: muscle energy or BLT to OA and C3; muscle energy to the thorax; myofascial release to the anterior cervical fascia, thoracic inlet, and abdominal diaphragm; work on the sinuses and anterior/posterior lymphatic chains; rib raising if time allows
  • Patient education: explain that antibiotics are unlikely to help right now; teach her self-administered OMT at home; encourage plenty of non-coffee fluids and hot showers to ease sinus congestion
  • Follow up in 1 week (she can cancel if resolved) — if not improved, consider antibiotics at that time
Big picture: the OMT priorities here are (1) free up the thoracic inlet and abdominal diaphragm (with rib raising) to help the immune system work more efficiently, and (2) free up the facial/cervical restrictions preventing the body from healing itself. Anything that helps the immune system work better helps the patient get better faster.
Section 6.3

Q30. Why is KC's acute sinusitis classified as most likely viral rather than bacterial at this point in her presentation?

Sinusitis symptoms typically need to persist 10-14 days before a bacterial cause becomes more likely; at 3 days (and with a sick contact), a viral etiology is most probable.
Section 6.3

Q31. Why is KC specifically counseled to avoid Sudafed (pseudoephedrine) for her sinus symptoms?

Sudafed (pseudoephedrine) can have variable side effects ranging from sleepiness to anxiety depending on the patient, and can paradoxically increase congestion and sympathetic tone — reasons to avoid it in this case.
Section 6.3

Q32. Which two osteopathic structures are identified as the highest OMT priorities for KC, to help her immune system work more efficiently?

The highest OMT priorities identified are freeing up the thoracic inlet and the abdominal diaphragm (with rib raising to help balance the visceral-somatic reflexes) to help the immune system function more efficiently.

7. Case 2: Otitis Externa

History & Exam

CC / HPI A 3-year-old male is brought in by his father for left ear pain, which started this morning. He has a history of a few prior ear infections, and his father would like an antibiotic. He denies fever or other symptoms. He was previously happy and healthy; no known sick contacts, though the family was at the beach about a week ago. Nobody else in the family is symptomatic. His ear hurts to touch.
PMH Born full-term via normal vaginal delivery, no complications. A few prior ear infections but no major illnesses. Up to date on vaccinations. No injuries or hospitalizations. NKDA. Meds: OTC Tylenol PRN for pain.
Physical Exam Vitals: all normal for age. General: normocephalic, atraumatic. Right ear: canal patent, TM pearly grey, normal. Left ear: tender to palpation over the tragus, pain with traction/pulling on the ear or with speculum insertion; external auditory canal erythematous and very wet; TM shows mild erythema, no fluid behind it, no bulging. Nose/mouth: normal. Neck: full ROM, no cervical lymphadenopathy. CV/lungs: normal.
Section 7.1

Q33. This patient's father requests antibiotics based on a history of prior ear infections. Which exam finding is most specific for localizing this episode to the outer ear rather than the middle ear?

Pain with traction on the auricle/tragus or with speculum insertion, along with a wet, erythematous external canal, localizes the process to the outer ear (otitis externa) rather than the middle ear — a key distinguishing exam maneuver from otitis media, which does not typically cause pain with ear traction.
Section 7.1

Q34. Which detail in this child's social history is a relevant risk factor for his current presentation?

Recent water/beach exposure is a classic risk factor for otitis externa ("swimmer's ear"), since retained moisture in the ear canal promotes the maceration and infection characteristic of this condition.
Section 7.1

Q35. Which finding on this exam argues against a diagnosis of acute otitis media as the primary process?

Acute otitis media classically shows a bulging TM with fluid behind it; the absence of bulging/effusion here, combined with pain provoked specifically by manipulating the outer ear, points away from otitis media and toward otitis externa instead.

Diagnosis & Plan

Osteopathic Structural Exam Head: OA dysfunction, fascial tension around the left ear. Neck: C4 dysfunction, hyoid pulled to the right; T3 dysfunction. Thorax: thoracic inlet dysfunction; T3-4 dysfunction. Ribs: left 1st rib and right ribs 4-7 inhaled; diaphragm dysfunction.
Diagnosis Otitis externa of the left ear, with somatic dysfunction of the head, neck, thorax, and ribs.
Plan
  • No labs or imaging needed

Section 7.2

Q36. Given the diagnosis of otitis externa, which treatment approach is most appropriate?

Otitis externa is treated with topical (ototopical) antibiotic drops to the affected ear, along with advice to avoid swimming until resolved and to use ear plugs with future swimming.

Q37. On osteopathic structural exam, this child's hyoid is found pulled to the right, with dysfunction at C4 and the thoracic inlet on the same side as his ear pain. What does this pattern most likely represent?

Given the anatomic and fascial relationships discussed throughout this lecture (anterior cervical fascia, hyoid attachments, thoracic inlet as a lymphatic bottleneck), somatic dysfunction near the affected ear is consistent with the same biomechanical/circulatory principles used to explain Case 1 — supporting a comprehensive OMT approach rather than treating the ear in isolation.
