1. Overview: Four Cases

Dr. Riesberg’s framingA case-based 15–20 minute lecture. Objectives: characterize and recognize commonly observed abnormalities of the endocrine system (history, physical findings) and discuss evaluation and treatment options. Four patients, four diagnoses, each with a classic exam clue.

His recurring themeThese conditions have non-specific presentations and are often missed (case 3 was missed at several primary care visits). Start with a thorough history and physical, and for the thyroid always look first at the TSH. Diagnosis often needs ongoing follow-up and lab evaluation.

Diagnosis: myxedema coma“A specifically insidious and dangerous type of hypothyroidism.” It does not require a comatose state: severely low thyroid hormone causes confusion or altered mental status that, together with the physical findings of severe hypothyroidism, defines it. No precipitant was identified. Treated in the ICU with intravenous thyroid hormone replacement; over days her vitals and mental status normalized, then rehab. At one year: hair regrown, periorbital swelling gone, foot skin normal.

Findings of hypothyroidism

  • Most manifestations come from either a generalized reduction of metabolic processes (fatigue, cold intolerance, bradycardia, weakness) or accumulation of matrix glycosaminoglycans in the interstitial spaces (coarse hair, hoarse voice, myxedema).
  • Severity ranges from nearly asymptomatic to severe (confusion, bradycardia, cardiovascular compromise). Onset is usually insidious, so signs are vague and appear late; they are non-specific, so a thorough history and exam are essential.
  • Old classic exam findings (before the 1950s assays): delayed ankle reflexes, low basal metabolic rate, bradycardia.

4. The Thyroid Axis, Diagnosis and Treatment

PlayerRole
TRHHypothalamic releasing hormone: stimulates the pituitary
TSHFrom the anterior pituitary; modulated by thyroid hormone (negative feedback) and TRH
DIO2 (type 2 deiodinase)Converts T4 to T3
T3 (triiodothyronine)The ACTIVE hormone; the free fraction enters target cells and binds nuclear T3 receptors, which bind thyroid response elements and modulate gene expression
Thyroid axis, feedback and T4-to-T3 conversion (slide 5)
Diagnosis and treatmentSerum TSH is the most reliable marker of thyroid status, provided pituitary disease is excluded and the patient is not on medications that alter TSH secretion. Treatment is replacing T4: levothyroxine (LT4), since nearly all patients convert T4 to T3 peripherally.

Pattern: primary hypothyroidism = high TSH with low free T4; central hypothyroidism = low or inappropriately normal TSH with low free T4 beyond source.

6. Case 3: Cushing Syndrome (Hypercortisolism)

The patient24-year-old woman with irregular menses, weight gain, shortness of breath, thinning hair, increased belly fat despite more running, easy bruising, weird purple stretch marks, and a hump at the base of the neck; her face has changed (round, full) compared with a photo one year earlier.
One year earlier (slide 10)
Now: round (moon) face (slide 10)
Cushing findings (slide 11)
Non-specific findings (slide 11)More specific
Abdominal weight gain; thin arms and legs; severe fatigue and muscle weakness; hypertension; high blood sugar; purple or pink stretch marks; backache; easy bruising; excess facial and body hair in womenMoon-like (round, full) face

Definition and causes

  • Cushing syndrome = the cluster of signs and symptoms from long-term exposure to excess cortisol or other glucocorticoids.
  • Exogenous (iatrogenic) glucocorticoid use is the most common cause: ask carefully about prescriptions, topical steroids and inhaled glucocorticoids.
  • Endogenous is split into ACTH-dependent and ACTH-independent.

Work-up logic

StepDetail
1. Confirm hypercortisolismScreening tests (for example a 24-hour urine cortisol)
2. ACTH levelACTH is pulsatile with a diurnal rhythm, so measure on multiple occasions
ACTH persistently <5 pg/mLACTH-independent (adrenal source, and this includes exogenous steroids)
ACTH persistently >20 pg/mLACTH-dependent: next decide pituitary (most common, 70–85%) vs another source (ectopic)
3. ImagingCT of the adrenals when ACTH-independent
Her resultSeveral low ACTH levels and a 24-hour urine with elevated cortisol; no exogenous, inhaled or topical steroids; CT showed a 3.5 cm adrenal adenoma; after surgical excision she recovered fully. Note: the slide title says “Cushing’s disease,” but strictly Cushing’s disease is a pituitary ACTH-secreting tumor; her adrenal adenoma is Cushing syndrome beyond source.

7. Case 4: Addison Disease (Primary Adrenal Insufficiency)

The patient42-year-old woman: darkening skin, especially on the knees and elbows (her face is darker than in old photos), fatigue, weakness, irritability, muscle cramps, craving for salty and acidic foods. BP 82/58 (remarkably low for a healthy 42-year-old), HR 92, T 37°C.
Hyperpigmentation (slide 13)
Adrenal axis: ACTH, cortisol, aldosterone (slide 14)
ItemContent
DefinitionAddison disease is the most common form of adrenal insufficiency: destruction of the adrenal cortex with reduced adrenal hormone output
Primary (Addison)Usually autoimmune (about 90%)
SecondaryInadequate pituitary hormone secretion (low ACTH), so reduced adrenal stimulation (the feedback loop)
EpidemiologyRare: 3–4 per million per year; more common in women; typical onset age 30–50; often with other autoimmune diseases
LabsLow sodium, high potassium (low mineralocorticoid effect on the kidney); low glucose (low cortisol)
DiagnosisACTH stimulation test: give synthetic ACTH and measure cortisol at 0 and 30 minutes. Cortisol should rise sharply; Addison is ruled out if cortisol rises above 515 nmol/L (about 18–19 µg/dL)
TreatmentDaily oral glucocorticoids, highest dose in the morning; a stress dose for surgery or trauma; a medical alert bracelet
CourseThe skin changes did not resolve immediately, which is common
Why these features beyond sourceHyperpigmentation comes from high ACTH (with melanocyte-stimulating hormone) in primary disease, so it is absent in secondary adrenal insufficiency; hyperkalemia, hyponatremia and hypotension reflect aldosterone deficiency, which is also spared in secondary insufficiency.

Seven take-home points
  1. Non-pitting edema = think thyroid/lymphatic, not heart/kidney.
  2. Always check TSH first; T3 is the active hormone (made by DIO2).
  3. Hashimoto is the most common primary hypothyroidism (iodine-replete areas).
  4. Graves is the most common US hyperthyroidism (7–8× women); methimazole.
  5. Exogenous steroid is the most common cause of Cushing syndrome.
  6. ACTH <5 independent, >20 dependent.
  7. Addison: low Na, high K, hyperpigmentation; ACTH stim test.
