00. Overview & Learning Objectives

  • Describe the application of osteopathic principles in a specific patient care setting
  • Incorporate the ABCS concept (Autonomics, Biomechanics, Circulation, Screening) into the history, physical, and treatment of the patient
  • Explain the predicted physiologic effects from somatic dysfunction related to the ABCS concept
This session works through a single detailed hospital case, but the underlying principles are meant to generalize across multiple body systems and patient situations — not just the renal/urologic system highlighted here.
Practice Question

01. Foundational Osteopathic Tenets

Common misconception: Many people think osteopathic manipulation is osteopathic medicine. In reality, the principles and practices of osteopathic medicine go far beyond applying manipulation — they inform how every patient encounter is approached, with or without OMT.
  1. The body is a unit; the person is a unit of body, mind, and spirit. Recognizing that structures, fluids, and tensions throughout the body are interrelated helps a physician see that somatic dysfunction or disequilibrium in one area produces both local and generalized adaptation throughout the body.
  2. The body is capable of self-regulation, self-healing, and health maintenance. The physician's role is to search for expressions of health, intervene in ways that optimize that health, encourage compensatory/adaptive changes toward a physiologic neutral, and stimulate a less costly and less painful adaptation to disequilibrium or dysfunction.
  3. Structure and function are reciprocally interrelated. The physician can manually interact with body structures to facilitate optimal function and adaptation — true not only for the musculoskeletal system, but also the autonomic, circulatory, and lymphatic systems. In fact, the structure-function relationship can become impaired before disease even appears, so a physician who can recognize this altered tissue behavior can intervene early.
  4. Rational treatment is based upon an understanding of these basic principles — body unity, self-regulation, and the interrelationship of structure and function.
Key framing: Every sick patient encounter reflects a situation in which the body was ultimately unable to self-regulate on its own, prompting the patient to seek external help. The physician's goal — alongside applying pharmacology, anatomy, and other medical knowledge — is to help restore the body's ability to self-regulate and heal by altering structure and function.

03. Case Presentation: History

Consult A 65-year-old male has been hospitalized for intractable back pain and renal failure. Before even seeing the patient, two questions should come to mind: Where is the back pain? And what is his baseline versus current renal function?
HPI The patient was doing well following a right total hip arthroplasty (THA) in May, walking up to 3 miles a day post-op. It is now July. About a month ago (June), he developed non-radiating mid-to-low back pain — present with activity, upon waking, and intermittently throughout the day. Pain is typically 2/10, up to 8/10 at its worst, with no specific movements that improve or worsen it. He has been applying heat and taking approximately 800 mg/day of ibuprofen (no more than 400 mg in a single dose).

One week prior to admission, the pain significantly worsened, with associated nausea and liquid stool. No urinary problems; urine appears clear. No sick contacts, fevers, chills, or sweats. He was admitted primarily for pain, and initial workup revealed acute kidney injury and elevated liver enzymes. Abdominal ultrasound showed fatty infiltrate of the liver.

04. Case Labs: BMP & Liver Trends

Comparing May (time of THA surgery) to June (roughly one month later, when back pain began):

  • May: basic metabolic panel and CBC were normal
  • June: bicarbonate (CO2) dropped — reflecting increasing acidosis; BUN and creatinine both elevated (azotemia); AST/ALT elevated 3–10 fold above the normal range (normally in the tens)
Reading bicarbonate: Bicarbonate is often reflected on a basic metabolic panel as "CO2." Associate bicarb (starting with "B") with base — as bicarbonate drops, the patient is becoming more acidic.

BUN-to-Creatinine Ratio

The normal BUN:creatinine ratio is approximately 10:1 to 20:1.

BUN:Cr RatioSuggestsWhy
> 20:1Prerenal process (e.g., volume depletion/dehydration, CHF, GI bleeding)Increased BUN reabsorption disproportionately elevates BUN relative to creatinine
10:1 – 20:1Normal, or a postrenal processBUN reabsorption is within normal limits
< 10:1Intrarenal (intrinsic) processRenal damage reduces BUN reabsorption, lowering the ratio

In this case, the BUN:creatinine ratio was less than 10:1, pointing toward an intrarenal process rather than a prerenal one.

05. Case Labs: Urinalysis & Anion Gap

  • Anion gap = (Sodium + Potassium) − (Chloride + Bicarbonate). A normal value is around 10 (typically 4–12 depending on the lab). This patient's anion gap was 17 — significantly elevated, raising concern for ketoacidosis, chronic kidney disease, or toxin ingestion.
  • Initial urinalysis was fairly unremarkable: no WBCs or leukocyte esterase, no RBCs, mildly low (but not concerning) specific gravity, no glucose, minimal protein, squamous cells present (indicating a contaminated sample), and hyaline casts present.
  • On day 3 of hospitalization, urinary sodium, creatinine, and eosinophils were added to the workup — these become relevant later for calculating the fractional excretion of sodium (see Section 20).

06. Autonomics: Sympathetic vs. Parasympathetic Balance

  • Normal tone: sympathetic and parasympathetic activity are evenly distributed and balanced
  • Physiologic/pathologic variations: sympathetic overdrive with normal parasympathetic tone (or vice versa); both systems in overdrive; or both systems diminished, with one having a net positive manifestation over the other

Anatomic Access Points

  • Sympathetics: best accessed via the thoracic and lumbar spine, where the sympathetic chain resides
  • Parasympathetics: accessed via the vagus nerve (occiput, upper cervical spine) and the pelvic splanchnics (sacrum)
Clinical implication: Net sympathetic overdrive → expect thoracolumbar somatic dysfunction, and be less likely to find occipital/sacral dysfunction. If both systems are ramped up (or both diminished), somatic dysfunction may be prominent in both the thoracolumbar spine and the occiput/upper cervical/sacral regions.

07. Renal Autonomic Physiology

Increased Sympathetic ToneIncreased Parasympathetic Tone
Increased afferent arteriolar constriction to the kidney—
Ureterospasm and decreased peristalsis of the uretersIncreased peristalsis of the ureters
Relaxation of the bladder wall → vesicoureteral refluxIncreased bladder wall tone
Increased tone of the external urinary sphincterRelaxation of the internal urinary sphincter

Downstream Effects of Increased Sympathetic Tone

  • Decreased glomerular filtration rate (GFR) → decreased urinary output
  • Elevated blood pressure (decreased renal perfusion activates the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system)
  • Decreased urine flow through the ureters (from spasm)
  • Incomplete bladder emptying, with more reflux and retrograde-flow complications

As parasympathetic tone drops, effects reverse in the opposite direction.

09. Chapman's Reflex Points

Chapman's reflex points are predictable, palpable anterior and posterior fascial tissue-texture changes that reflect visceral (viscerosomatic) dysfunction. These charts are commonly tested and should be memorized and visualized for exams and boards.

Quick screening: A "Quick Chapman's Screen" allows a rapid (30–45 second) screen across multiple body systems, useful when time is limited.

11. Biomechanics: Renal Anatomy & Nephroptosis

  • The kidney's capsule and surrounding peritoneum compartmentalize it from adjacent structures, but there are close anatomic relationships between the kidney, its vasculature, the psoas major, the quadratus lumborum, and the obliques/transverse abdominis — tension in one area can impact adjacent structures
  • The kidneys reside in the retroperitoneal space and are relatively mobile (not intensely fixed) — during inhalation, the kidney normally moves caudally; during exhalation, it should gently slide back cephalad
  • If a kidney fails to move cephalad with exhalation, this is called a dropped kidney, or nephroptosis
Nephroptosis is an exam finding, not a formal clinical diagnosis — it is noted primarily by osteopathic physicians. It is more common in women, and while often asymptomatic, it can be associated with flank pain, nausea, chills, increased blood pressure, hematuria, and proteinuria.
Practice Questions

12. Circulation: Renal Vasculature & Autonomic Plexuses

  • The abdominal aorta gives off the superior mesenteric artery and inferior mesenteric artery, with the paired renal arteries branching off between these two levels
  • These arterial branch points correspond anatomically to the superior and inferior mesenteric ganglia — relevant autonomic structures that can be targeted for inhibitory osteopathic treatment
  • As with any medical problem, the circulation component of ABCS asks: what is the impact on arterial supply, venous drainage, and lymphatic drainage?

13. Clinical Reasoning: Summary Before Exam

Before proceeding to the physical exam, the history and labs are synthesized:

  • Renal failure — appears acute (normal labs in May)
  • Liver enzyme elevation — appears acute
  • Nausea and diarrhea — acute
  • Back pain — acute; unclear whether mechanical/primary, related to the recent THA, or visceral (GI/renal, possibly adrenal) in origin
Returning to the osteopathic tenets: What areas have not been able to compensate or correct themselves (tenet 2)? What structures are involved, what is their normal function, and how has that function become altered due to structural problems (tenet 3)?

14. Physical Exam Findings

  • General: Afebrile, vital signs stable, no distress
  • Lymph: No palpable cervical, supraclavicular, axillary, or inguinal lymphadenopathy
  • Eyes: Extraocular movements intact; sclera/conjunctiva nonicteric
  • Cardiac: S1/S2 without murmurs, rubs, or gallops; no jugular venous distension
  • Pulmonary: Breathing unlabored and symmetric; lungs clear to auscultation bilaterally
  • Abdomen: Bowel sounds positive in all four quadrants; no tenderness, rebound, or guarding; no hepatosplenomegaly
  • GU: No suprapubic tenderness; negative Lloyd's sign (costovertebral angle tenderness) bilaterally
  • MSK: No joint swelling; good range of motion in all limbs (additional osteopathic findings in Section 15)
  • Neuro: Cranial nerves II–XII intact; sensation intact globally; 5/5 muscle strength bilateral upper and lower extremities; symmetric 2+ patellar and Achilles reflexes; alert and oriented

15. Osteopathic Structural Exam Findings

  • No referred pain
  • T8–L2 tight bilaterally — difficult to discern a Type I versus Type II segmental somatic dysfunction
  • Left anterior renal Chapman's point present
  • Mild right rotation of the atlantoaxial (AA) joint
  • Decreased primary respiratory mechanism (PRM) — a cranial concept covered later in the curriculum
  • Sacrum not moving well
  • Diaphragm tight on the right
  • Ptosis of the right kidney (positive nephroptosis finding)
Practical tip: In a hospitalized patient, rolling them for a posterior exam can be difficult given pain, IV lines, or other equipment. When needed, slide your hands behind the trunk to palpate the spine, or use anterior tender points for reference — these can help both evaluate for dysfunction diagnostically and be used therapeutically.

16. Anterior Tender Points: Diagnosis & Treatment

Tender PointLocationClassic Treatment Position
Psoas2/3 of the distance from the ASIS to midline, pressing deep posteriorly toward the psoas bellyMarked bilateral hip flexion with external rotation; side-bend the lumbar spine toward the point
Iliacus1/3 of the distance from the ASIS to midline, pressing deep posteriorly toward the iliacus bellyMarked bilateral hip flexion with external rotation as the primary motion

Anterior lumbar (counterstrain) tender points are located along a line from the ASIS toward the pubic tubercle — L1 near the ASIS, L2–4 further along, and L5 near the pubic tubercle. Treatment for these points generally requires a greater degree of passive hip flexion (often bringing up both legs for maximal patient relaxation), with rotation toward the side of the tender point when treating L2 through L4.

Why this matters for hospitalized patients: When a patient cannot be easily rolled to access posterior tender points, anterior tender points can be used both diagnostically and as a treatment reference point — assessing the impact on lumbar tension afterward is a good way to confirm the intervention's effect.

17. Treatment Approach: Autonomics, Biomechanics & Lymphatics

SystemTarget(s)
SympatheticsSomatic dysfunction/facilitated cord segments and rib raising/inhibition at T10–L2 (using lumbar transverse processes similarly to ribs, since the lumbar spine has none); treat Chapman's points; inhibitory release to the inferior mesenteric ganglion, adrenal, and kidney
Somatic nervesC3–5 (phrenic nerve) — mixed motor/sensory, also carries sympathetic fibers; the sacral plexus (pudendal nerve) is also mixed and may need addressing
ParasympatheticsTreat the OA (occipitoatlantal) and AA (atlantoaxial) joints; consider cranial treatment; treat the sacroiliac joint, sacrum, and innominate

Case treatment: A mix of counterstrain (CS), myofascial release (MFR), and balanced ligamentous tension (BLT) was chosen. The specific modality matters less than fitting the technique to the patient and situation — in a hospital bed, more involved techniques can be difficult, so treating accessible anterior structures (like anterior tender points) and reassessing the effect on lumbar tension is a practical approach.

20. FENa & Causes of Acute Kidney Injury

The fractional excretion of sodium (FENa) helps distinguish prerenal from intrinsic/postrenal acute kidney injury. It is most reliable when urinary output is low (< 500 mL/day).

FENa = (Urine Na ÷ Plasma Na) ÷ (Plasma Cr ÷ Urine Cr) × 100

FENa ValueSuggests
< 1%Decreased renal perfusion (prerenal)
1–2%Nonspecific — could be prerenal hypoperfusion or intrinsic disease (e.g., ATN)
> 2%Acute tubular necrosis (intrinsic)

This patient's calculated FENa was 3.06%, suggesting intrinsic renal damage.

Causes of Acute Kidney Injury by Category

CategoryExamples
PrerenalIntrarenal vasoconstriction (certain drugs), systemic vasodilation (e.g., sepsis, neurogenic shock), volume depletion (diuretic overuse, vomiting/diarrhea, burns, blood loss)
IntrinsicGlomerular, interstitial, or tubular injury (from drugs, infections, systemic disease such as sarcoidosis or lupus, ischemia, or nephrotoxins); vascular causes (renal vein thrombosis, malignant hypertension, renal artery thrombosis/infarction)
PostrenalUreteral/bladder obstruction (prostatic hypertrophy, neurogenic bladder, retroperitoneal fibrosis, malignancy); pelvic causes (transitional cell carcinoma, pelvic malignancy compressing ureters, inflammatory aortic aneurysm); intrarenal obstruction (stones, crystals, clots, tumors, paraproteins)

21. Day 3 Follow-Up: Leg-Length Discrepancy

Subjective Possibly a bit better. Creatinine still elevated but trending down. Back pain persists without any clear modifying factors.
Exam Left kidney Chapman's point improved, though the back exam was otherwise unchanged. On reassessment (always reassess!), new findings emerged: the right medial malleolus and kneecap were both caudal compared to the left, along with a right anterior innominate rotation — raising the possibility of a leg-length discrepancy pattern contributing to the ongoing back pain.
In-room trial: A folded pamphlet/brochure was placed under the patient's left heel, and he was asked to stand. He immediately reported his back pain relaxed/improved — this simple maneuver was used both diagnostically and as an early therapeutic intervention.
Practice Questions

22. Discharge Plan & Clinical Takeaways

The patient was stabilized and discharged home with a left heel lift, continuing as a therapeutic intervention after having been used diagnostically in the hospital. Additional outpatient workup was still indicated:

  • Further evaluation for the elevated AST/ALT — ruling out hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and HIV
  • Continued kidney function evaluation, typically with a 24-hour urine collection if creatinine remains elevated
  • Balanced pelvic girdle and core training to help address the leg-length discrepancy

The patient went home in stable, improving condition; final long-term outcomes were not available to the presenter.

Key Clinical Reasoning Concepts

  • Somato-visceral: a structural/somatic issue causing visceral changes
  • Viscerosomatic: an internal (visceral) process causing external (somatic) manifestations
  • Viscero-visceral facilitation and somato-somatic responses can also occur
Keeping a broad, open mind helps a physician recognize these patterns when they occur. The ultimate goal in all of these situations is to help the patient achieve relief from their allostatic, or cumulative chronic stress load, and move toward a more efficient homeostatic normalcy and, ultimately, health.
