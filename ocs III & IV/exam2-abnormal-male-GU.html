1. Overview: What Dr. Park Wants You to Know

Dr. Park’s framingThis is a terminology and diagnosis series: define the abnormality, recognize how it presents, and know the exam finding that identifies it. He says plainly to focus on terminology and diagnosis, not treatment. The self-study deck (SDL 2) carries the note “Should know every slide in the SDL.”

Learning objectives (slides)

  • Define the terminology of male genitourinary and pelvic abnormalities.
  • Define terminology of symptoms and signs of male genitourinary and pelvic pathology.
  • Describe abnormal physical exam findings.
  • Identify and demonstrate the skills for a male sexual history and a genitourinary review of systems.
  • Practice the physical exam and the special exam techniques that rule out specific pathology.
The goal of the exam (SDL 3, slide 10)“Main goal of the physical exam is to rule in or rule out these pathologies”, and the list is the SDL 1 list: every penile, scrotal, anal and rectal, prostate and hernia diagnosis in sections 6–10. Think of each diagnosis as a finding you are trying to elicit.

2. Symptom Terminology (SDL 2)

Each symptom has a definition, sometimes a cutoff, and a short list of associations. Learn the cutoffs: they are the testable numbers.

Urinary symptoms

TermDefinitionCommonly associated with
DysuriaPainful or difficult urination; burning or stingingUrethritis, urinary tract infection (UTI)
FrequencyUrinating more often than usual; typically >8 times in 24 hoursUTI, bladder irritation, diabetes
UrgencySudden, strong urge to urinate; difficulty holding or delayingUTI, overactive bladder
HesitancyDifficulty starting the flow despite feeling the needBenign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), bladder obstruction, neurologic conditions
IncontinenceInvoluntary leakage of urine (classically with coughing or sneezing)See subtypes below
RetentionInability to completely empty the bladder. Acute = sudden and painful; chronic = gradual and often painlessObstruction (BPH), nerve problems, medications
PolyuriaUrinating much more than usual; typically >3 L/dayDiabetes mellitus, diabetes insipidus, excess fluid intake
OliguriaDecreased urine production; <400 mL/day in adultsDehydration, kidney disease, urinary obstruction
AnuriaExtremely limited or absent urine output; <100 mL/dayAcute kidney failure, severe dehydration, complete obstruction
NocturiaWaking at night to urinateUTI, overactive bladder, congestive heart failure, poorly controlled diabetes
StranguriaPainful, slow, difficult urination with a sensation of incomplete emptyingUTI, bladder stones, prostate pathology
HematuriaBlood in the urine (pink or red)Infection or cancer anywhere along the tract (kidney, bladder, urethra), kidney stones, trauma
Incontinence subtypes: names onlyDr. Park’s note: know all the names of the subcategories but not the characteristics of each for this SDL. The six: stress, urge, overflow, functional, mixed, post-prostatectomy.

Sexual and rectal symptoms

TermDefinitionCommonly associated with
HematospermiaBlood in the semen (pink or red)Prostatitis, sexually transmitted infections (STIs), trauma, prostate cancer
HematocheziaBloody bowel movement, usually bright redHemorrhoids, anal fissures, diverticulosis, colon cancer, inflammatory bowel disease
PriapismAbnormally prolonged erection, lasting >4 hours; usually very painfulSickle cell disease, spinal cord injury, medications (erectile dysfunction drugs, antidepressants)
Erectile dysfunction (ED)Inability to achieve or maintain an erection sufficient for sexual activityCardiovascular disease, diabetes, psychological factors (stress, anxiety, depression), neuropathy, medication side effects
ProctalgiaPain in the rectum or anal region. Sudden, severe, short-lived = proctalgia fugax; chronic = levator ani syndromeMuscle spasm, hemorrhoids, anal fissures, irritable bowel syndrome

Easy-to-confuse pairs

PairDifference
Frequency vs urgencyFrequency = how often (>8/day). Urgency = how suddenly (strong, hard to delay).
Hesitancy vs stranguriaHesitancy = trouble starting the stream. Stranguria = painful, slow flow with a feeling of incomplete emptying.
Oliguria vs anuria<400 mL/day vs <100 mL/day.
Polyuria vs frequencyPolyuria is a large volume (>3 L/day). Frequency is a large number of voids (>8/day).
Hematuria vs hematospermia vs hematocheziaBlood in urine, in semen, in stool (bright red).
Proctalgia fugax vs levator ani syndromeFugax = sudden, severe, short-lived. Levator ani = chronic.

4. History Taking: Sexual History and Review of Systems (SDL 3)

The platinum ruleSDL 3 reminds you: treat others the way THEY want to be treated. “A great physician makes all their patients feel safe and cared for.” The deck also assigns a 7-minute video on sexual orientation and gender identity.

Male sexual health history (slide questions)

  • How do you identify your gender? Do you have preferred pronouns?
  • How would you describe your sexual orientation?
  • Any significant challenges or hardships because of your orientation or gender identity that I can help with?
  • Are you currently in a relationship that involves sex?
  • What types of sexual activity (vaginal, oral, anal)?
  • Do you use condoms or other protection? How often?
  • Ever diagnosed with a sexually transmitted infection (STI)?
  • Ever experienced sexual abuse or trauma?

5. Physical Exam Techniques (SDL 3)

Preliminaries (slide)

  • Obtain consent and document it in the chart.
  • Have a chaperone in the exam room.
  • Prepare supplies: patient drapes, gloves, lubricant, paper towel, light source, fecal occult blood testing (FOBT) materials.
  • Explain what you are going to do before you start, and explain what you are doing during each step.
Exam supplies (SDL 3, slide 9)

Penis and scrotum (demonstration video)

StepWhat you do and look for
PenisInspect and palpate the shaft; hold the penis by the glans. In an uncircumcised patient, retract the foreskin to inspect the glans. Inspect the meatus (where the urethra opens): note discharge. Look for lesions, curvature, plaques.
ScrotumInspect the skin, then palpate. Two-handed testicular exam: the testis feels like an egg; the epididymis lies posterolaterally.
Spermatic cord and vas deferensPalpate the cord between thumb and fingers above the testis.
Standing examExamine standing to look for varicocele (enlarges standing and with Valsalva) and hernia.
TransilluminationShine a light behind the scrotal swelling: a hydrocele lights up (a solid mass does not).

Special techniques: what each rules in

TechniqueRules in
Standing exam with ValsalvaVaricocele; inguinal hernia
TransilluminationHydrocele
Cremasteric reflexTesticular torsion (reflex absent)
Retract foreskinPhimosis, balanitis, glans lesions
Valsalva (bearing down) during rectal examRectal prolapse
Transillumination of a hydrocele (SDL 1, slide 19)

Digital rectal exam (DRE): technique

  1. Explain, and position the patient: left lateral decubitus (lying on the left side).
  2. Inspect the perianal skin; ask the patient to bear down (prolapse).
  3. Lubricate and place the finger at the anus, aiming toward the umbilicus.
  4. Assess sphincter tone, rotate the finger around the rectal wall.
  5. Palpate the prostate anteriorly.
  6. Check stool on the glove (FOBT).
When NOT to do a DREPark gives two reasons: no finger or no rectum. Otherwise you do it.

Objective findings on DRE palpation (slide 16)

FindingNormal / abnormal
Rectal toneNormal, increased or decreased
Rectal wallMasses, strictures, tenderness
Prostate sizeNormal, or enlarged (mild, moderate, severe)
Prostate textureFirm, rubbery = normal; soft, boggy = acute inflammation; hard or indurated = suggests malignancy
SymmetrySymmetrical or asymmetrical lobes
NodularityAbsent, or present (smooth or irregular)
TendernessNon-tender is normal; tender suggests prostatitis
StoolConsistency and color: fecal impaction, blood
Prostate on DRE: the three patternsBoggy and tender = acute prostatitis. Smooth, symmetric, enlarged, non-tender = BPH. Hard, indurated, nodular or asymmetric = suspicious for cancer.

6. Penile Abnormalities (SDL 1)

Foreskin and urethral opening

TermDefinition
PhimosisForeskin cannot be pulled back. Physiologic: common in children, resolves on its own. Pathologic: chronic inflammation or scarring, often from balanitis or balanoposthitis.
ParaphimosisUrologic emergency: foreskin trapped behind the corona of the glans, in uncircumcised males. Strangulation of the glans, venous engorgement, edema, necrosis. Urgent intervention.
HypospadiasCongenital: urethral opening on the underside (ventral) of the penis. Corrected surgically in childhood.
EpispadiasRare congenital: opening on the upper (dorsal) side.
Phimosis, degrees of foreskin retraction (SDL 1, slide 3)
Ventral vs dorsalVentral = lower surface, faces the scrotum when erect. Dorsal = upper surface, faces the abdomen. Hypospadias is the common one and is ventral; epispadias is rare and is dorsal.

Inflammation

TermDefinition
BalanitisInflammation of the glans: bacterial, fungal or viral infection, poor hygiene, allergy, dermatitis. Redness, swelling, itching, pain, sometimes discharge.
BalanoposthitisInflammation of the glans and the foreskin (balanitis + posthitis).
UrethritisInflammation of the urethra, usually from sexually transmitted infections. Think penile discharge and dysuria.
Urethritis (SDL 1, slide 14)

Penile lesions: the high-yield comparison

High yield (Park)Chancre = painless (syphilis). Chancroid = painful (Haemophilus ducreyi). This is the most tested pair in the penile lesion slides.
LesionCauseKey features
ChancreTreponema pallidum (primary syphilis)Painless ulcer; round, firm, non-tender; first sign of syphilis; at the site of entry (genital, mouth)
ChancroidHaemophilus ducreyiPainful ulcer; starts as a red papule then an ulcer with irregular, soft edges; swollen groin lymph nodes that can abscess
Herpes simplexHerpes simplex virus, typically type 2 (HSV-2)Small red bumps, then painful vesicles that rupture, leaving painful ulcers
Condyloma acuminataHuman papillomavirus (HPV)Genital warts: soft, flesh-colored, single or multiple, cauliflower-like
Pearly penile papulesNormal variant; not infectionDome-shaped, flesh-colored or white, 1–3 mm, in one or more rows around the corona. No treatment needed

Structural problems

TermDefinition
Penile fractureRupture of the tunica albuginea of the corpora cavernosa from trauma to an erect penis, most often a slip during vigorous intercourse. Rare but serious.
Peyronie’s diseaseFibrous scar plaque in the tunica albuginea; causes abnormal curvature during erection.

7. Scrotum and Testes (SDL 1)

Congenital, infection and swelling

TermDefinition and key points
CryptorchidismOne or both testes fail to descend into the scrotum. The most common congenital abnormality in male infants; unilateral or bilateral.
CellulitisInfection of the skin of the scrotum.
AbscessLocalized collection of pus from bacterial infection: painful, red, swollen, warm; may rupture or need drainage.
Fournier gangreneLife-threatening necrotizing fasciitis of the perineum, genitals and surrounding tissue; polymicrobial (aerobic and anaerobic).
Scrotal lymphedemaFluid buildup from damaged or blocked lymphatic vessels; in adults, usually from cancer treatment or surgery.
Testicular descent and cryptorchidism (SDL 1, slide 15)

Masses in the scrotum: the key comparison

Park’s identifiersVaricocele = “bag of worms”, enlarges with Valsalva. Hydrocele = transilluminates, painless. Spermatocele = painless, smooth, movable mass above the testicle.

Spermatocele (epididymal cyst)Benign fluid-filled cyst of the epididymis, often containing spermPainless, smooth, movable mass above the testicle

8. Anus and Rectum (SDL 1)

TermDefinition and key points
Anal skin tagsBenign overgrowth of anal epithelium; very common and painless.
HemorrhoidsEngorged, dilated veins in the anal canal or perianal area, classed internal or external by the dentate (pectinate) line.
Anal fissureSmall tear or ulcer in the anoderm, typically posterior midline; severe pain during and after defecation, bright red blood.
Anal fistulaAbnormal connection between the anal canal lining and the perianal skin; often from an infected anal gland that formed an abscess draining to the skin.
Anal cancerMalignant tumor, most commonly squamous cell; generally painless until the surface ulcerates.
Rectal prolapseProtrusion of the rectal wall through the anus, partial or complete.

Hemorrhoids and the dentate line (SDL 1, slide 26)

Why a fissure hurts

The anoderm is thin, delicate and highly innervated, running from the dentate line to the anal verge, and it has no sebaceous glands or hair follicles. Tears here are intensely painful.

TermDefinition and key pointsDRE
Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH)Non-malignant enlargement; hyperplasia of stromal and epithelial cells; compresses the urethra, causing lower urinary tract symptoms. Older men.Enlarged, non-tender
ProstatitisInflammation, acute or chronic; bacterial or non-infectious. Dysuria, frequency, urgency, pelvic pain.Boggy and tender

BPH symptoms (case 5)

Hesitancy, weak stream, nocturia, frequency, urgency, incomplete emptying. The case patient has had them for 2 years with no dysuria, a normal penis and scrotum, and a significantly enlarged, non-tender prostate. If it progresses, the complication is urinary retention.

Acute prostatitis (case 1)

A 42-year-old with dysuria, frequency, pelvic discomfort, mild fever and perineal pain, and a new dating partner. Exam: normal penis and scrotum, boggy and tender prostate. Pathophysiology: bacterial invasion of the prostate via the urethra.
