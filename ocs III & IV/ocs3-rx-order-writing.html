00. Overview & Learning Objectives

  • Learn how to write appropriate prescriptions
  • Learn the appropriate abbreviations used
  • Learn how to read various pharmacy abbreviations regarding medications and instructions for use
  • Learn how to avoid common errors in prescription writing
Practice Question

01. Who Can Prescribe? & What Needs a Prescription?

  • Who can prescribe: physicians, physician assistants, nurse practitioners, dentists, and veterinarians, among others. It's important to understand who can — and cannot — prescribe.
  • What requires a prescription: a drug that requires a prescription is one considered potentially harmful if not used under the supervision of a licensed healthcare provider.
Practice Questions

02. Parts of a Prescription

  1. The prescriber's name, practice address, and phone number (appears at the top, and again near the signature)
  2. The name of the person for whom the drug is intended (the patient)
  3. The date the prescription is written
  4. The medication name, strength, quantity, and directions on how to take it
  5. The signature of the prescriber
  6. The number of refills
Most important element: The handwriting must be completely legible — this is absolutely crucial to avoiding prescription errors.

05. Prescription Errors: Scope of the Problem

It is estimated that 44,000 prescription errors happen, and 98,000 deaths occur from errors in drug orders. This is a substantial, largely preventable source of patient harm — which is exactly why careful, standardized prescription writing matters.

Practice Question

06. Avoiding Errors: Writing Conventions

  • Avoid unnecessary decimal points
  • Avoid trailing zeros in decimals
  • Write "mL" instead of "cc" or "cm," to avoid misreading a "c" as a zero
  • Write units as "IU" or "U," rather than ambiguous symbols
  • Avoid writing "teaspoons" or "tablespoons"
  • When "as needed" (PRN) is written, specify the indication, along with the maximum amount and frequency within a 24-hour period
  • When possible, specify the times or events at which the medication should be taken
  • Write numbers as words when possible — especially important for controlled substances
Practice Questions

07. Good Prescription Writing Habits

  • Write the prescription in front of the patient — this lets you tell them exactly how to take it as you write, and gives them a natural moment to ask questions
  • Avoid distractions while writing — don't try to talk with staff, answer a page, or take a call while writing; focus on the prescription
  • Do not make any changes or cross-outs — if you make a mistake, start a new prescription
  • Proofread before signing
  • Never loan your prescription pad to anyone else

09. Electronic Prescribing (e-Prescribing)

  • E-prescribing enables a provider to electronically send an accurate, error-free, and understandable prescription directly to the pharmacy from the point of care
  • It has been a huge element in improving the quality of patient care
  • E-prescribing gained momentum with its inclusion in the Medicare Modernization Act (MMA) of 2003
  • In July 2006, the Institute of Medicine published a report on the role of e-prescribing in reducing medication errors, which received widespread publicity and helped build awareness of e-prescribing's role in improving patient safety

10. Controlled Substances: The Controlled Substances Act

The Controlled Substances Act, passed in 1970, covers the following with respect to drugs:

  • Classification and regulation, according to content and purpose
  • Manufacturing
  • Distribution
  • Exportation and sale

This act is the basis for the five drug schedules (I–V) covered in the following sections.

Practice Question

11. Schedule I Drugs

  • Highest potential for use disorder and misuse
  • No accepted medical use — considered illicit, "street" drugs
  • Examples: heroin, LSD, mescaline, MDMA, methaqualone, methamphetamine
  • Marijuana — though legal in some states — remains classified as a Schedule I drug at the federal level, as of this writing
Practice Questions

12. Schedule II Drugs

  • Reduced potential for use disorder compared to Schedule I, but still at high risk for physical and psychological dependence, with high capacity for misuse
  • Commonly used to treat severe pain, anxiety, insomnia, and ADHD
  • Examples: fentanyl, hydromorphone, meperidine, methadone, morphine, oxycodone, dextroamphetamine, methylphenidate, methamphetamine, pentobarbital, secobarbital
  • Historically paper-only; now also allowed to be sent via EPCS (Electronic Prescribing of Controlled Substances)
  • No refills are allowed
  • Has the tightest regulations of any schedule
Practice Questions

13. Schedule III Drugs

  • Lower misuse potential than Schedule I and II
  • More often leads to psychological dependence than physical dependence
  • Commonly used for pain control (not severe pain), anesthesia, or appetite suppression
  • Examples: benzphetamine, ketamine, phendimetrazine, anabolic steroids
  • Opioid analgesics in this schedule include products containing not more than 90 mg of codeine per dosage unit, and buprenorphine
  • Can be prescribed verbally over the phone, on paper, or electronically
  • Refill limit: up to 5 refills within 6 months
Practice Questions

14. Schedule IV Drugs

  • Even lower misuse potential than Schedules I, II, or III; limited risk of physical or psychological dependence
  • Examples: alprazolam, carisoprodol, clonazepam, clorazepate, diazepam, lorazepam, midazolam, temazepam, tramadol, triazolam
  • Can be used for pain control if the provider deems it medically necessary and believes the patient would benefit, although not frequently used this way
  • Can be prescribed verbally, on paper, or electronically
  • Refills permitted up to 5 times within 6 months from the date of the initial prescription
Practice Questions

15. Schedule V Drugs

  • Least likely of the controlled substances to be misused; result in very limited physical or psychological dependence
  • Examples: cough medicines containing codeine, antidiarrheal medications containing atropine/diphenoxylate, pregabalin, ezogabine
  • Despite low abuse potential, still need to be managed appropriately and administered with care
  • Codeine-containing formulations must contain less than 200 mg of codeine per 100 mL
  • Partial prescription fills cannot occur more than 6 months after the issue date; a partial fill is treated the same as a refill
