00. Overview & Learning Objectives

Depression module

  • Strong evidence supports physical activity for the treatment and prevention of depression, including both aerobic and resistance training.
  • Useful for recommending treatment in patients with a depressive mood disorder, or in populations where depression comorbidity worsens outcomes when untreated.
  • Understanding the biological mechanisms of the antidepressant effect of exercise helps match patients to effective treatment.
  • Barriers to implementing exercise as treatment exist in real-world clinical settings — a target for skills built during OCS, ESP, and DPR events.

Anxiety module

  • Anxiety is a basic, often adaptive emotion — not always pathological.
  • Psychotherapy and psychopharmacotherapy remain first-line treatments for anxiety disorders.
  • Physical inactivity is itself a risk factor for developing anxiety disorders.
  • Physical activity/exercise training has an anxiolytic effect in both healthy people and patients with anxiety disorders.
  • The anxiolytic effect is mediated by both biological and psychological mechanisms.
Framing pearl: Across both modules, the instructor emphasizes physical activity broadly (not just structured "exercise") — walking, chores, stairs — because that framing makes patient-centered recommendations more achievable.

01. Depression: Definition & Epidemiology

Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) — definition

  • Persistently low/depressed mood and/or anhedonia (decreased interest in pleasurable activities)
  • Feelings of guilt or worthlessness
  • Lack of energy, poor concentration
  • Appetite changes
  • Psychomotor retardation or agitation
  • Sleep disturbances; suicidal thoughts
  • Symptoms present ≥ 2 weeks minimum (usually much longer in practice) and must significantly impair daily life

Epidemiology

FeatureFinding
Lifetime prevalence5–17%; nearly 2× higher in women than men
Proposed reasons for sex differenceHormonal differences, childbirth effects, differing psychosocial stressors, learned helplessness
RaceNo significant differences
Socioeconomic statusNo significant differences
Mean age of onset~40 years, trending younger with rising incidence in younger populations
Relationship statusMore common in divorced, separated, widowed, or those lacking close personal relationships
GeographyMore common in rural areas
ComorbiditiesAlcohol/substance use disorders; panic attacks, social anxiety, OCD
Suicide riskHigher with comorbid substance/alcohol abuse and early life stressors
GeneticsHigh twin concordance rate

Two behavioral/cognitive theories

  • Learned helplessness theory — depression linked to experiencing uncontrollable events
  • Cognitive theory — depression linked to cognitive distortions in susceptible individuals

Treatment access/effectiveness gap

Only 55% of people with a depressive disorder receive treatment; of those, only 32% experience symptom alleviation — roughly 17% of all people with depression achieve alleviation of symptoms. This gap is a key rationale for exercise as an accessible adjunct/alternative.

02. Depression Screening: PHQ-9 & BDI-II

PHQ-9 (Patient Health Questionnaire-9)

  • Self-administered, 9 questions, each scored 0–3 (not at all → nearly every day) over the past 2 weeks
  • Sensitivity/specificity ~88% for MDD at a cutoff of ≥10
  • Covers: anhedonia, depressed mood, sleep disturbance, fatigue/energy, appetite change, worthlessness/guilt, concentration, psychomotor change, suicidal ideation
  • Extra (10th, unscored) question asks how difficult symptoms have made work/home/relationships — not at all / somewhat / very / extremely difficult — gauges functional impact

PHQ-9 scoring (remember by 5s)

<5: none 5–9: mild 10–14: moderate 15–19: moderately severe ≥20: severe

Clinical pearl: Psychomotor slowing (moving/speaking so slowly others notice) is a striking finding — the instructor notes the "life sucked out of the room" quality when interviewing a patient with significant MDD.

Beck Depression Inventory (BDI-II)

  • Self-administered, 21 statements, each with 4 graded choices (0–3) covering the characteristic symptoms of MDD
  • Max score = 63
  • 100% sensitivity, 96% specificity in older adult depressed outpatients
  • Test-retest reliability: 0.73–0.92 (consistent over time, good reliability/validity)
  • More often used in research studies and preferentially in older patients; PHQ-9 is more common in primary care

BDI-II interpretation (from slide, not narrated in lecture)

1–10: normal ups & downs 11–16: mild mood disturbance 17–20: borderline clinical depression 21–30: moderate depression 31–40: severe depression >40: extreme depression

03. Depression: Mechanisms of the Antidepressant Effect of Exercise

Serotonin

Exercise increases neural discharge of serotonin (raphe nucleus, hippocampus), tryptophan levels, and serotonin metabolism/availability — a mechanism analogous to SSRIs.

Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF)

  • BDNF is lower in untreated MDD vs. healthy adults; inversely correlates with symptom severity; rises with treatment
  • Exercise increases peripheral BDNF
  • Effect is greater in males, and with longer-duration sessions
Practical takeaway: Consider recommending exercise more strongly to men (larger BDNF response) and longer session durations for women.

HPA axis / Cortisol

MDD patients have elevated cortisol; acute and chronic aerobic exercise reduce cortisol and blunt stress-related cortisol spikes — parallel to the HPA effect seen in anxiety.

Endocannabinoid system

  • A single bout of aerobic exercise activates the endocannabinoid system
  • Activation improves depression, general tension, and total mood disturbance; positively correlates with vigor
  • Chronic effects are less clear but may confer a protective effect against stress exposure (more robust response to future stress)

Psychosocial mechanisms

  • Low self-efficacy is associated with depressive symptoms
  • Chronic exercise/physical activity improves self-efficacy → decreased depressive symptoms
  • Competing theory: exercise as a distraction from stress (evidence is conflicting)

04. Depression: Evidence for Exercise as Treatment

Aerobic exercise

  • Multiple studies show positive response on depressive symptoms with aerobic exercise vs. placebo (stretching)
  • 3×/week was just as effective as 5×/week
  • Both vigorous and light aerobic exercise were better than treatment-as-usual
  • 12-week, 4-arm study (treatment-as-usual vs. yoga/light vs. moderate vs. vigorous): all three exercise arms had significantly lower post-treatment depression scores than treatment-as-usual

Resistance training

  • 10-week supervised resistance training: 59% improved vs. 26% of controls
  • Improvement maintained through 10 further weeks of unsupervised, twice-weekly resistance training
  • At 26-month follow-up: 73% of the resistance-training group were classified as non-depressed vs. 36% of controls
Resistance training is a validated option for patients who dislike or cannot perform aerobic exercise.

Comparative effect size & augmentation

  • No significant difference in effect size between exercise and SSRIs or psychotherapy for depressive symptoms
  • Exercise as an adjunct to SSRIs or psychotherapy can augment effects (more robust studies still needed)
  • Aerobic exercise augments medication effects in patients with non-remitted MDD (harder-to-treat course)
  • Exercise also improves both physical and psychosocial quality-of-life indicators in depressed patients

Dose & adherence

  • No difference by frequency — 3×/week is sufficient, 5×/week not required
  • Suggested duration: 45–59 minutes per session is most effective, though a routine the patient can sustain matters most
  • There may be a minimum weekly dose needed for symptom reduction (less clear for quality-of-life benefit)
  • No robust data on optimal intensity — any exercise is better than none
  • No statistical difference in adherence between exercise and SSRIs; dropout rates comparable across exercise, SSRIs, and psychotherapy
  • Better adherence with home-based vs. lab-based programs
  • Altering session frequency does not affect adherence; some patients do better with a daily routine (personality-dependent)
  • Patients recruited into supervised sessions, or recruited from inpatient settings, are more likely to adhere (may bias study data upward)

06. Depression: Predicting Response & Practical Recommendations

Predictors of a better response to exercise

  • Higher baseline BDNF
  • Higher baseline TNF-alpha and IL-6 (elevated inflammatory markers)
  • Better baseline physical health
  • Lower anxiety
  • Hypersomnia and atypical depressive symptoms
Future direction: using baseline inflammatory markers to help decide whether to recommend exercise as a first-line treatment (heterogeneity of treatment response is the central challenge).

Bottom-line recommendations

  • Exercise is efficacious as monotherapy or augmentation to antidepressant medication or CBT
  • Both aerobic and resistance training are effective
  • WHO recommendation: 150 min/week moderate-intensity or 75 min/week vigorous-intensity aerobic activity, plus strength training ≥2 days/week

07. Anxiety: Definitions & Classification

Anxiety vs. panic

  • Anxiety is a normal, adaptive emotion — secures survival via fight-or-flight, signals danger
  • Physiologic changes: sweating, faster heart rate/breathing; cognitive/behavioral correlates: disordered thoughts, avoidance/leaving the situation
  • Panic = faster onset, more intense defensive reaction allowing rapid escape from perceived danger

When anxiety becomes pathological

  • Emerges in situations that are not objectively dangerous
  • Is excessively strong
  • Is anticipatory and constrains the person's life

Per WHO ICD classification, anxiety disorders fall under neurotic, stress-related, and somatoform disorders.

Key anxiety disorder definitions

DisorderDefinition
Generalized anxiety disorder (GAD)Excessive, persistent, unrealistic worry about everyday things, lasting >6 months
AgoraphobiaAnxiety in ≥2 of: public places, crowds, traveling far from home, traveling alone
Social anxiety disorderAnxiety around fear of scrutiny by others
Specific phobiasAnxiety restricted to highly specific situations/objects (heights, thunder, dental treatment, spiders, snakes); the stimulus is avoided whenever possible
As with MDD, a disorder diagnosis requires the anxiety to affect the patient's life and cause distress — not just be present.

08. Anxiety Screening: GAD-7

  • Self-administered screening tool for generalized anxiety disorder
  • 7 questions, each scored 0–3 (not at all → nearly every day); max score 21
  • Covers: nervousness/being on edge, inability to stop/control worry, worrying too much, trouble relaxing, restlessness, irritability, fear something awful might happen

GAD-7 scoring (remember by 5s)

<5: minimal 5–9: mild 10–14: moderate ≥15: severe

09. Anxiety: Epidemiology, Impact & Risk Factors

FeatureFinding
Lifetime prevalence (US)31% lifetime, 22% 12-month (age 13+) — among the most common mental disorders; notably lower in the EU (14%)
Subtype-specific prevalenceSpecific phobias 12–15%; OCD 1–2.3%
Age of onsetOften childhood/adolescence, chronic recurrent pattern
SexWomen much more likely to develop anxiety disorders, except OCD (higher in men)
Risk factorsGenetic predisposition, early life stress/trauma, chronic health conditions, substance abuse, physical inactivity

Economic & individual impact

  • Major health-economic burden (likely underestimated): psychiatric/non-psychiatric care, emergency care, hospitalization, medications, reduced productivity, absenteeism, suicide
  • Predicts unfavorable sociodemographic, clinical, and functional outcomes — especially interpersonal outcomes

10. Anxiety: First-Line Treatments (Psychotherapy & Pharmacotherapy)

Psychotherapy

  • CBT is well-supported: cognitive therapy and exposure therapy, alone/combined/with relaxation training, effective across GAD, PTSD, social anxiety disorder, and phobias — no differential efficacy by diagnosis
  • Neuroimaging: CBT alters neural activity in networks regulating negative emotion, fear conditioning/extinction; higher pretreatment left orbitofrontal metabolic activity predicts better OCD outcomes
  • Internet-assisted CBT is as efficacious as in-person CBT for anxiety

Pharmacotherapy

ClassExamples / Notes
SSRIsSertraline, paroxetine, citalopram, escitalopram — once daily by mouth; good evidence base
SNRIsVenlafaxine (and desvenlafaxine)
BenzodiazepinesExtraordinarily effective, rapid onset, can be dosed PRN; not recommended long-term due to abuse potential, tolerance, and CNS depressant effects
Non-benzodiazepine optionsBuspirone-type agent up to TID PRN; hydroxyzine up to TID (often dosed at bedtime to aid sleep)
Endocannabinoids (CBD)Preclinical anxiolytic effect via CB1 and 5-HT1A receptors, reduced fear/stress overactivation in animal models; clinical data limited but CBD appears to reduce symptoms in social anxiety, GAD, and PTSD, generally well tolerated — dosing standards remain the main challenge

Complementary/alternative interventions

  • Hypnosis, biofeedback, acupuncture, osteopathy, homeopathy — popular but generally poor evidence base
  • Some controlled evidence for lavender oil in GAD; inconsistent results for kava
  • Conflicting results for meditation; yoga has been shown to be effective
  • Relaxation training (applied relaxation, autogenic training, Jacobson's progressive relaxation) is anxiolytic; mindfulness is an emerging area

11. Anxiety: Psychological Mechanisms of Exercise

  • Improved coping skills & self-efficacy — exercise offers a felt "way out" of the trapped/helpless experience common in anxiety disorders; regaining a sense of control reduces stress and improves symptoms
  • Deflection of negative thought content — a competing theory; group exercise adds social interaction/reinforcement that can further relieve anxiety
  • Interoceptive exposure — endurance training in panic disorder functions like a type of exposure therapy: repeated exposure to increased heart rate/pulse/breathing lets the patient learn these sensations are natural, not life-threatening, altering the cognitive/affective reaction over time
Summary: exercise reduces anxiety sensitivity (desensitization), and group/social exercise settings may further help deflect negative thought content.

12. Anxiety: Biological Mechanisms of Exercise

  • Serotonin — endurance training normalizes serotonin receptors, paralleling the change seen with SSRIs
  • Endorphins — exercise-induced changes in these endogenous opioid-receptor agonists are linked to mood; evidence is mixed but likely contributes meaningfully
  • HPA axis / cortisol — anxiety disorders may involve HPA overactivity and chronically elevated cortisol; exercise has positive effects on HPA stress reactivity, reducing/preventing cortisol elevation
  • Atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP) — appears to modulate fear/panic and may drive psychological adaptation during exercise
  • BDNF — reduced BDNF is linked to both general anxiety and anxiety disorders; exercise normalizes BDNF, supporting nerve regeneration (especially hippocampal) — effect shown especially for aerobic > resistance training
Individual response depends on genes, age, and hormonal status — important to factor into patient-specific recommendations.

13. Anxiety: Evidence & Practical Recommendations

  • Regular physical activity has an anxiolytic effect in healthy people as well as those with established anxiety disorders
  • A single session of physical activity reduces panic reaction
  • Evidence supports endurance training for panic disorder, PTSD, and OCD; less support for GAD and social anxiety disorder specifically
  • Low baseline physical activity is itself a risk factor for developing anxiety
  • Group exercise environments promote adherence better than individual environments; identifying with an exercising group increases regular exercise
  • In diagnosed anxiety disorders or high chronic stress, aerobic exercise has the stronger recommendation (both aerobic and resistance training help improve anxiety disorder status generally)
  • Physical inactivity itself contributes to coronary heart disease, diabetes, certain cancers, obesity, hypertension, and all-cause mortality — increasing PA helps across all of these

Recommendations

  • WHO: 150 min/week moderate-intensity or 75 min/week vigorous-intensity aerobic exercise, plus strength training ≥2 days/week
  • Consider each patient's social/cultural context to support adherence
  • Safety: inactive at-risk patients should have a medical checkup before starting a moderate-to-vigorous program
  • Clinical approach: assess whether inactivity was a risk factor in the anxiety's development, and consider PA as treatment or adjunct while supporting the patient in becoming/staying active
