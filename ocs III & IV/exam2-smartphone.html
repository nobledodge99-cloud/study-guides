1. Overview and Objectives

How this guide was builtThe SDL packet is the learning-objectives sheet (title and reading list only), chapter 103 (The Digital World and Addiction Treatment), and chapter 101 (Digital Health Technology for Behavior Change), both from Rippe, Lifestyle Medicine, 4th ed. The listed supplement, Tofighi, Abrantes & Stein (2018), was not attached; I pulled it from the open-access PubMed Central copy and tag it beyond source. There is no lecturer transcript, so red marks what the chapters themselves emphasize (Key Points boxes, Clinical Applications, repeated points), not what a professor said.

The big picture

  • The SDL title is smartphone-based technologies in addiction treatment. Chapter 103 is the core; chapter 101 gives the general digital-health toolkit and the evidence for each tool.
  • SUD care has a huge access gap; phones, telemedicine and apps are proposed as ways to close it, but evidence is still emerging and some apps are harmful.
  • Expect questions on: which app type does what, the prescription digital therapeutic (PDT) example, telemedicine and the Ryan Haight Act, harms and privacy, how to judge an app, and how this connects to the pillars of lifestyle medicine.

Two different “gap” numbers6.5% = share of those needing SUD treatment who got specialty facility treatment. 35% = share of people with a history of opioid use disorder with access to maintenance medication. Do not interchange them.

Loneliness and stigma

  • The US Surgeon General (Dr. Vivek Murthy) declared loneliness a public health concern in May 2023. Loneliness worsens substance use, hinders access to recovery environments and contributes to gaming addiction.
  • The Surgeon General urges people to connect beyond the digital screen and use technology within apps more effectively.
  • Stigma drives isolation and inadequate access to care. Telemedicine and apps can help people overcome self-imposed or other-imposed stigma and may encourage more people to seek help.
  • The digital world cuts both ways: pandemic isolation led to over-reliance on digital apps and behavioral addictions and eye complications.

Where digital tools fit in primary care

Treating SUD in primary care, including starting or refilling buprenorphine (Suboxone), is described as an integral part of the solution; apps and telemedicine support it.

What health apps achieve (chapter 101 outcomes)

DomainEvidence as stated
Weight and nutritionDigitized self-monitoring programs: moderately effective, higher adherence. 41-study review: automated nutrition apps had low to medium impact. Apps are adjuncts or low-intensity tools, not standalone intensive weight management
Physical activity2017 review of 21 randomized controlled trials (RCTs): small, significant effect on sedentary behavior; small to medium, not significant effect on total activity and walking
Mindfulness and mental healthPossible positive effect on depression and anxiety in non-clinical groups; more rigorous RCTs needed
Smoking cessation2019 Cochrane review: automated text-message programs raise quit rates (moderate certainty); apps themselves: very low certainty

Why many commercial apps disappoint

  • Success depends on building on clinical knowledge and evidence-based behavior-change techniques: goals and planning, feedback and monitoring, shaping knowledge, social support.
  • Commercial weight-loss apps included on average only 18.83% of the strategies in the landmark Diabetes Prevention Program.
  • Of 137 diabetes apps, under 38% included diet, glucose-control or exercise education.
  • Services also need usability, financial incentives, training, staffing and workflow integration; one technology alone is not enough.
  • Research goes stale fast because mobile technology changes quickly.

4. Prescription Digital Therapeutics (PDTs) and Digital Therapeutic Outcomes

Exam anchorThe PDT example for SUD in both chapters is reSET / reSET-O: an app that delivers cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) plus contingency management (rewards), prescribed along with medication in outpatient treatment.

reSET / reSET-O (chapter 103)

FeatureDetail
TypePDT: requires a prescription from a physician or treatment provider
ContentCBT + contingency management-based rewards
UsePrescribed with medication services in an outpatient setting
Pilot studyHigh attrition (53%), but users said it was a safe space to disclose recurring substance use
Practical catchContingency management is hard to staff in clinics; the app can be used without monetary rewards from the clinic
Regulatory/outcome claimsChapter 103: FDA approved for clinical use in 2019, claimed better retention than care as usual. Chapter 101: first FDA-authorized PDT was for opioid use disorder; improved outcomes, high retention, fewer hospital encounters at 24 weeks
Date discrepancy (beyond source)The chapters say FDA approval in 2019. FDA public records show reSET (non-opioid SUD) was authorized in September 2017 and reSET-O (opioid use disorder, used with buprenorphine) in December 2018. For the exam, use the chapter's wording, but know the dates are looser than the chapter implies.

5. Telemedicine and Access to Medication Treatment

Key points (chapter 103.3.3 to 103.3.4)

  • During the COVID-19 emergency, provisions of the Ryan Haight Act were relaxed: clinicians could prescribe medications for opioid use disorder by telemedicine alone, without an in-person physical exam. This became the default for outpatient addiction care.
  • Veterans Administration study: patients with video visits had better retention than office-only or phone-only. Those non-service-connected, older, male, Black, or experiencing homelessness were less likely to have telemedicine access (a disparity).
  • JAMA Psychiatry study: receiving MOUD by telehealth increased odds of treatment retention and decreased odds of a medically treated overdose.
  • Alcohol use disorder (AUD): a condition with impaired control over alcohol use despite harm, affecting at least 1 in 20 US adults (chapter figure), yet few are treated. Virtual programs combine medication, group counseling, behavioral therapy, peer support, chatbots and digital adherence tools.
  • Review of telehealth in addiction treatment during COVID-19: as effective as in-person for retention, therapeutic alliance and substance use. Optimal in-person/telehealth mix still unknown.
  • A WHO-funded four-country study: web-based alcohol e-help reduced problematic and dependent drinking; web programs convert easily to mobile in developing countries.
Clinical Applications boxAccessing care: telemedicine startups offer medication for SUD (commercial insurance or cash-pay); most health systems now have robust telemedicine. Psychotherapy: telehealth-only companies, evening and weekend options, myStrength prescribable for a set time, Woebot chat bot.

7. Text Messaging, Social Media, Wearables and Mutual Help

Text messaging (chapter 101.6)

  • Short Message Service (SMS) is the most widely used data application worldwide; people open 90% of texts within 3 minutes.
  • Benefits: personal, low-cost, instant, easy, scalable; push information; timed reminders at decision points; less invasive than calls; can mirror counseling (tailored advice, monitoring, goal setting, feedback). Good for low-income, low-resource, hard-to-reach populations.
  • Evidence: positive outcomes in diabetes self-management, cardiovascular disease, weight loss, physical activity, smoking cessation, medication adherence.
  • Design questions: message content, frequency, one-way vs two-way, tailoring, privacy (patients must understand SMS privacy risk), user testing, reading level no higher than 8th grade for adults.

Social media (101.4)

  • Cochrane review: may increase physical activity and well-being; other outcomes uncertain.
  • Embeddedness in an online weight-loss network was the variable most tied to weight loss. Users valued convenience, anonymity, non-judgmental support.
  • Loneliness affects 25% to 60% of older adults; online options can fill an in-person void.
  • Clinicians may engage patients there, but follow ethics: confidentiality, no defamation, privacy cannot be guaranteed, maintain public trust.

Wearables (101.5)

  • Fitbit started in 2010; trackers are accurate to about 25% of true step counts, heart rate within 10%, energy expenditure error ~30%; they tend to underestimate energy expenditure and overestimate sleep.
  • More than half of buyers stop using them (one-third before 6 months), but 80% still used trackers at 6 months in an insurer wellness program: wearables work best inside broader programs (peers, gamification, clinician contact).
  • Continuous glucose monitors reduce severe hypoglycemia in type 1 diabetes; biofeedback may reduce anxiety.

Mutual and self-help (chapter 103.4)

  • Mainstay for nearly a century; Alcoholics Anonymous began in 1935 (Bill Wilson and Dr. Bob Smith). Others: Narcotics Anonymous, Al-Anon, Gamblers Anonymous, Debtors Anonymous, Refuge Recovery, LifeRing, Women for Sobriety, SMART Recovery.
  • COVID-19 moved meetings online: better access across time zones and for shift workers, a barrier for those without video access. AA now offers online meetings by default, some 24/7 “hop on/hop off”.
  • Attendance was more likely in people who followed more than one type of mutual support group.
  • Apps and online groups are sometimes the only peer support for pornography, gaming, excessive Internet use, or disordered eating.
  • SMART Recovery (CBT-based) launched the SMART Track app with some promise.

8. Do Apps Work? Evaluation, Harms and Privacy (103.5 to 103.6)

High-yield warningNot every recovery app helps. A Tofighi systematic search of commercial app stores found features that exacerbate use: a forum where people offered to sell drugs, binge-drinking games, and advice to decrease the smell of alcohol; some apps promoted more smoking, cannabis or alcohol use. App stores delete harmful apps when detected.

Evaluating apps

  • Availability does not equal use or effectiveness; evaluate adherence, usability and clinical outcomes.
  • Early evaluation used a Likert scale (strongly agree to strongly disagree). The Mobile App Rating Scale (MARS) rates engagement, functionality, aesthetics and information; in a 2019 study most apps had a low MARS score.
  • Developers should assess risk of lost reputation, lost privacy, poor data quality, poor lifestyle or clinical decisions, inappropriate clinical action.

Harmful information and privacy

  • Forums and social media can worsen use: a 2017 Twitter study found about 49% of opioid tweets were for procuring illicit drugs.
  • Recovery apps may lack privacy protection and may not be HIPAA compliant (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act).
  • Marketing: cannabis companies used social media to advertise pop-up sales and delivery during lockdowns; e-cigarette companies' cessation claims reduce perceived risk, especially in youth. The Health Belief Model and Theory of Planned Behavior explain how risk perception shapes experimentation.

Practical vetting checklist

AskWhy
Is it a PDT or a general app?PDTs have FDA authorization; general apps may have none
Evidence behind it?Many apps have no RCT; most had low MARS scores
Does it follow behavior-change principles?Goals, feedback, social support, shaping knowledge
Privacy and HIPAA?Recovery data is sensitive
Any harmful features?Drug-selling forums, binge games
Is a human connected?Human-supported programs tend to do better (see supplement)

9. Supplement: Tofighi, Abrantes & Stein (2018), Technology-Based Interventions for SUD in Primary Care

Sourcingbeyond source Narrative review from Medical Clinics of North America 102:715–731, summarized from the open-access author manuscript. Details here may be more than the lecturer emphasizes; the red items are the ones that match chapter themes.

Technology types and evidence

TypeFindings
Computer and web-basedMeta-analysis for alcohol: small but significant effect (g = 0.20); multi-session modular programs (g = 0.61) beat single-session feedback (g = 0.27). CBT4CBT: more negative urine screens, longer abstinence. TES (Therapeutic Education System): abstinence 48% vs 37% in methadone patients; multisite OR 1.62
Text messagingCost-effective, widely used. Naltrexone adherence first month 19 vs 3 days; post-detox alcohol texting plus as-needed counselor calls improved low-risk drinking and retention
Interactive voice response (IVR)Automated scheduled calls; good for low literacy or texting discomfort; evidence for non-nicotine SUD unclear
Smartphone appsA-CHESS: after residential care, fewer risky drinking days and higher abstinence at 8 and 12 months; features include self-assessment, peer groups, counselor support, GPS alerts near high-risk places
Forums and social media12-step sites most popular; quality and clinical impact not well established
Emerging sensorsSCRAM and WrisTAS transdermal alcohol monitors; Wisepill and ingestible digital pills for adherence; Soberlink breathalyzer with facial recognition; electrodermal activity and heart rate rise with craving; machine learning for just-in-time intervention

Primary care implementation points

  • Most trials were in specialty or research settings, not primary care; implementation studies are needed.
  • Human-linked programs (clinician or peer) preferred to fully automated ones; more effective interventions are longer and more intensive, so adherence is the challenge.
  • Access barriers mirror socioeconomic barriers (phones, computers, internet); free phones or in-clinic computers help.
  • Privacy risks: stored content, interception, limited clinician HIPAA familiarity; remove identifiers and stigmatizing terms, use encryption and passwords, delete content regularly.
  • Many commercial apps are unvalidated and some sell user data; the FDA has trouble regulating general-wellness claims.
  • Barriers to adoption: clinician readiness, reimbursement, hardware, interoperability, privacy, regulation, long-term engagement.

10. Emerging Technologies and the Lifestyle Medicine Link

Emerging technologies (chapter 101.7)

TechnologyKey points
Multi-omicsGenome, epigenome, transcriptome, proteome, metabolome, microbiome. ~150 genetic variants tied to obesity. A Cochrane review found little to no effect of communicating DNA-based risk on smoking or activity
Virtual and augmented reality (VR, AR)VR = artificial simulated environment via headset; AR adds enhancements to reality. Pokémon Go raised step counts (10,000-step goal 15.3% to 27.5%) but returned to baseline by week 6. VR tested in COPD, gait training, stroke hand therapy, chronic low back pain
New sensorsSingle-lead ECG (Apple Watch, Zio Patch for atrial fibrillation), continuous glucose monitors, hybrid closed-loop insulin pumps; data sharing to health systems is slow
AI and machine learningAI mimics human cognition; machine learning trains on past data to predict from new data. Fastest advances in precision oncology and radiology; slow in general care

Why this matters to lifestyle medicine (103.7 to 103.8)

  • Sleep apps: CBT for insomnia (CBT-I) may beat education alone; CBT-I Coach (US Veterans Administration, free) improved sleep in combat veterans; Nenne Navi (Japan) cut sleep latency in caregivers. Calm and Headspace add meditation.
  • Physical activity for youth: about 80% of youth miss 1 hour of moderate-to-vigorous activity daily; clear design, community and gamification raise engagement (ages 4 to 18).
  • Sleep, mindfulness and mood apps speak to other lifestyle medicine pillars; less screen time frees time for social connection.
  • Stay vigilant for new electronic and chemical addictions and for the tools to combat them.
Clinical Applications box (rest)Self-help groups: apps for AA, NA, SMART Recovery with sober-day counters and online meetings. Education: Kick It California, free telephone coaching. PDTs: reSET and reSET-O deliver contingency management; may be insurance-covered. Lifestyle: coaching for distress tolerance and coping; CBT-I, Calm, Headspace.
