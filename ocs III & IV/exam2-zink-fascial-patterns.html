1. Overview: Who Was Zink and Why Does This Matter?

Dr. Langevin’s framingThis is something he learned early in his osteopathic training from his mentor; it was not widely taught, but it is “a very useful tool to get a really good quick picture of patients.” Fascia is his “bread and butter.” The lecture is short and the core is one idea: the common compensatory pattern (CCP) and what it predicts.

Learning objectives (slide)

  • Define and discuss Zink’s concept of the Common Compensatory Pattern (CCP).
  • Rapidly evaluate a patient’s pattern.
  • Apply it in a clinical setting.

Dr. J. Gordon Zink

  • An Ohio osteopathic physician who taught at the Ohio school and kept a full-spectrum private practice (like family practice: delivered babies, prescribed medications, saw inpatients).
  • Worked on the respiratory–circulatory (cardiorespiratory) and lymphatic model of osteopathic medicine; his goal was to improve lymphatic flow, which developed into a fascial pattern.
  • His initial article was in 1979: he looked at 1,000 hospitalized patients and recorded the pattern. He kept studying it in his own outpatient clinic.
A.T. Still (slide 3)“The fascia is the place to look for the cause of disease and the place to consult and begin the action of remedies in all diseases.”
Zink’s four transverse diaphragms and the compensated vs uncompensated patterns (slide 14)

2. The Common Compensatory Pattern (slides 4–5)

The CCP is an alternating pattern of fascial preferences, seen in the body’s fascia as it responds to stress when optimal alignment isn’t possible.

IdeaDetail
OriginDeveloped from Zink’s lymphatic research; patterns noticed in both healthy and diseased people.
What influences itStructural (trauma), functional, and emotional stresses can all alter it.
Fascia’s jobAbsorb and distribute forces: standing from sitting, walking, running, stairs.
When it goes wrongFascial strain disrupts normal balance and homeostasis: inflammatory responses, altered sympathetic and parasympathetic activity (local or global), altered blood flow and neurologic input, changes in stride and gait.
Why a pattern formsNot known. Ideas: handedness, leg dominance, how society arranges sitting and movement, fetal position and the twisting of delivery, postural models (Pope 2003). Hard to tease out in research.

Why compensation matters clinically

Compensated (alternating)Uncompensated (non-alternating)
ToleranceTolerate trauma, illness and somatic dysfunction betterReduced resilience; do not tolerate insults as well
HealingRepair and recover fasterSlower recovery
Response to osteopathic manipulative treatment (OMT)Predictable; takes a lot less workRequires more work, longer, more frequent visits
Still get hurt or sick?Yes, but they bounce backYes, and they do not bounce back as easily
Dr. Langevin’s examplesA well-compensated patient who slips on ice or goes over the handlebars, or gets hip or low back pain from new shoes that changed gait, responds quickly. Smokers are insulting the system: fascia is less healthy and blood flow is poor (why hand surgeons will not operate until they stop smoking), so they are less likely to be compensated and need more work. Uncompensated patients are his “frequent fliers.”

3. Transition Zones and Their Diaphragms (slides 6, 13)

Zink looked at four transition zones: junctional areas where a horizontal (transverse) diaphragm must let lymphatic, venous and arterial flow pass. When one is altered, it impedes flow. Each junction is a change in form and function, so it is easily stressed.

Memorize the order (top to bottom)OA = tentorium · CT = thoracic inlet · TL = respiratory diaphragm · LS = pelvic diaphragm. Slide 13 repeats this table as the key.

4. The Four Diaphragms in Detail (slides 7–12)

OA = tentorium cerebelli

  • A very thick fascia that is part of the dura; a separate compartment holding the cerebellum and lower brainstem like a bowl.
  • Tightly connected to the straight sinus and superior sagittal sinus, with attachments down to C2, so a torsion or rotation shows up at OA.
  • A vital diaphragm: venous drainage plus the delicate lymphatic drainage of the brain, which must clear fluid to avoid pressure build-up (arterial blood in, extracellular fluid out).
Tentorium cerebelli and venous drainage (slide 7)

CT = thoracic inlet

  • A fairly solid ring: T1, the first rib and the sternal brim (manubrium); a tight channel many structures pass through. The clavicle is used as a fulcrum when treating.
  • The key tissue: Sibson’s fascia (suprapleural membrane), the “diaphragm” of the inlet: 2–3 times thicker than the pleura, sits like a cap, attaches to the anterior transverse process of C7 and the inside of the first rib, and is crossed superficially by the scalene muscles.
  • Treatable: helps shoulder pain and lymph flow. Techniques: ligamentous articular strain (LAS), myofascial release, or simply treating C7 and T1.
Thoracic inlet with Sibson’s fascia (suprapleural membrane) (slide 8)

TL = respiratory diaphragm

  • Driven by the phrenic nerve (C3–C5), with the intercostals for respiration; both automatic and voluntary (you can hold or blow out a breath).
  • Strong anchors to T12 and L1–L3 (crura) “like holding down a parachute” so it can pump; it is muscle plus fascia.
  • Dysfunction at T12–L1 is common; old EMG studies showed one side firing unequally and improving with treatment.
  • Passes the thoracic duct and lymphatics, aorta and inferior vena cava; thin-walled lymph channels are easily affected by diaphragmatic distortion, which was the essence of Zink’s study.
Respiratory diaphragm (slide 9)
Lymphatics through the diaphragm (slide 10)

LS = pelvic diaphragm

  • The pelvic floor, a bowl lined with thick fascia (not just muscle: “when you see red, think myofascial”); represented by L4, L5 and S1 and how the pelvis moves.
  • At rest it has a twisting preference, commonly shifted or rotated to the right.
Pelvic diaphragm (slide 11)

5. The Diaphragm Seesaw: Why Flow Depends on It (narration)

This section is Dr. Langevin’s explanation of how the respiratory and pelvic diaphragms work together to drive flow.

Pelvic floor during inhalation and exhalation (slide 12)
PhaseRespiratory diaphragmPelvic diaphragmPressures
InhalationContracts and descendsRelaxes (perineum drops): a bellows effect absorbs the forceThorax: low; abdomen: high
Exhalation (passive unless forced)Relaxes; pushed upActivates a littleThoracic pressure rises, drives air out
  • The low thoracic pressure speeds lymphatic flow and drives venous return (cardiac preload).
  • This seesaw between inhalation and exhalation drives respiration, lymphatics and venous flow. If it is dysfunctional, flow is not optimized, and that can be corrected.
Link to the CCPThese four diaphragms are the sites Zink examined. Free, alternating fascial tension at each lets the pump and the lymph and venous flow work; non-alternating tension impedes it.

6. Zink’s Patterns: Compensated vs Uncompensated (slides 14–15)

At each of the four zones, you record whether the fascial preference is left (L) or right (R), from top (OA) to bottom (LS).

Compensated (alternating) vs uncompensated (non-alternating) patterns (slide 15)
Compensated (alternating)Uncompensated (non-alternating)
DefinitionDirections alternate: L R L R or R L R LAnything that does not fully alternate
ExamplesLRLR (common, about 80%); RLRL (uncommon, about 20%)RRLR, RLLR, all four the same, or two adjacent zones the same and so on
LabelFunctional; in “healthy” peopleDysfunctional; usually traumatic in origin or chronic illness
OutcomeTolerate stress, injury and illness betterPoor tolerance, slower recovery
Read it as OA – CT – TL – LSThe classic CCP is OA left, CT right, TL left, LS right. Note that the LS (pelvic diaphragm) “commonly shifts right,” matching LRLR. A few people (rare, ambidextrous) show no pattern at all.

Who is compensated?

Healthy habits (not smoking, moderate alcohol, healthy eating, regular activity) tend to go with alternating patterns; poor habits, being overweight and inactive tend to go with non-alternating patterns. Dr. Langevin’s point: these people do not respond as well to an insult (trauma, virus, bacteria), so they need more attention.

7. Clinical Use and Treatment (slides 16–17)

What treating the CCP does (slide 16)

  • Relieves abnormal myofascial torsions.
  • Affects the autonomic nervous system.
  • Improves diaphragmatic function.
  • Improves venous and lymphatic flow, which brings better healing and resilience and less swelling.

The sequence (slide 17: goals of treatment)

  1. AGR assessment (slide wording; assessment first).
  2. Treat the area of trauma or complaint (low back, shoulder, knee, ankle).
  3. Perform a quick Zink exam.
  4. Areas already treated for the chief complaint do not need to be treated or Zink-examined again.
  5. Free up any abnormal pattern to encourage a more normal alternating pattern.
Dr. Langevin’s practice notes
  • Zink exam is quick and done after the complaint is treated; he checks leg length at the end, then T12–L1 rotation, then C7–T1 rotation.
  • If the pattern is not alternating, treat those areas briefly.
  • Document the pattern, for example “T12–L1 rotated right, C7–T1 rotated right.”
  • No need to retest after freeing them: the pattern probably will not be alternating immediately, especially if long-standing. You are encouraging free motion and a more compensated pattern.
  • Compensated patients may need only one or two visits; uncompensated patients need extra work.

Zink’s own treatment was high-velocity, low-amplitude (HVLA) thrust, highly targeted to one facet or a whole diaphragm, so he could do history, exam, prescribing and osteopathic treatment within 15 minutes. Other approaches (LAS, myofascial release) are used today. beyond source

Exam technique (not taught in this lecture) beyond sourceThe Zink screen is a quick hands-on test of each junction (OA, CT, TL, LS) for the direction of fascial preference (the direction it moves or rotates with greater ease); follow your OMM lab method for hand placement.
