1. Overview: Five Procedures Every Generalist Should Know

Dr. Bell’s framingDr. Bell (DO, FACOG) is not a urologist; gynecologists deal with the female bladder every day, so they need some urology. He limits the talk to five procedures every generalist should know, and the goal is that you can talk to a patient who may need one and understand the risks and complications (not that you can perform it). For every procedure he walks through the same three things: indications, contraindications, complications. He says you cannot just rely on the specialist to supply them.

Learning objectives (slide)

  • Summarize basic urologic procedures.
  • List the indications, contraindications and complications of these procedures.
  • Recognize when these procedures might be applied to your patient population.

The five procedures and why each matters to a generalist

Foley anatomy

  • A retention balloon inflated with saline keeps the catheter in the bladder. Two channels: one inflates the balloon, one drains urine to a bag.
  • Three-way (three-chamber) Foley: the third channel is normally plugged and is used to instill medication into the bladder or to irrigate clots when there is bleeding after a bladder procedure.
  • Female: short urethra, close to the vagina and rectum, so infection is a big issue. Male: the catheter passes through the penis and prostate into the bladder, so it must be longer.
Foley catheter: balloon sits in the bladder; the other end attaches to a drainage bag
Foley in a female: balloon in the bladder, urethra near the vagina and rectum
Foley in a male: passes through the penis and prostate

Suprapubic catheter: how it is placed

  1. Often done in the operating room, but can be done with local anesthetic; skin is sterilized.
  2. The bladder must be full: a Foley is placed first and the bladder is filled with fluid (very hard if the bladder is not full).
  3. Small stab incision, then a metal sleeve with a trocar (Bell’s analogy: the stake driven through Dracula’s heart) goes through the stab wound into the bladder.
  4. Remove the trocar; fluid returns = you are in the bladder.
  5. Pass the catheter through the sleeve, inflate the balloon, remove the sleeve, suture the catheter in place.
Female suprapubic catheter

Sizing (slide)

  • Diameter: French (Fr) sizing, 6–26; each French size = 0.33 mm (6 Fr = 6 × 0.33 ≈ 2 mm).
  • Length: a male needs a longer catheter than a female.

Use of catheters: indications, non-indications, contraindications (slide)

IndicationsNOT indicationsContraindications
  • Bladder drainage: relief of acute urinary retention or bladder outlet obstruction
  • Prevention of retention due to clots (very vascular bladder after bladder surgery)
  • Measurement of urine output (post-op; large surgeries, elderly: do not push into heart failure)
  • Perioperative use: urologic or prolonged surgery, large intraoperative infusion or diuretics; in gynecologic and pelvic surgery the bladder is attached to the anterior uterine wall, so drain it to see
  • Urine specimen when the patient cannot void
  • Perineal wound healing in incontinent patients (perineum = between vagina and anus; keep urine off it)
  • Instillation of medication (three-way catheter)
  • Urinary incontinence
  • Confusion
  • Prolonged bed rest with no indication
  • Measuring output in a patient who can void when hourly output is not needed (use a urinal or a “hat”)
  • Prolonged postoperative catheterization without an indication (he used to leave them 3–4 days; not anymore)
  • Urethral trauma (e.g., pelvic fracture)
  • Urethral stricture
  • Recent urologic surgery (men): talk to the urologist first
Bell’s story: do not fall into the trapAs an intern he agreed to a nurse’s request for a catheter in a confused patient who kept wetting the bed. His resident “tore him apart”: catheters are a big cause of infection and it would be his fault. Incontinence and confusion are not indications: change the bed or use a diaper.

3. Cystoscopy

Cystoscopy = endoscopy of the urinary bladder; it lets the physician look at the urethra, bladder and possibly the ureters. It may be coupled with ureteroscopy (a flexible scope passed through the bladder into the ureter).

Types of cystoscopes (slide)

RigidFlexible
BuildMetal tube with a lens and light on one end and an eyepiece (attached to a camera) on the other; does not bendBendable (thumb controls tip deflection); lens and light at one end, eyepiece at the other
Best forBiopsies and removal of growthsVisualization (can biopsy, but smaller view and smaller biopsies)
SettingUsually the operating room (may need anesthesia); possible in the office with good local anesthesiaUsually the office, under local anesthetic

Ports on a cystoscope

  • You cannot see the bladder until it is distended: one port infuses saline, another drains it as needed.
  • A third port passes instruments for biopsies or to remove masses.

Ureteroscope

  • A flexible scope passed into the bladder, then into the ureter: used to look for obstruction or disease of the ureter.
  • With a basket it can remove a stone that is not passing, an alternative to lithotripsy.

Female vs maleThe female urethra is short (about 5 cm, he says possibly less). In the male the scope goes through the urethra, through the penis and the prostate into the bladder, so it is a little more complicated.

4. Extracorporeal Lithotripsy

Definition (slide)A noninvasive procedure to treat renal stones that are too large to pass. It uses shock waves to break down the stone. Extracorporeal = outside the body: the shock waves come from outside. The patient is usually in a tub of water.
  • Bell: it is used more and more; when he trained there was no lithotripsy.
  • Ureteroscopy? (his slide question): lithotripsy can also be done from inside: a laser passed through a ureteroscope in a patient with a blocked ureter. Advanced, not available everywhere, newer.

Indications, contraindications, complications (slides)

IndicationsContraindications (Bell’s reasoning)Complications
  • Stones too large to pass
  • Obstruction of the ureter
  • Severe pain
  • Pregnancy: could injure the fetus
  • Coagulopathy: more bleeding; it is a blind, uncontrolled procedure
  • Aortic aneurysm: shock waves could rupture it; “the patient could be dead before you ever get to the operating room”
  • Severe untreated hypertension
  • Untreated UTI: a patient with a UTI and a stone should be on antibiotics before lithotripsy
  • Internal bleeding (including rupture of an aneurysm)
  • Infection
  • Kidney damage
Pattern to seeCystoscopy and lithotripsy share two contraindications: untreated UTI and coagulopathy. Lithotripsy adds pregnancy, aortic aneurysm and severe untreated hypertension. (Kemp’s stone lectures: extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy is the treatment of choice for stones 10–25 mm.)

5. Circumcision

A common elective procedure to remove the foreskin that covers the glans penis. Bell: the most common elective surgical procedure in the world. In the United States about 61% of neonates and 75% of all males 15 and over are circumcised. In some hospitals the pediatrician, or the family physician who delivers the baby, does it, so a generalist may have to.

Techniques (slide)

NewbornAdult (less common)
  • Gomco clamp: 1935
  • Plastibell: mid-1950s
  • Mogen clamp: 1954 (designed by a rabbi, not a physician)
  • Shang ring
  • Stapler device
  • Stitches: sleeve or dorsal slit technique (the foreskin is freed from the glans, a clamp is placed on the dorsal side, a slit is made, the foreskin is removed, small dissolvable sutures are placed)
  • Glue (newer; he is not sure it is available in the United States)

6. Vasectomy

Definition (slide)A surgical procedure for male sterilization. The physician cuts and seals the vas deferens, which carries sperm from the testes to the penis, so sperm no longer mix with the semen.

Procedure: inject a small amount of local anesthetic into the scrotum, make a small incision, bring the vas up, cut it, and tie or seal it. Some family doctors do it in the office; urologists do it too.

Vasectomy: the vas deferens is cut and sealed

Advantages (slide)

  • Very low risk, low cost, nearly 100% effective
  • Outpatient, local anesthesia (no general anesthesia, no hospitalization)
  • Must be sure the patient desires sterilization: if he has second thoughts, might want more children, or is worried about divorce, do not suggest it (same for women)
Bell’s counseling points
  • Never guarantee 100%. There is a small chance of failure (same with female sterilization; a woman with signs of pregnancy must come in, since it could be an ectopic pregnancy).
  • Follow-up semen analysis over the next 3 months: if any sperm are left, a pregnancy can occur, so the patient is not protected until the specimen shows no sperm. Men are often embarrassed to come back and give the sample, which is a reason some avoid vasectomy.

Complications (slide)

ComplicationBell’s note
InfectionVery rare, but possible with any surgery.
SpermatoceleSperm collect in the scrotum.
HydroceleThe scrotum fills with fluid, like a balloon.
Swelling and bruising of the scrotumExpected after any surgery: ice for the first 24 hours; over-the-counter ibuprofen or acetaminophen; usually no narcotics.

Myths (slide)

Vasectomy does not affect sexual performance, masculinity, sex drive, erections or ejaculate (he is “shooting blanks”: still ejaculating, just no sperm). It does not damage the sex organs, and it does not raise the risk of heart disease or cancer. Done well, with a clear sample, the only change is no sperm.

Vasectomy vs female sterilizationEasier for the male than the female. The exception is a woman having the procedure at the time of a cesarean section. If she must come back at 6 weeks, she needs a general anesthetic: more risk and more expense.

Bell’s one-liners

  • Catheters: incontinence and confusion are not indications; catheters are the leading cause of infection in hospitalized patients.
  • Rigid cystoscope: biopsy and removal; flexible: visualization.
  • Cystoscopy: do not do it with an untreated UTI or severe coagulopathy.
  • Lithotripsy: pregnancy, aneurysm, coagulopathy, untreated hypertension or UTI are out.
  • Circumcision: Gomco (bell protects the glans, 3–5 min, Vaseline gauze), Plastibell (stays 7–10 days, single use), Mogen (no glans protection).
  • Vasectomy: never promise 100%, semen analysis in 3 months, make sure he wants to be sterile; it does not affect performance.
