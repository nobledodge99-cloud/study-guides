1. Overview: Why Every Physician Needs This

Dr. Jois’ framing“No matter what specialty you choose, you are going to encounter and treat patients who have substance use disorders.” Physicians are not asked to be the social worker, mental health counselor or behavioral therapist: the physician’s role is primarily to address and find the risky substance use disorder, then find the people and resources to best help the patient. As an emergency physician she sees the accidents, trauma, miscarriages and relapses that risky use causes.

Objectives (slide)

  1. Quantify the prevalence of certain risky substance use in the US.
  2. Define addiction.
  3. Understand the factors behind addiction as a way to select intervention therapies.
  4. Discuss evidence-based behavioral strategies to mitigate substance misuse and addiction.
  5. Summarize intervention techniques physicians can use.

From moral failing to medical disorder

  • When scientists began studying addictive behavior in the 1930s, people with addiction were thought morally flawed or lacking willpower, which shaped society’s response: an emphasis on punishment rather than prevention and treatment.
  • Brain research has overturned this: addiction is a true medical disorder that affects the brain and changes behavior, with biological, environmental and genetic risk factors.

2. Defining Addiction and the Brain (slides 11–24)

DefinitionAddiction is a chronic relapsing disorder characterized by compulsive drug seeking and use despite known adverse consequences. It is a brain disorder: it involves functional changes to brain circuits involved in reward, stress and self-control, and many changes last long after the person stops using.
PET scans: D2 dopamine receptors in a comparison subject, 1 month after cocaine use and 4 months after (slide 18)

Cocaine PET example: compared with a non-user, the person with a cocaine history has lower levels of the D2 dopamine receptor. Four months after stopping, receptors are starting to return but have not reached baseline. Low D2 receptors may contribute to loss of control.

The three-stage cycle of problematic use (“Problematic Substance Use: Neurological”)

All addictive substances increase the release of dopamine, which activates the brain’s reward centers.

StageWhat happensBrain region
Binge / intoxicationPerson consumes the substance and experiences its rewarding or pleasurable effectsBasal ganglia
Withdrawal / negative affectNegative emotional state in the absence of the substance; ordinary rewards lose their powerExtended amygdala

3. Why Some People Are More Vulnerable: Risk and Protective Factors (slides 25–31, 55–60)

“How can I have one glass of wine and walk away while someone else finishes the bottle?” There is no single factor; as with any disease, the more risk factors, the greater the chance that taking a substance leads to use and addiction. Protective factors reduce overall risk. Both can be biological or environmental.

BiologyGenes plus the environment’s effect on gene expression (epigenetics) account for about 40–60% of the risk of addiction. Stage of development, gender and ethnicity also matter. Teenagers and people with underlying mental disorders are at greater risk than the general population.

  • Home and family: parents or older family who use drugs, misuse alcohol or break the law raise a child’s risk.
  • Peers and school: peer influence grows in the teen years; struggling in school or having poor social skills adds risk.

Other factors that raise risk

FactorDetail
Early useThe earlier substance use begins, the more likely serious problems and addiction: possible harm to the developing brain, plus shared early risk factors (unstable home, abuse, genetics, mental illness)
Route of administrationSmoking or injecting increases addictive potential: the drug reaches the brain within seconds, producing an intense rush that fades within minutes; the contrast drives repeated use
Times of transitionAdults: divorce, job loss. Teens: moving, divorce, changing schools (elementary to middle school; entering high school with new availability of cigarettes, alcohol and drug-using older peers); leaving high school for college or work, separated from family and school structure

4. Trauma and Adverse Childhood Experiences (slides 45–50)

Trauma is defined by three E’s: the Events, the Experience of those events, and the long-lasting adverse Effects. There is a strong connection between trauma and problematic substance use.

Form of traumaDefinition
AcuteOne incident of relatively short duration
ChronicRepetitive and prolonged
ComplexProlonged and repeated trauma that is invasive and interpersonal
Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs)Traumatic childhood events: physical abuse, emotional abuse, parental neglect (and sexual abuse). The stress affects brain development, causing long-term health and emotional consequences and raising the risk of problematic substance use and substance use disorder: emotional or physical maltreatment and sexual abuse in childhood all increase later risk.

Pregnancy and cessation

  • Pregnant women who smoke: preterm delivery, stillbirth, low birth weight, sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS).
  • Risk and severity of smoking-related illness depend on how long and how much the patient has smoked. It is beneficial to quit smoking at any age.
  • After quitting: risk of heart disease declines sharply; risk of stroke falls to nonsmoker levels in 2–5 years; risk of death from lung cancer drops by 50% in 10 years.

6. Habit Formation and Behavioral Intervention (slides 81–102)

The habit loopTo form a habit you need a trigger, a behavior and a reward. If the behavior is rewarding, we keep doing it.
Positive reinforcementNegative reinforcement
TriggerSeeing a piece of cakeStress
BehaviorEat the cakeEat the cake
RewardTastes good: dopamineFeel better: the negative feeling is reduced
We learn to…Repeat behaviors that feel goodRepeat behaviors that reduce bad feelings

Changing a habit

  • To reduce smoking, overeating or any other habit, reduce the reward value of the unhealthy behavior. Changing reward value is not an intellectual process: “we can’t think our way out of anxiety.”
  • Be clear and aware of how rewarding the behavior is right now, not when the habit was first laid down.
  • Compare it with more rewarding behaviors: a “bigger, better offer.” Curiosity feels better than craving or worrying.
  • Example: when the patient craves a cigarette, help them get curious about what the urge feels like in the body; this brings curiosity to the front of awareness and shows that cravings do not last forever, so they can ride it out without smoking.
  • Mindfulness = bringing awareness, curiosity and non-judgment to the present moment experience.

Behavioral intervention: three steps (slide)

#StepHow
1Recognize the habit loopMap the trigger, behavior and reward (tool: Mapmyhabit.com); map what happens if the behavior is no longer rewarding
2Update the reward valueNotice the result: ask the patient what a cigarette tastes and smells like and “what is it I’m really getting from this?” (stress snacking: 20 seconds of feeling better)
3Find the bigger, better offerCuriosity: “Why do I feel like snacking? I’m stressed”; think through the cause of the stress and how to mitigate it instead of jumping into the rewarding behavior

7. Recovery, Counselors and Counseling Approaches (slides 103–129)

SAMHSA definitionThe Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA) is the US Department of Health and Human Services agency leading public health and service delivery to promote mental health, prevent substance misuse, and provide treatment and recovery supports. Recovery is a process of change through which individuals improve their health and wellness, live a self-directed life, and strive to reach their full potential.
  • Recovery is highly individualized, not one-size-fits-all, not prescriptive. Some people pursue several approaches at once, others in sequence, others stick with one.
  • It is built on the person’s strengths, abilities, resources and values and is holistic: it addresses the whole person and community.
  • The goal could be abstinence, controlled use or somewhat reduced use. Many recover without help, but people are more likely to have long-term recovery with counseling, peer- and community-based recovery supports and medication.
  • Benefits: physical health, emotional well-being, relationships, school and career, financial security, and benefits for families, workplaces and communities. “With the right support, everyone can recover.”

SAMHSA’s four dimensions of a life in recovery

Health, Home, Purpose, Community (slide 113)
DimensionMeaning
HealthOvercoming or managing the disease or symptoms; informed, healthy choices for physical and emotional well-being
HomeA stable and safe place to live
PurposeMeaningful daily activities; independence, income and resources to participate in society
CommunityRelationships and social networks that provide support, friendship, love and hope

Counselors

Counselors need to: understand substances and addiction treatment and recovery; understand mental health conditions and common medical conditions; provide trauma-informed treatment; know how to establish a therapeutic alliance; identify and address health disparities and social determinants of health; use a strengths-based, person-centered approach; and link clients to treatment and community recovery resources.

Counseling and therapy approachesContent
Individual counselingFace to face or by telephone
Group therapyHear others’ stories and journeys; listening to and analyzing others’ behavior gives knowledge and feedback
Motivational interviewingExplore ambivalence; show the disconnect between current behavior and the patient’s values and desired outcomes
Harm reductionEncourage positive change and reduce negative health consequences of risky behaviors: safer injection practices; syringe services programs; overdose education and naloxone distribution; test strips to check drugs for fentanyl; sexual health education; protective behavioral strategies; client goal-setting. It does not replace the recovery plan: it is a step so that, if the patient relapses, there is less harm

Research-based prevention programs

Boost protective factors and reduce risk factors; for various ages, in school, home, primary care or the emergency department. Universal programs address risk and protective factors common to all children in a setting (a school or community); selective programs target groups with specific factors that raise risk; indicated programs are for youth who have already started using drugs.

8. Interventions in Primary Care: Screening, FRAMES, Brief Intervention and MI (slides 130–150)

The primary care office or emergency department is often the first point of contact. Primary care–based substance use disorder (SUD) care helps people who can’t afford or access specialty care, won’t use specialized care because of stigma, or have mild SUDs that don’t need more intensive intervention.

Screening for alcohol

ToolContent
CAGE (1984), 4 itemsCut down: ever felt you should cut down? Annoyed: do people annoy you by criticizing your drinking? Guilty: ever felt bad or guilty about drinking? Eye-opener: a drink first thing in the morning to steady nerves or get rid of a hangover?
NIAAA single question (National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism)“How many times in the past year have you had 5 or more drinks in a day (men) or 4 or more (women)?” Any answer other than zero is a positive screen

A positive screen for excessive drinking that is not a serious alcohol use disorder calls for a 5–30 minute brief intervention, a cost-effective way to reduce further risky use; severe cases may need referral to specialty addiction treatment.

FRAMES

LetterElementMeaning
FFeedbackAbout personal risk or impairment
RResponsibilityThe patient owns the change
AAdviceThe physician offers advice to change
MMenuAlternative self-help or treatment options
EEmpathyCounseling in an empathic style
SSelf-efficacyOptimistic empowerment

Brief intervention: five steps

  1. Introduce the issue in the setting of the patient’s health.
  2. Screen, evaluate and assess.
  3. Provide feedback.
  4. Talk about change and set goals.
  5. Summarize and reach closure.

The steps stay the same regardless of the number of sessions or length. Empathic, active-listening interventions are more effective than coercion or confrontation.

Motivational interviewing (MI)

  • Encourage the patient to talk about the positive aspects of changing: “change talk.”
  • Encourage the patient to argue against substance use out loud, with open-ended questions: “How would your life be different if you quit drinking?”
  • Affirm any change talk: “You said when you are sober your work is creative.”

SMART goals for avoiding risky substances

MeasurableHow much will you reduce it by?

9. Therapies, Medications and the Road to Recovery (slides 148–180)

Core messageOvercoming risky substance use is not prescriptive and not one-size-fits-all. Relapse is not a failure and the road is not a straight line. The goal may not always be abstinence: it may be cutting down, understanding cravings or mitigating triggers.

Tips for overcoming addiction (slide)

  • Learn to cope with cravings in healthy ways (curiosity: why do you have the craving?).
  • Remove addictive substances from the home (“don’t buy the wine and bring it home”).
  • Try therapy such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT).
  • Pick a clear and specific goal (SMART).
  • Understand that relapses may happen and you are not a failure.
TherapyKey points
Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)Identify and change the thoughts and behaviors that play a part in addiction. Thoughts create feelings, feelings create behavior, and behavior reinforces thoughts. Very effective across addictions, but not for everyone: some patients are not suited to analyzing their thoughts and feelings
Mindfulness-based cognitive therapyMay be easier for some patients; helpful for underlying anxiety or depression; breathing, meditation, removing stressors
Motivational enhancement therapy (MET)Increases readiness and motivation to start and stay in treatment. Five principles: applied empathy; establish discrepancy; argument-free zone; understanding resistance; encouraging self-efficacy
Family therapyHelpful especially with teens and young adults: families learn to support recovery and improve family function (family stressors can lead to substance use)
MedicationsTreat withdrawal symptoms, help patients remain in treatment and prevent relapse; the drug depends on the addiction (opioid, nicotine and alcohol medications exist). Never used solely: combine with CBT-type counseling and family support; useful in the short term and long term

The road to recovery (slides)

ThemeDetail
Changes in relationshipsFriendships change; avoid people who trigger use; it takes time to find a “new normal” and to rebuild trust; strengthen positive, supportive relationships
Avoiding replacement addictive behaviorsAddictive behaviors share neurological and psychological processes and create rewarding feelings, so replacement is common: find rewarding, healthy strategies
Treating co-occurring mental health conditionsSubstance use commonly occurs with other mental health conditions
Find distractionsTalk to a friend, a favorite show, read, volunteer, go for a walk while the craving passes

“Recovery is not a final destination but a journey”: an ongoing process of facing and coping with life without retreating into addictive behaviors, which takes continuous commitment (commitment can waver, especially when distressed). Lifestyle changes, behavioral therapy, medications and mutual support groups all play a role.
