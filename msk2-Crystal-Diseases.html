01Overview & Birefringence Fundamentals

Crystal deposition diseases present as episodic or intermittent joint attacks — that pattern alone should raise a crystal disease on the differential. The five crystals covered in this lecture, where they deposit, and what disease each causes:

CrystalDiseaseLocation
Monosodium urate (MSU)GoutJoint fluid, plasma
Calcium pyrophosphate dihydrate (CPPD)PseudogoutJoint fluid, plasma
Basic calcium phosphate (BCP / hydroxyapatite)Milwaukee shoulderPeri-articular tissue and bursa (not the joint itself)
Calcium oxalateOxalate arthropathyKidney, terminal tufts of digits, retina
Cholesterol crystals—Joint fluid of chronically inflamed/infected joints

Polarized Light Microscopy — the key discriminator

Birefringence describes how a crystal bends and colors polarized light depending on its orientation relative to the axis of the polarizing filter (the "compensator," shown as an arrow on slide images).

MSU (Gout)CPPD (Pseudogout)
ShapeNeedle-shapedRhomboid
BirefringenceNegativePositive (weak)
Parallel to light (compensator axis)YellowBlue
Perpendicular to lightBluePink
Ease of detectionEasy to findNot as easy to find
High-yield mnemonic: MSU is Needle-shaped and Negatively birefringent (yellow when parallel). CPPD is Rhomboid and gives a weak Positive birefringence (blue when parallel). BCP crystals do not react to polarized light at all — that's a key distinguishing negative finding.

02Hyperuricemia & Gout Pathophysiology

Epidemiology

  • Risk increases with age, body mass, and the degree/duration of hyperuricemia
  • Classically a disease of middle-aged males — 2–3% of the adult male population
  • Incidence in females approaches that of males after menopause (loss of the uricosuric effect of estrogen)
Not everyone with high uric acid develops gout — some patients with very high levels never have an attack. Hyperuricemia is necessary but not sufficient.

Pathogenesis of hyperuricemia

Uric acid is normally excreted about two-thirds renally and one-third through the GI tract. The uric acid pool size depends on the balance of production versus renal filtration/excretion.

Decreased renal excretion (~90% of cases)Increased urate production (~10% of cases)
Impaired renal function, dehydration, acidosisEthanol, myeloproliferative disorders
Low-dose salicylates, diuretics, pyrazinamideIneffective erythropoiesis, widespread psoriasis
Cyclosporine, levodopa, ethambutol, nicotinic acidCytotoxic drugs, glycogen storage disease
HypothyroidismG6PD deficiency, HGPRTase deficiency, ↑ PRPP synthetase activity

Why the joint becomes inflamed

Inflammation is driven by how the crystals are "coated":

  • Hot, red, acute attack: uric acid crystals coated with IgG antibody stimulate white cells, which bring in chemotactic factors, lysosomal enzymes, prostaglandins, interleukins, oxygen radicals, and collagenases — producing the acute inflammatory response
  • Tophi (chronic disease): white, pasty/cheesy deposits of uric acid crystals coated with Apo-E rather than IgG
Hyperuricemia as a cardiovascular marker: Hyperuricemia/gout should alert the clinician to an overall increased cardiovascular disease risk — especially in patients with other CV risk factors. Always screen for CVD risk in these patients.

03Clinical Features & Course of Gout

Presentation

  • Most common presentation: sudden, intense pain, classically with no preceding trauma
  • Classic site: first MTP joint ("podagra") — the great toe is erythematous, swollen, and hot to the touch
  • Other common sites: ankle, foot, knee — but it can occur anywhere
  • Pain is severe enough that patients often can't tolerate bedsheets touching the foot, or putting on socks/shoes

Acute gouty arthritis

  • Abrupt onset, classically mono-articular
  • Episodic disease — attacks can be separated by weeks, months, or years
  • Attacks become poly-articular over time
  • Tophi indicate chronic, long-standing urate overload

Physical exam patterns

PatternDefinition
MonoarthritisOne hot, red, swollen, tender joint
OligoarthritisMore than one joint, but fewer than 4 joints
PolyarthritisMany joints affected (seen with chronic tophaceous disease)

The three clinical stages of gout

Not every patient progresses through all three stages.

StageNameFeatures
IAsymptomatic hyperuricemiaNo arthritis; possible independent risk for atherogenesis
IIAcute intermittent arthritisAttacks last 3–10 days with pain, swelling, stiffness, redness, fatigue, ± fever; intervals of 1–2 weeks up to 2–10 years; initially 1–2 joints, may progress to 4–5 joints over time
IIIChronic arthritis with acute exacerbationsTophi develop in bone and cartilage; continuous arthritis with acute attacks superimposed on the chronic picture
Tophi mark the transition into advanced disease — they represent hardened uric acid deposits that erode bone and cartilage and cause joint deformity.

04Evaluation of Gout

Gold standard: Synovial fluid analysis with polarized light microscopy, showing needle-shaped, negatively birefringent MSU crystals.
  • Uric acid level: may be elevated or normal/low during an acute attack — it is not diagnostic on its own
  • Consider inflammatory markers/other rheumatologic tests when the picture is unclear: ESR, CRP, ANA, rheumatoid factor, anti-CCP
  • Consider Lyme disease serology in endemic areas
  • Always assess cardiovascular disease risk

05Treatment of Gout

Gout treatment splits into two categories: prophylactic (urate-lowering, long-term) and acute (anti-inflammatory, symptom control).

Indications for prophylactic treatment

  • Recurrent attacks (2–3 per year)
  • Tophaceous gout
  • Radiographic changes showing joint destruction
  • Uric acid kidney stones
  • Patient with debilitating polyarticular attacks
  • Risk of acute tumor lysis syndrome (e.g., before chemotherapy)
Traditional teaching says not to start allopurinol, probenecid, or febuxostat during an acute attack — but if a patient is already on them, do not stop during an acute flare. (Emerging evidence questions how strict this rule needs to be.)

Diet and alcohol

Even the strictest purine-free diet only lowers uric acid by about 1 mg% — medication is far more effective than dietary restriction. Alcohol consumption can trigger gout flares and should be addressed in counseling.

06Pseudogout (CPPD)

Key clinical features

  • Inflammatory arthritis whose incidence increases with age
  • Rhomboid, weakly positively birefringent CPPD crystals, seen in the plane of polarized light
  • Associated with chondrocalcinosis (cartilage calcification)
  • Most commonly affects the knee

Associated medical conditions

  • Hyperparathyroidism
  • Hypothyroidism
  • Hemochromatosis
  • Wilson's disease
  • Osteoarthritis

Evaluation

Work-up is initially similar to gout (synovial fluid analysis, uric acid, tests for other joint disease), but should also screen for the associated conditions above: parathyroid disease, thyroid disease, liver disease, hemochromatosis.

Imaging hallmark: calcification of the articular cartilage, seen well on plain knee X-ray and on ultrasound.

Treatment

Similar to acute gout: colchicine, NSAIDs, intra-articular steroid injections.

Milwaukee shoulder is caused by BCP crystal deposition in the bursa, not the joint space — and BCP's failure to polarize is itself a useful clue when a chronically swollen joint doesn't fit the gout/pseudogout pattern.

08Clinical Pearls & Case Review

Clinical pearl: A presumptive diagnosis of acute osteoarthritis — especially in large joints — that isn't responding to adequate treatment could actually be pseudogout (CPPD). Try colchicine: if it works, CPPD is the most likely cause.
Clinical pearl: Intra-articular corticosteroid injections work well for both CPPD and OA, but they will mask the presence of CPPD if it's present. Try colchicine first if CPPD is suspected, before injecting steroids.
Case 1

A 76-year-old male has one week of worsening right index finger pain. He recently doubled his furosemide dose (for mild CHF) to 80 mg/day due to swelling, and recalls hitting the same finger with a hammer two months ago that had since improved — now it's worse again. Aspiration yields yellow fluid with white, thick material; exam shows apparent tophi with joint space narrowing on X-ray. Polarized microscopy shows needle-shaped crystals that are blue when perpendicular to the light and yellow when parallel.

Diagnosis: Negative birefringence + needle-shaped crystals = gout (MSU). Note the diuretic dose increase as a precipitant — diuretics reduce renal urate excretion.

Drug of choice for this acute flare: NSAIDs, colchicine, or corticosteroids.

Case 2

A 68-year-old rancher with OA since age 50 presents with a painful, swollen right knee unrelieved by acetaminophen/codeine. A neighbor's colchicine gave him diarrhea but improved the knee temporarily. Uric acid is normal. Aspiration yields 60 cc of amber fluid. Ultrasound of the anterior knee (transverse view) shows calcification of the femoral hyaline cartilage; X-ray shows CPPD deposition in the same location. Polarized microscopy shows rhomboid crystals with positive birefringence.

Diagnosis: Pseudogout (CPPD) — normal uric acid plus a positive response to colchicine (which is not gout-specific) both fit, and the cartilage calcification confirms it.

Case 3

A 60-year-old female who cleans condos at a ski resort has chronic swelling of the right shoulder and knee over 6 months. History is notable only for OA and HTN (treated with an ARB + diuretic). CBC/BMP are unremarkable except an elevated monocyte count and ESR of 70 mm/hr. Uric acid, calcium, and phosphorus are all normal. Exam shows a markedly swollen, fluctuant shoulder with X-ray showing widespread calcification around the humeral head. Aspiration yields 20 cc of opaque serosanguinous fluid.

Diagnosis: Basic calcium phosphate (hydroxyapatite) deposition — Milwaukee shoulder. Note that uric acid, calcium, and phosphorus are all normal — BCP disease doesn't show up on standard metabolic screening, and the crystals themselves won't polarize.
