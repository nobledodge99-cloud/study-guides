1. Cholinergic Agonists (Direct-Acting)

Direct muscarinic and nicotinic agonists — act straight on the receptor rather than by boosting synaptic ACh.

Acetylcholine

Non-selective agonist at both muscarinic and nicotinic receptors. Approved for local ophthalmic use, but other agents are generally preferred clinically. Rapidly hydrolyzed by cholinesterase, so it is not clinically durable.

Bethanechol

mAChR-selective agonist acting primarily on the urinary and GI tract. Used for neurogenic bladder and urinary retention. Resistant to cholinesterase breakdown.

Methacholine

Selective, potent mAChR agonist used diagnostically for bronchial airway hyperreactivity — the basis of the methacholine challenge test.

Pilocarpine

Pure mAChR agonist. Used for xerostomia, for inducing miosis during ophthalmic procedures, and for glaucoma, where it reduces intraocular pressure.

Cevimeline

Synthetic, M3-selective muscarinic agonist given as an oral tablet for xerostomia in Sjögren's syndrome or in patients with radiation-damaged salivary glands. ADRs: excessive sweating, nausea, blurred vision.

Varenicline

Partial agonist with high affinity and selectivity for α4β2 nicotinic receptors (NN). Stimulates and moderately sustains mesolimbic dopamine release, which reduces craving and withdrawal. FDA approved for smoking cessation (brand Chantix). ADRs: nausea, insomnia, headache.

2. Cholinergic Antagonists (Direct-Acting, Muscarinic + Ganglionic)

Muscarinic blockers plus one ganglionic blocker — note how their CNS penetration and charge (quaternary vs. tertiary amine) predict their side-effect profile.

Atropine

The prototype non-selective competitive muscarinic antagonist; blocks parasympathetic discharge broadly. Clinical uses span several systems:

  • GI hypermotility / traveler's diarrhea — atropine is a component of Lomotil (paired with diphenoxylate), added specifically to discourage abuse, since misuse causes dizziness, constipation, dry mouth, and blurred vision.
  • Ophthalmology — long-acting mydriasis and cycloplegia (duration roughly 6 hours to 12 days); atropine is widely distributed and carries a CNS toxicity risk, especially in children.
  • Anesthesia — blocks vagal reflexes triggered by visceral manipulation.
  • Antidote for cholinergic/organophosphate poisoning, paired with pralidoxime; atropine is a first-line antidote alongside pralidoxime and a benzodiazepine for organophosphate exposure.
Anticholinergic toxidrome mnemonic "Mad as a hatter (confusion), red as a beet (flushing), hot as a hare (hyperthermia), blind as a bat (mydriasis/blurred vision), dry as a bone (dry skin/mouth), bladder loses its tone (urinary retention), and the heart runs alone (tachycardia)."

Scopolamine

Competitive muscarinic antagonist used for motion sickness. Distributes more into the CNS than atropine and carries more CNS-related ADRs: drowsiness, dizziness, agitation, confusion.

Benztropine

Tertiary amine muscarinic antagonist used for Parkinson disease and movement disorders to reduce tremor — usually as an adjunct to dopaminergic therapy, since it's less effective alone.

Glycopyrrolate

Muscarinic antagonist used to reduce GI secretions and paired with neostigmine during reversal of neuromuscular blockade, where it blocks the unwanted parasympathetic/cholinergic effects of neostigmine.

Ipratropium

Inhaled quaternary ammonium muscarinic antagonist — charged, so it has minimal CNS effects. Used for asthma and COPD.

Tiotropium

Inhaled muscarinic antagonist (brand Spiriva), same class as ipratropium but with slower metabolism and longer duration, allowing once-daily dosing. Used for asthma and COPD.

Oxybutynin

M3-selective muscarinic antagonist for overactive bladder. ADRs: dry mouth/eyes, dizziness, constipation, blurred vision, and UTI risk from a large post-void residual.

Caution Bladder antimuscarinics (oxybutynin, darifenacin) are linked to an increased risk of dementia.

Darifenacin

M3-selective muscarinic antagonist for overactive bladder, with the same ADR and caution profile as oxybutynin.

Tropicamide

Short-acting mydriatic muscarinic antagonist for ophthalmologic exams. Onset 15–60 minutes, duration 6–24 hours — in contrast with atropine's much longer duration.

Mecamylamine

Non-selective ganglionic (nicotinic, NN) blocker; competitively blocks ACh at both parasympathetic and sympathetic ganglia, effectively blocking all autonomic outflow. Its net effect on any given tissue depends on that tissue's resting autonomic tone. It was one of the first effective hypertension therapies but has little clinical use today, since it affects both divisions non-selectively and produces intolerable ADRs.

3. Cholinesterase Inhibitors & Regenerator (Indirect-Acting)

These raise synaptic ACh indirectly by blocking its breakdown — therapeutic in small doses, dangerous to deadly in excess.
General mechanism: these agents bind acetylcholinesterase (and butyrylcholinesterase) and block its enzymatic activity, raising ACh concentration and stimulating both nicotinic and muscarinic receptors. The consequences can be therapeutic at low dose or deadly at high dose/organophosphate poisoning.

Neostigmine

Reversible AChE inhibitor — a carbamate, charged with low lipid solubility, giving it a short-to-moderate duration of action. Used for myasthenia gravis and for reversal of neuromuscular blockade during anesthesia, typically paired with glycopyrrolate or atropine.

Pyridostigmine

Reversible AChE inhibitor — also a charged carbamate, but with a longer duration of roughly 4–6 hours. Used for myasthenia gravis, reversal of neuromuscular blockade, and as military pretreatment for anticipated nerve gas exposure.

Edrophonium

Reversible AChE inhibitor, very short-acting (5–15 minutes). Used as a diagnostic test for myasthenia gravis.

Physostigmine

Reversible AChE inhibitor — a carbamate that, unlike neostigmine/pyridostigmine, is uncharged and lipid-soluble, so it crosses the blood-brain barrier. Used as the antidote for anticholinergic poisoning, reversing the "mad as a hatter" toxidrome.

Donepezil

Reversible AChE inhibitor used for dementia associated with Alzheimer disease.

Pralidoxime (PAM)

A cholinesterase regenerator, not an inhibitor. Mechanism: reactivates AChE by removing the organophosphate from the enzyme's active site — it must be given before "aging" occurs, or it becomes ineffective. It is a quaternary ammonium compound (permanently charged), so it does not cross into the CNS and is ineffective against CNS toxicity.

Standard antidote sequence Current standard antidote for organophosphate poisoning: (1) parenteral atropine, (2) pralidoxime, (3) a benzodiazepine for seizures.
Cholinergic crisis mnemonic Acute cholinesterase-inhibitor poisoning (e.g., from organophosphate pesticides like malathion, or nerve agents like sarin) causes a cholinergic crisis remembered by "SLUDGE and the Killer B's": Salivation, Lacrimation, Urination, Defecation, GI upset, Emesis — plus Bradycardia, Bronchorrhea, Bronchospasm. It also causes neuromuscular junction effects: muscle fasciculations followed by paralysis.

4. Adrenergic Agonists (Direct + Indirect-Acting)

Catecholamines, selective agonists, and the indirect-acting agents (amphetamine, ephedrine, tyramine, cocaine) that work through the presynaptic terminal rather than the receptor itself.

Norepinephrine

α1 = α2, β1 >> β2. The natural sympathetic/CNS neurotransmitter; a weak β2 agonist, so it cannot rescue anaphylaxis. Heart: direct positive inotropy/chronotropy via β1, but this is offset by indirect reflex bradycardia (blocked by atropine) — the net effect is increased force with decreased or unchanged rate. Vessels: α1-mediated vasoconstriction raises blood pressure. Therapeutic use: pressor agent for hypotension.

Epinephrine

β1 = β2 > α1 = α2. Heart (β1): increased rate, force, work, and O2 consumption. Lungs (β2): bronchodilation. Metabolic effects: hyperglycemia (β2/α2 — increases glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis, and glucagon release; diabetic patients may need more insulin) and lipolysis (β1/β3). The vascular effect is dose-dependent: at low/therapeutic doses, β2 vasodilation in skeletal muscle can dominate; at high doses, α1 vasoconstriction dominates.

Epinephrine reversal A nonselective alpha-blocker such as phentolamine can flip epinephrine's pressor response into a depressor response by unmasking β2 vasodilation.

Therapeutic uses: anaphylaxis, asthma/COPD rescue, hypotension/shock, cardiac arrest, adjunct to local anesthetics (prolongs action, reduces systemic absorption/bleeding), and intraocular surgery (mydriasis). ADRs: anxiety, tremor, headache, pulmonary edema, hyperglycemia.

Phenylephrine

Pure α1-selective agonist; a pressor agent. Vasoconstriction raises both systolic and diastolic blood pressure, which triggers reflex bradycardia (blocked by atropine). Therapeutic uses: short-term BP support during anesthesia, OTC nasal decongestant, and inducing mydriasis.

Clonidine

α2-selective agonist that stimulates presynaptic α2 receptors in the CNS to decrease sympathetic outflow. IV dosing can transiently raise blood pressure (via peripheral α2B) before lowering it (via central α2A); oral dosing lowers blood pressure through decreased cardiac output/preload. Therapeutic uses: hypertension, and as a sedative for benzodiazepine/opioid/alcohol withdrawal. ADRs: postural hypotension, dry mouth, sedation, depression.

Rebound risk Abrupt discontinuation causes rebound/hypertensive crisis, from α1 receptor upregulation during chronic use.

Dobutamine

β1-selective agonist. Increases rate and force of contraction and cardiac output without raising O2 demand as much as other sympathomimetics; minimal vascular effects. Therapeutic uses: MI, heart failure, cardiogenic shock. ADRs: may increase infarct size, arrhythmias.

Albuterol

β2-selective agonist, roughly 10-fold more potent at β2 than β1, giving lung-over-heart selectivity. Therapeutic use: bronchospasm in asthma; aerosol delivery limits systemic absorption and ADRs. ADRs: muscle tremor, tachycardia (β1 and reflex), rarely arrhythmia, anxiety, restlessness, headache, hyperglycemia, hypokalemia.

Isoproterenol

Non-selective β1 = β2 agonist with essentially no α activity; a synthetic catecholamine. Heart: increased rate/force (β1). Vessels: β2 vasodilation (increases systolic BP, decreases diastolic BP, little/no change in mean BP). Lungs: β2 bronchodilation. Therapeutic use: heart block/bradycardia (rarely used today). ADRs: flushing, angina, tachycardia, arrhythmia.

Fenoldopam

Selective D1 agonist causing peripheral arterial dilation and natriuresis. Short half-life (~10 minutes), given as a continuous IV infusion. Therapeutic use: hypertensive crisis/emergency. ADRs: reflex tachycardia, flushing, headache, increased intraocular pressure (contraindicated in glaucoma).

Dopamine

D1 = D2 > β1 > α, strictly dose-dependent:

  • Low dose: vasodilation (D1 stimulation → cAMP-mediated relaxation of mesenteric/renal vasculature; presynaptic D2 stimulation inhibits further NE release)
  • Intermediate dose: increased cardiac contractility (direct β1 stimulation)
  • High dose: vasoconstriction (α receptor activation)

Therapeutic use: cardiogenic shock, per the older ACCF/AHA 2013 guideline — though more recent evidence suggests no benefit or possible harm.

Amphetamine

Indirect-acting. Mechanism: taken up into the presynaptic neuron via NET, transported into the vesicle by VMAT, displaces NE into the cytosol, then reverses NET to release NE into the synapse. Enters the CNS, causing CNS stimulation. Repeated dosing in a short period causes tachyphylaxis (vesicular NE depletion). Dextroamphetamine is used for ADHD; methamphetamine is a heavily abused related drug.

Ephedrine

From the ephedra plant (ma huang). A mixed direct and indirect adrenergic agonist: reverses NET (indirect) and is also a weak direct agonist at α1 = α2 = β1 = β2. Long duration of action because it resists MAO/COMT breakdown, and it enters the CNS. Therapeutic uses: nasal decongestant, and maintaining blood pressure during spinal anesthesia (its long duration is useful here). Pseudoephedrine, an enantiomer, is the behind-the-counter OTC decongestant; ephedra-containing dietary supplements are FDA-banned.

Tyramine

A normal byproduct of tyrosine metabolism, found in high amounts in fermented foods (aged cheese, cured meats, tap beer). Shares the same indirect mechanism as amphetamine (reverses NET) but does not enter the CNS. Normally metabolized in the gut/liver by MAO-A.

MAOI interaction Dangerous in patients taking MAO inhibitors — too much tyramine gets absorbed intact and can precipitate a hypertensive crisis (the classic "cheese reaction").

Cocaine

Mechanism: inhibits NET (blocking reuptake of NE/DA), and at high doses blocks Na+ channels. Therapeutic use: local anesthetic (injected locally, blocks sodium channels). Recreational abuse: enters the CNS and inhibits dopamine reuptake in the limbic "pleasure center" pathway; can be smoked, snorted, or injected for rapid onset and is highly abused. Toxicity symptoms include hypertension, MI, stroke, and the sensation of "bugs crawling on the skin" (formication).

5. Adrenergic Antagonists (Beta-Blockers + Alpha-Blockers)

Beta-blockers share a class-wide effect/ADR profile with individual agents distinguished mainly by selectivity; alpha-blockers split into non-selective (competitive vs. noncompetitive) and α1-/α2-selective agents.
Beta-blocker class effects (background — applies to all three below): decreases HR, cardiac output, exercise tolerance, cardiac O2 demand, and AV conduction (caution: can cause AV block); blocks β2-mediated bronchodilation (precaution/contraindicated in asthma/COPD); reduces aqueous humor secretion (used for glaucoma); reduces glycogenolysis/glucagon secretion (caution in diabetics — can mask/prolong hypoglycemia). Therapeutic uses across the class include hypertension, angina, arrhythmia, post-MI (improves survival), migraine prophylaxis, essential tremor, and hyperthyroidism. Contraindicated in asthma, vasospastic angina/peripheral vascular disease, decompensated heart failure, and significant bradycardia. ADRs include bronchospasm, hypotension, fatigue/weakness, sexual dysfunction; abrupt discontinuation can cause rebound tachycardia/arrhythmia — never stop a beta-blocker suddenly.

Propranolol

The prototype non-selective beta-blocker (β1 = β2 >>> α). Carries the full class risk of bronchospasm, since it blocks β2 as well as β1.

Metoprolol

β1-selective ("cardioselective") beta-blocker.

β1-Selective class mnemonic "BE A MAN" = Betaxolol, Esmolol, Atenolol, Metoprolol, Acebutolol, Nebivolol.

Lower risk of bronchospasm than non-selective agents, and doesn't usually prolong hypoglycemia. Same therapeutic uses as the class (angina, MI, heart failure, hypertension — though no longer first-line for hypertension). ADRs also include CNS effects (dizziness, depression, fatigue, memory loss, vivid dreams) and arrhythmia risk, especially if stopped suddenly.

Carvedilol

A non-selective beta-blocker plus an α1-blocker. Causes vasodilation and reduced heart rate, lowering blood pressure. Showed very dramatic improvement in clinical trials for stable congestive heart failure. Unique ADRs beyond typical beta-blocker effects: orthostatic hypotension and dizziness, from the added alpha blockade.

Phentolamine

Prototype non-selective α1 = α2 competitive (reversible) antagonist. Used for diagnosis and short-term management of pheochromocytoma.

Phenoxybenzamine

Non-selective α1 = α2 noncompetitive (irreversible) antagonist. Used preoperatively in pheochromocytoma to reduce sweating and hypertension before surgery.

Clinical pearl Always give the alpha-blocker (phenoxybenzamine) before a beta-blocker in pheochromocytoma management — never beta-block first, or you risk unopposed alpha-mediated hypertensive crisis. Sequence: alpha-blocker → beta-blocker → surgery.

Prazosin

α1-selective blocker. Mechanism: prevents vasoconstriction of arteries and veins, decreasing systemic vascular resistance, venous return, and preload. Usually does not increase heart rate/cardiac output, since there's no α2 blockade and thus no increased NE release. Also relaxes prostate smooth muscle. Therapeutic uses: third/fourth-line hypertension agent (add-on in refractory cases), and for concurrent hypertension plus benign prostatic hyperplasia. ADRs: dizziness, low energy, nasal congestion, headache, drowsiness, orthostatic hypotension.

First-dose effect Notable "first-dose effect" of postural hypotension/syncope — usually dosed at bedtime to minimize this.

Doxazosin

α1-selective blocker, same class and profile as prazosin.

Tamsulosin

α1-selective blocker with higher affinity for the α1A receptor subtype, making it more prostate-selective with less blood pressure effect. Used specifically for benign prostatic hyperplasia.

Yohimbine

α2-selective blocker with relatively little legitimate clinical usefulness. Extracted from yohimbine tree bark and marketed as a dietary/health supplement for erectile dysfunction, though efficacy is questionable.
