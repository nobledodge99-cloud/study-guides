The Basics First

Everything below builds on three ideas: what preload/afterload are, what shape a murmur is, and how physical maneuvers shift one or the other. Get these three down and almost every murmur pearl below just falls out of them.

Preload vs. Afterload

Preload

Volume stretching the ventricle before it contracts (~LV end-diastolic volume). ↑ with venous return: leg raise, squatting, rapid IV fluids. ↓ with standing quickly, Valsalva strain phase, nitrates, diuretics.

Afterload

Resistance the ventricle must pump against (~systemic vascular resistance / aortic pressure). ↑ with sustained handgrip, phenylephrine, cold exposure. ↓ with vasodilators (amyl nitrite), ACE inhibitors.

HOCM and MVP are the two big "opposite" murmurs on boards — anything that shrinks the ventricle (↓ preload) makes both of them louder, while it makes almost everything else softer.

S1 Mitral + Tricuspid Closure

S1 = AV valve closure = mitral + tricuspid = "LUB" = start of systole

Marks the end of diastole / beginning of systole. Loudness depends on valve position and contractility at the moment the valves slam shut.

Loud S1 Mitral stenosis Short PR interval Hyperdynamic (fever, exercise, anemia)
Soft S1 Mitral regurgitation Long PR / 1° AV block Calcified, rigid mitral valve

S2 Aortic + Pulmonic Closure

S2 = semilunar valve closure = A2 + P2 = "DUB" = start of diastole

Splitting of S2 is the high-yield part — A2 normally comes before P2, and the gap between them is where the pathology lives.

Physiologic split

A2 → P2, widens with inspiration (↑ venous return delays P2). Normal.

Wide, fixed split

Split stays wide in both inspiration and expiration. Classic for ASD (left-to-right shunt equalizes filling).

Paradoxical split

P2 comes before A2; splits with expiration instead. Seen with delayed A2: LBBB, severe AS, HOCM.

Single S2

One semilunar valve doesn't contribute — severe AS/PS, or Eisenmenger physiology.

S3 The "Kentucky" Gallop

S3 = early diastole = rapid ventricular filling = "Ken–TUCK–y" (S1–S2–S3)

Low-pitched — use the bell, patient in left lateral decubitus, listen at the apex. Caused by blood hitting a volume-overloaded or already-full ventricle.

Normal / physiologic Kids Young athletes Pregnancy
Pathologic Systolic (dilated) heart failure Volume overload — MR, VSD

S4 The "Tennessee" Gallop

S4 = late diastole = atrial kick into a stiff ventricle = "TEN–nes–SEE" (S4–S1–S2)

Also low-pitched, bell, apex — but it lands right before S1, not after S2. Needs a functioning, coordinated atrial contraction to generate it.

Diastolic dysfunction Chronic HTN Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy Aortic stenosis Post-MI (stiff scar)
Never in A-fib no organized atrial "kick" to generate it

Aortic Valve

AS — Aortic Stenosis

AS = crescendo–decrescendo systolic = RUSB → carotids = harsh
Pulsus parvus et tardus Soft / single / paradoxically split S2 ▲ with passive leg raise / squatting (↑ preload) ▼ with Valsalva / standing (↓ preload)

AR — Aortic Regurgitation

AR = early diastolic decrescendo = LUSB, lean forward + expire = blowing
Widened pulse pressure Bounding / "water-hammer" pulse (Corrigan) Head bobbing (de Musset) Pistol-shot femorals (Traube) Austin Flint murmur (severe AR — regurgitant jet hits mitral leaflet, mimics MS)

HOCM — Hypertrophic Obstructive Cardiomyopathy

HOCM = crescendo–decrescendo systolic = LLSB → no carotid radiation = louder with ↓ preload

Asymmetric septal hypertrophy narrows the LV outflow tract; the more empty/underfilled the ventricle, the worse the obstruction — the opposite logic from a fixed valve like AS.

Autosomal dominant — sarcomere protein gene mutations (e.g. β-myosin heavy chain) Leading cause of sudden cardiac death in young athletes Often an S4 (diastolic dysfunction from the hypertrophied, stiff septum)
▲ louder with Valsalva strain, standing suddenly (↓ preload → smaller LV cavity → worse obstruction) ▼ softer with squatting, leg raise, handgrip (↑ preload/afterload → bigger LV cavity → less obstruction)

Screening pearl: any young athlete with exertional syncope, chest pain, or a family history of sudden cardiac death gets worked up for HOCM before clearance to play.

Mitral Valve

MR — Mitral Regurgitation

MR = holosystolic = apex → axilla = blowing
Soft S1 Widely split S2 (LV empties into low-pressure LA — shorter ejection)

MS — Mitral Stenosis

MS = mid–late diastolic rumble = apex, left lateral decubitus, bell = low-pitched
Opening snap after S2 (shorter S2–OS interval = more severe) Loud S1 Presystolic accentuation (if still in sinus rhythm) Most often rheumatic in origin

MVP — Mitral Valve Prolapse

MVP = mid-systolic click + late systolic murmur = apex
Click moves earlier / murmur lengthens with standing, Valsalva (↓ preload = leaflets prolapse sooner) Click moves later / murmur shortens with squatting, handgrip (↑ preload or afterload)

Tricuspid Valve

TR — Tricuspid Regurgitation

TR = holosystolic = LLSB = blowing
Carvallo's sign — louder with inspiration Prominent JVP "cv" wave, pulsatile liver Often secondary — RV dilation from pulm HTN or left heart disease

TS — Tricuspid Stenosis

TS = diastolic rumble = LLSB = low-pitched, rare
Louder with inspiration (right-sided rule) Almost always rheumatic, usually with MS

Pulmonic Valve

PS — Pulmonic Stenosis

PS = crescendo–decrescendo systolic = LUSB → back / left shoulder = harsh
Widely split S2 (delayed P2) Ejection click that decreases with inspiration (the one exception to the right-sided-louder-with-inspiration rule)

PR — Pulmonic Regurgitation

PR = early diastolic decrescendo = LUSB = louder with inspiration
Graham Steell murmur — PR from pulmonary hypertension

One Rule That Covers Most of the Above

Right-sided murmurs (TR, TS, PR) ↑ with inspiration  |  Left-sided murmurs (MR, MS, AS, AR) ↑ with expiration

Inspiration ↑ venous return to the right heart, so right-sided murmurs get louder. The pulmonic ejection click is the one well-known exception — it gets softer with inspiration.

Congenital Lesions

VSD — Ventricular Septal Defect

VSD = harsh holosystolic = LLSB, palpable thrill = no axillary radiation
Most common congenital heart defect Smaller defect → louder, higher-pitched murmur (small hole, high-velocity jet) Large, unrestrictive VSDs may be nearly silent but cause early heart failure

PDA — Patent Ductus Arteriosus

PDA = continuous "machine-like" murmur = left infraclavicular / upper LSB = spans S1–S2 and beyond
Risk factors: maternal rubella infection, prematurity, high altitude birth Wide pulse pressure + bounding pulses (continuous aorta→PA runoff, same physiology as AR) Closes with indomethacin/NSAIDs (↓ prostaglandins) Kept open with PGE1 in duct-dependent congenital lesions

Pericardial Disease

Pericardial Effusion

Effusion = fluid in the pericardial space = muffled/distant heart sounds
Can be asymptomatic if it accumulates slowly (pericardium stretches to compensate) Pericardial friction rub if concurrent pericarditis — scratchy, 3-component, loudest sitting forward at end-expiration, LLSB ECG: low voltage ± electrical alternans if large

Cardiac Tamponade

Tamponade = Beck's triad = ↓BP + ↑JVP + muffled heart sounds
Pulsus paradoxus — SBP drop >10 mmHg with inspiration Tachycardia, dyspnea; rapid accumulation matters more than total volume Diagnosis: echocardiography (diastolic RV/RA collapse) Treatment: emergent pericardiocentesis

Don't confuse with Kussmaul's sign (JVP rises with inspiration) — that one is classic for constrictive pericarditis, not tamponade.

Left vs. Right Heart Failure

Backs up intoKey findings
Left-sided HFthe lungsDyspnea, orthopnea, PND, bibasilar crackles, S3 gallop, pink frothy sputum if severe pulmonary edema
Right-sided HFthe systemic venous systemJVD, hepatomegaly / hepatojugular reflux, dependent (peripheral) edema, ascites
Most common cause of right-sided HF = left-sided HF

Cor pulmonale = right heart failure caused primarily by lung disease (e.g. COPD, pulmonary hypertension) rather than by left heart failure.

Gallops Side-by-Side

S3 — Kentucky

Timing: right after S2

Mechanism: passive filling

Think: volume overload / systolic HF

S4 — Tennessee

Timing: right before S1

Mechanism: atrial kick

Think: stiff ventricle / diastolic dysfunction

Summation gallop = S3 + S4 fuse into one sound when tachycardia shortens diastole enough that they overlap

Where This Fits With Murmur Man

S1 and S2 are the bookends the murmur poster already uses — everything on the "SYST." row happens between S1 and S2, and everything past S2 (LD/ED) is diastolic. S3 and S4 are just extra sounds that show up inside that diastolic window, on either side of it — S3 tucked right after S2, S4 tucked right before the next S1.

Quick Pearl

S3 after S2 = Ken-tuck-y. S4 before S1 = Ten-nes-see. Say the words out loud — the stress pattern matches the sound.

Quick Pearl

Wide + fixed split S2 that doesn't budge with breathing = think ASD first.
