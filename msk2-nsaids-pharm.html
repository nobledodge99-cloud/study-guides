1. Mechanism of Action & Therapeutic Effects

Inflammation is a protective response to tissue injury and infection, but it can contribute to pain, swelling, and impaired function when excessive or persistent. NSAIDs are one of four major anti-inflammatory drug classes (alongside glucocorticoids, DMARDs, and gout therapies), and are the focus of this guide along with acetaminophen (APAP).

Arachidonic Acid Pathway

  • Primary mechanism: NSAIDs inhibit cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes, decreasing conversion of arachidonic acid into prostaglandins and thromboxanes.
  • NSAIDs inhibit both COX-1 and COX-2 (except celecoxib, which is COX-2 selective).
  • Net effect: ↓ prostaglandins, ↓ prostacyclin (PGI₂), ↓ thromboxane (TXA₂)
  • Glucocorticoids act upstream by inhibiting phospholipase A₂, which is why they block the whole cascade (leukotrienes included), whereas NSAIDs only block the COX branch.

COX-1 vs. COX-2

ExpressionConstitutive — active under normal conditions in many tissuesInducible — increased during inflammation

Reversibility: Most NSAIDs bind COX reversibly. Aspirin is the exception — it binds irreversibly, producing a prolonged effect on platelet function that lasts the lifespan of the platelet.

Common Clinical Uses of NSAIDs

  • Tissue injury/inflammation pain: musculoskeletal injury, arthritis, dental pain, postoperative pain
  • Prostaglandin-mediated conditions: primary dysmenorrhea, acute gout, tension-type headache, mild-to-moderate migraine
  • Generally limited benefit for neuropathic pain

2. Aspirin, Platelets & Cardiovascular Use

  • Irreversible COX-1 inhibition: aspirin permanently acetylates platelet COX-1, blocking thromboxane A₂ synthesis for the lifespan of the platelet (~7-10 days) — platelet function only recovers as new platelets are produced.
  • Other NSAIDs inhibit platelet COX-1 reversibly — platelet function recovers as the drug is eliminated, so they do not provide the sustained antiplatelet effect needed for cardioprotection.

Cardiovascular Use of Low-Dose Aspirin

  • Secondary prevention: primary role — patients with established atherosclerotic CV disease (prior MI, ischemic stroke, certain revascularization procedures)
  • Primary prevention: not routinely recommended for most patients without established CV disease — bleeding risk must be weighed against ischemic-event reduction
Ibuprofen–aspirin interaction: Ibuprofen can physically block aspirin's access to the platelet COX-1 binding site, interfering with aspirin's irreversible antiplatelet/cardioprotective effect.
Timing fix: for immediate-release aspirin, take ibuprofen ≥ 8 hours before or ≥ 30 minutes after aspirin. Repeated ibuprofen dosing can still make reliable separation difficult.

Aspirin — Additional Safety Concerns

  • ↑ Bleeding risk
  • Reye syndrome → avoid in children/adolescents with a viral illness
  • Salicylate toxicity (tinnitus can be an early sign)

4. Major Toxicities of NSAIDs

The same reduction in prostanoid production that produces therapeutic effects can also disrupt protective physiologic functions. Key concept: NSAID toxicities are largely predictable from the normal functions of prostaglandins, prostacyclin, and thromboxane. Major systems affected: GI tract, kidneys, platelets, cardiovascular system, respiratory tract, pregnancy.

Gastrointestinal Injury & Bleeding

  • Normal protective role of prostaglandins: ↑ mucus/bicarbonate secretion, maintain mucosal blood flow, support epithelial repair
  • Effects of NSAID use: dyspepsia and erosions → peptic ulceration → GI bleeding
  • Higher-risk patients: older adults, prior ulcer/GI bleed, higher doses or multiple NSAIDs, concurrent bleeding-risk medications
  • COX-2–selective inhibitors reduce GI risk — they do not eliminate it

Renal Effects & Fluid Retention

  • Normal role of prostaglandins: maintain renal blood flow (especially important when perfusion is reduced), promote sodium excretion
  • Effects of NSAID use: ↓ renal blood flow/GFR → acute kidney injury; ↓ sodium excretion → sodium/water retention → peripheral edema, ↑ BP, worsening heart failure
  • Higher-risk: dehydration, CKD, heart failure, cirrhosis
  • These renal effects occur with both nonselective and COX-2–selective NSAIDs.

Cardiovascular & Platelet Effects

  • Thrombotic risk: ↓ prostacyclin + preserved platelet thromboxane → shift toward vasoconstriction/thrombosis → ↑ MI and stroke risk; fluid retention/↑BP further raise CV risk
  • Platelet function/bleeding: COX-1 inhibition → ↓ thromboxane → impaired platelet aggregation → ↑ bleeding risk (temporary with most NSAIDs); risk compounds with anticoagulants/antiplatelets
  • Celecoxib: little direct effect on platelet aggregation, but GI and CV risks remain
Clinical principle: use the lowest effective dose for the shortest appropriate duration.

Hypersensitivity Reactions

  • Selective allergic reactions: immune-mediated, to an individual NSAID
  • Cross-reactive COX-1-mediated reactions: occur with multiple, unrelated COX-1-inhibiting NSAIDs; may cause cutaneous or respiratory symptoms
  • NSAID-exacerbated respiratory disease (N-ERD): classic cross-reactive respiratory reaction; associated with asthma, chronic rhinosinusitis, nasal polyps; COX-1 inhibition ↑ leukotriene signaling → bronchospasm

Pregnancy & Fetal Toxicity

Gestational AgeConcernGuidance
≈ 20 weeks and laterFetal renal dysfunction, oligohydramniosUse only when necessary; lowest effective dose, shortest duration

Other Effects (recognize, don't drive routine selection)

  • CNS: headache, dizziness; tinnitus can be an early sign of salicylate toxicity
  • Hepatic: liver enzyme elevations may occur; significant hepatotoxicity uncommon (contrast with acetaminophen)
  • Chronic analgesic nephropathy: prolonged/excessive analgesic use → slowly progressive chronic kidney injury

Individual NSAIDs — Core Agents know in depth

All NSAIDs inhibit COX enzymes and share many therapeutic effects, toxicities, and drug interactions. Individual agents differ in COX selectivity, reversibility, potency/duration, dosage forms, therapeutic uses, and adverse-effect profile.

Aspirin

Unique features: irreversible COX inhibition; persistent platelet inhibition.

Major clinical role: low-dose antiplatelet therapy; secondary prevention of MI/ischemic stroke; primary prevention only in limited, individualized cases.

Major safety concerns: ↑ bleeding risk, Reye syndrome (avoid in children/teens with viral illness), salicylate toxicity.

Key point: irreversible platelet inhibition distinguishes aspirin from every other NSAID.

Ibuprofen (Advil, Motrin)

Common uses: pain, fever, dysmenorrhea, musculoskeletal pain, arthritis.

High-yield distinctions: OTC doses are primarily analgesic/antipyretic; higher scheduled doses give a greater anti-inflammatory effect. Shorter acting than naproxen.

Important: may reduce low-dose aspirin's antiplatelet effect (see timing rule above).

Naproxen (Aleve, Naprosyn)

Common uses: same as ibuprofen.

High-yield distinctions: longer acting than ibuprofen → less frequent dosing.

Important: relatively favorable cardiovascular profile among NSAIDs — but this does not mean it is cardiovascularly safe; class CV risk still applies.

Diclofenac

Dosage forms: oral and topical.

Common uses: inflammatory pain, musculoskeletal pain, localized OA pain (topical).

High-yield distinctions: notable ↑ cardiovascular risk; rare hepatotoxicity → monitor LFTs with prolonged systemic therapy. Topical formulation ↓ systemic exposure.

Indomethacin (Indocin)

Major clinical role: acute gout; closure of patent ductus arteriosus.

High-yield distinctions: relatively high GI toxicity; more CNS adverse effects (headache, dizziness, confusion).

Pharm pearl: often reserved for selected short-term indications rather than routine use.

Ketorolac (Toradol)

Major clinical role: moderate-to-severe acute pain; IV/IM administration; opioid-sparing analgesia.

High-yield distinctions: maximum total duration = 5 days; oral therapy only as a step-down after parenteral therapy.

Major safety concerns: high GI toxicity, renal toxicity. Not for chronic or minor pain.

Celecoxib (Celebrex) — Selective COX-2 Inhibitor

Selectivity: selective COX-2 inhibition.

Major distinctions: ↓ GI ulcer risk vs. nonselective NSAIDs; little effect on platelet aggregation.

Common uses: OA, RA, ankylosing spondylitis, acute pain, dysmenorrhea; suitable for chronic NSAID therapy.

Major safety concerns: renal and cardiovascular risks remain; GI ulceration/bleeding still possible.

Pharm pearl: often preferred when GI risk > cardiovascular risk.

Meloxicam — Preferential COX-2 Inhibitor

Selectivity: preferential (not selective) COX-2 inhibition; more COX-1 inhibition as dose increases.

Major distinctions: less GI toxicity vs. nonselective NSAIDs; possible modest GI advantage at lower doses.

Common uses: OA and RA; common option for chronic symptom control.

Major safety concerns: renal and cardiovascular risks remain; GI toxicity still possible. Not equivalent to celecoxib.

Acetaminophen (APAP / Paracetamol)

Therapeutic Profile

  • Effects: analgesic, antipyretic
  • Key differences from NSAIDs: no meaningful anti-inflammatory activity; little effect on platelet function; minimal GI toxicity
  • Common uses: mild-to-moderate pain, fever; useful when anti-inflammatory effect is not required; alternative when NSAIDs are undesirable due to GI/bleeding risk, CV risk, or NSAID hypersensitivity; often combined with opioids

Metabolism & Overdose

  • Major toxicity: dose-related liver injury
  • Normal metabolism: a small amount is converted to the toxic metabolite NAPQI; glutathione detoxifies NAPQI under normal conditions
  • Overdose: primary conjugation pathways become saturated → ↑ NAPQI formation → glutathione depletion → NAPQI binds hepatocellular proteins → hepatic necrosis

Recognizing Overdose

  • Early symptoms may be mild or absent
  • Later: ↑ aminotransferases, RUQ pain, coagulopathy, jaundice, acute liver failure in severe cases

Treatment

  • N-acetylcysteine (NAC) replenishes glutathione; most effective when given early, but may still help after liver injury begins

Major Risk Factors for Toxicity

  • Multiple acetaminophen-containing products taken together (unintentional cumulative overdose)
  • Excessive or repeated supratherapeutic dosing
  • Fasting or malnutrition
  • Chronic heavy alcohol use
  • Preexisting liver disease

5. Clinical Selection

Before Selecting Therapy, Consider:

  • Expected duration of treatment
  • Comorbidities and concurrent medications
  • Overall toxicity risk

General NSAID Principles

  • Lowest effective dose
  • Shortest appropriate duration
  • Reassess if therapy continues
  • Monitor renal function, blood pressure, GI bleeding, and selected liver enzymes
Clinical ScenarioPreferred Approach
Anti-inflammatory effect requiredNonselective NSAID or celecoxib
Pain/fever without substantial inflammationAcetaminophen
↑ GI ulcer/bleeding riskAcetaminophen, topical NSAID, celecoxib, or gastroprotection (PPI) if systemic NSAID is necessary
↑ Renal riskAvoid systemic NSAIDs when possible; consider acetaminophen or topical NSAID
Established cardiovascular diseaseAvoid NSAIDs when possible; if needed, lowest dose/shortest duration — naproxen may be preferred (relatively favorable CV profile)
PregnancyAvoid NSAIDs ≥20 wks, avoid entirely ≥30 wks (except limited obstetric use); acetaminophen generally preferred
Central principle: match the medication to the patient's risk profile.

Key Takeaways

  • Mechanism: Inhibit COX enzymes → ↓ prostaglandin synthesis
  • Therapeutic effects: Analgesic, antipyretic, and anti-inflammatory
  • Most effective for: Inflammatory or prostaglandin-mediated conditions
  • Major toxicities: Gastrointestinal, renal, cardiovascular, platelet, hypersensitivity, and pregnancy
  • Before prescribing: Assess patient risk factors and review concomitant medications
