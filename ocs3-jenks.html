Practice Question

01. Definition of Clinical Practice Guidelines

"Clinical practice guidelines are systematically developed statements to assist practitioner and patient decisions about appropriate health care for specific clinical circumstances" — Institute of Medicine, 1990
  • Guidelines are recommendations for diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis, based on a careful review of the current available medical evidence on a disease or treatment topic
  • They help healthcare professionals understand the most important conclusions from all available studies on a topic, and how to use that information to improve patient care
  • Guidelines are not fixed protocols that must be followed — they identify and describe generally recommended courses of intervention, and are intended to complement, not replace, clinical judgment
  • Patient and provider should work together to determine the best course of action for each individual patient
  • Guidelines are produced by many different organizations — healthcare organizations, medical societies, governmental bodies, and professional bodies — at the local, regional, national, and international levels
  • Because different organizations may review the same evidence differently, guidelines produced by different organizations on the same topic may make different recommendations

02. History of Clinical Practice Guidelines

  • The first known practice guidelines probably appeared in the Hippocratic Corpus, a collection of medical texts written between the 6th and 4th centuries BCE — authorship by Hippocrates himself is debated, but this collection likely contains the earliest clinical guidelines between ancient Greece and today
  • Beginning in the 1850s: standardization of medical terminology
  • 1880–1914: standardization of physician credentials
  • Turn of the 20th century: large public health programs developed to prevent and treat endemic diseases like tuberculosis
  • Until the 1970s: medical practice was regulated mainly by standardized training and credentials recognized by medical schools and state authorities, rather than by formal guidelines
  • 1970–1990: practice guidelines started to appear
  • 1989: the federal Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) was established with a mandate to produce practice guidelines
  • 1990s: modern clinical practice guidelines developed alongside an Institute of Medicine report and the emergence of evidence-based medicine (EBM)
  • By 2022: more than 3,000 practice guidelines had been published worldwide

03. Key Principles of Guideline Development

  • Establish a multidisciplinary guideline development group
  • Include a wide range of experts from both local and regional contexts
  • Identify the problem and develop recommendations that are applicable and transferable across sites and health systems
  • Collaborate and consult with people both inside and outside the development group
Important limitation: Even with these key principles in place, there is still inconsistency in clinical practice and quality of care between different health organizations worldwide. The process for developing clinical practice guidelines has not yet been standardized, so guidelines of varying quality continue to be produced.

04. Criteria for Trustworthy Guidelines (IOM Standards)

Based on the consensus report Clinical Practice Guidelines We Can Trust (Institute of Medicine of the National Academies), the most trustworthy guidelines can be recognized by how well they meet the following eight standards:

StandardComments
1. TransparencyAn explicit description of the development and funding process should be publicly accessible
2. Conflict of interestConflicts of interest for the guideline development group should be managed by reporting, exclusion, and divestment
3. Members of the guideline development groupThe group should be multidisciplinary and balanced, comprising clinicians, methodological experts, and representatives (including a patient or consumer) of the population expected to be affected by the guideline
4. Review of the literatureThe guideline should be based on a rigorous, systematic review of the literature, considering the quality, quantity, and consistency of the aggregate evidence
5. Rating strength of evidence and recommendationsEach recommendation should be accompanied by its underlying reasoning, potential benefits and harms, the evidence and its quality, the contribution of values/experience, a rating of confidence in the evidence, a rating of the strength of the recommendation, and any differences of opinion
6. Presentation of recommendationsThe guideline should state precisely the recommended actions, when they should be performed, and how compliance could be measured
7. External reviewThe guideline should be reviewed by the full spectrum of relevant stakeholders, with an open period for public comment before it is finalized
8. UpdatingThe guideline should state its date of publication and evidence review, and be updated when new, clinically important evidence becomes available
Board tip: The IOM has noted that all of these criteria should be met for a guideline to be judged trustworthy — meeting only some of them is not sufficient.

05. GRADE: Grading Recommendations & Evidence

The Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) system is widely used to grade both the strength of a recommendation and the certainty of the underlying evidence — these are graded separately.

ComponentLevels
Strength of recommendation2 levels: Strong or Weak
Certainty of evidence4 levels: High, Moderate, Low, Very low
  • A strong recommendation is appropriate when benefits clearly outweigh risks (or vice versa) for nearly all patients
  • A weak recommendation is appropriate when risks and benefits are closely balanced or uncertain
Certainty of EvidenceTypical Source
HighWell-performed randomized controlled trials, or other overwhelming evidence (e.g., a well-executed observational study with very large effects)
ModerateRandomized trials with important limitations, or other study designs with special strength
LowObservational studies, or controlled trials with very serious limitations
Very lowNon-systematic observations, biological reasoning, or observational studies with serious limitations
Format note: Some sources (e.g., older UpToDate guideline grading) implement GRADE using a 3-level evidence-quality scale paired with a number/letter combination — for example, 1A (strong recommendation, high-quality evidence) down to 2C (weak recommendation, low-quality evidence) — rather than the 4-level "high/moderate/low/very low" scale described above. Both formats exist in practice, so recognize either presentation.

06. Other Factors in Evaluating Guideline Credibility

Guidelines should consider more than simply whether an intervention has a measurable effect. Other clinically relevant factors — collectively part of the GRADE Evidence to Decision Framework — include:

  • The magnitude of the effect
  • Harms from the intervention
  • Convenience and side effects
  • The clinical skills necessary to carry out the intervention successfully
  • Patient preferences
  • Cost and cost-effectiveness
  • The workforce necessary to implement the recommendations
On patient preferences: The role of (and variation in) patient preferences is now recognized as important for most healthcare decisions. Guidelines on most topics should explicitly state what assumptions about patient preferences were used in reaching each recommendation.
Practice Question

07. Guideline Currency, Sponsorship & Conflicts of Interest

Currency (How Up-to-Date Is It?)

  • Guidelines may be produced only once, or updated at several-year intervals
  • In fast-moving fields — such as HIV treatment — guidelines may be out of date in less than a year
  • The median survival time for a guideline (before it needs meaningful revision) is about 5.5 years
  • In the absence of strong justification, a guideline that has not undergone review (with updating, if needed) within 5 years of publication should not be considered current

Sponsoring Society

  • Statements formally endorsed by respected national bodies are subject to scrutiny by health professionals, and sometimes the public through media coverage
  • These organizations have a strong incentive to safeguard their reputation by ensuring their guidelines stand up to scrutiny
  • However, the good standing of a sponsoring organization does not guarantee quality — it is preferable that guidelines undergo careful review and endorsement by experts outside the organization (or at least by sponsoring-organization representatives other than the panel members)

Conflicts of Interest

  • Guidelines should report conflicts of interest — financial or otherwise — for each panel member
  • The IOM Committee on Standards for Developing Trustworthy Clinical Practice Guidelines recommends written disclosure of any commercial, noncommercial, intellectual, institutional, patient, or public activity pertinent to the guideline's scope
  • Conflicts of interest should be publicly disclosed and updated regularly
  • No one with a relevant conflict of interest should decide the direction or strength of a recommendation
