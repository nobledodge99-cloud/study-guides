1. Pancreatic Carcinoma: Definition, Epidemiology and Risk Factors

SourceBuilt entirely from the ScholarRx Brick Exchange "Pancreatic Carcinoma" (no slides or lecture video were provided). The brick has no red highlighting; its own summary points are treated as highest yield.
  • Pancreatic carcinoma generally means tumors of the exocrine pancreas (ductal or acinar cells), as opposed to pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) of endocrine cells. ~85% are ductal adenocarcinoma (what "pancreatic cancer" usually means).
  • Incidence: rising; 4th most common cause of cancer death in the US; slightly more common in men; increases with age (mean age at diagnosis 71). 2019 estimate: 56,770 new cases, 45,750 deaths.
  • Mortality: overall 5-year survival 9.3%; localized disease 37.4%; but >80% have already spread beyond the pancreas at diagnosis.

Risk factors

  • Family history of pancreatic cancer; cigarette smoking (one of the most important: nearly doubles the risk); obesity; chronic pancreatitis (often from alcoholism); diets high in processed and red meats (still under investigation).

2. Stepwise Genetic Progression

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma develops by stepwise accumulation of driver mutations in KRAS, p16, p53 and DPC4/SMAD4; in addition ~50% overexpress HER2.

Stepwise progression: K-ras, then p16, then p53 and DPC4 (click to enlarge)
StepGeneChange and frequencyFunction / consequence
1KRAS (12p)Activating mutation in almost all casesGTPase that relays signals while GTP-bound and is turned off by converting GTP → GDP; mutation disrupts GTPase activity, so it is stuck in the active state
2p16 (9p)Loss of function in ~95% (intermediate step)Normally inhibits CDK4/6 to block G1 → S transition; loss → unregulated proliferation
3p53 (17p)Inactivated in 50-75% (late)Halts cell cycle on DNA damage for repair or apoptosis; loss → cycle continues despite damage
3DPC4/SMAD4 (18q)Loss of function in ~55%"Deleted in Pancreatic Cancer, locus 4"; inhibits TGF-β-reliant cell development; loss → unregulated cell-cycle progression
Order to rememberKRAS (activated) → p16 (lost) → p53 and SMAD4 (lost). The only activating driver is KRAS; the others are tumor-suppressor losses.

3. Gross and Histologic Appearance, Location and Metastasis

  • Location: ~75% in the head or neck. Body 15-20%, tail 5-10%.
  • Gross: large, firm, white, infiltrative mass; often large because of late diagnosis; may replace normal pancreatic tissue entirely (only surrounding fat remains).
Gross: firm white tumor replacing the pancreas (Figure 4) (click to enlarge)
  • Histology: infiltrating well- to poorly formed malignant glands/ducts arranged haphazardly in a desmoplastic stroma: normal fibroblasts secreting dense collagen, blood vessels and inflammatory cells. Perineural and vascular invasion are common (routes of spread). Grading (differentiation) is less reliable for prognosis.
Histology: malignant glands, desmoplastic stroma and nerve (Figure 5) (click to enlarge)

"Silent" tumors

  • Body and tail tumors do not obstruct the pancreatic or bile duct, so no pain or jaundice; they grow large before symptoms appear.

Metastasis

  • Distant spread = by definition stage 4; most patients are stage 4 at diagnosis. Common sites: liver, lungs, peritoneum.

4. Clinical Course, Diagnosis and Treatment

Presentation

  • Often nonspecific: upper back and upper abdominal pain, pain after eating, indigestion, fatigue and weight loss.
  • Biliary obstruction (head tumors): jaundice, pruritus, pale and foul-smelling stools (malabsorption).
  • Mimics gallstones, IBS, gastritis, chronic pancreatitis, gastroenteritis, liver disease, indigestion.

Diagnosis

  • CT, MRI or PET; endoscopic ultrasound; blood tests. Biopsy (characteristic histology) confirms the diagnosis.
  • CA 19-9: the most specific marker, used for prognosis, not diagnosis (lower level = better prognosis). Mnemonic: the "9" looks like a pancreas (big head and a tail).

Treatment

ModalityKey points
SurgeryOnly when the whole tumor can be removed; palliative resection is not recommended; possibly underused
Chemotherapy / radiation5-fluorouracil, paclitaxel and others; radiation can enhance chemotherapy; reduces tumor size; extensive adverse effects, so used only when benefit outweighs harm
Palliative carePrognosis is poor, so care is largely supportive: stents to bypass biliary or intestinal obstruction, radiation to shrink tumor, nerve blocks for pain

Goal when possible: eliminate the cancer; tumor size tends to correlate with extent of disease.

5. Pancreatic Neuroendocrine Tumors and the Case

Pancreatic NETs (clinical correlation)

  • <5% of pancreatic tumors; benign or malignant, slow growing; arise from islet (hormone-producing) cells.
  • Insulinoma is the most common NET: hypoglycemia, weakness, sweating.
  • Most are nonfunctional (no hormone excess), so often discovered late.

Case connection: AT

73-year-old man, 20 pack-year smoker, 2 months of fatigue and 15-lb weight loss, then jaundice. Likely pancreatic head adenocarcinoma (biliary obstruction). On imaging and biopsy the cancer had not spread outside the pancreas, which means better survival and options of surgery, radiation and chemotherapy: refer to oncology and schedule follow-up.
