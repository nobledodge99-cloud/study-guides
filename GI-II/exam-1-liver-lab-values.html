1. What "Normal" Means and How Liver Disease Presents

  • Goal: review the clinical utility of liver enzymes and liver function tests. Abnormal results may accompany symptoms or be incidental (e.g. an insurance physical).
  • First step with abnormal liver tests: decide whether the disease is primarily hepatocellular or cholestatic. Correct classification makes the specific diagnosis a logical process.

How the reference range is made

  • Normal range = mean ± 2 SD in a reference population of presumably healthy volunteers. By convention values above the 97.5th percentile are "elevated". So by definition ~2.5% of people without liver disease have an abnormal value.
  • Some volunteers have subclinical liver disease, which skews the range upward.
  • Normal tests do not exclude disease: at least 16% of chronic hepatitis C patients have normal aminotransferases yet show hepatocyte injury on biopsy. A patient whose baseline ALT is 20–30 may rise only to the low 50s and still be "normal".
  • Slight AST or ALT elevations (within 1.5 times the upper limit of normal) do not necessarily indicate liver disease. About 1.5× ULN is the gray area.

2. How to Think About Liver Tests: Four Categories

Four ways to use the tests

  1. Screen for liver injury: liver cell injury vs disruption of bile flow.
  2. Diagnose the general type: hepatocellular vs cholestatic vs infiltrative.
  3. Quantitate liver function (synthetic function).
  4. Diagnose the specific disease with disease-specific tests (viral hepatitis serology, Wilson disease, hemochromatosis).

Four categories of liver tests

CategoryTests
Hepatocellular damageAST, ALT (aminotransferases)
CholestasisAlkaline phosphatase, GGT

Enzymes vs function"Liver enzymes" (AST, ALT, ALP, GGT) show injury or disrupted bile flow. True "liver function tests" are albumin and PT/INR (synthetic function). Bilirubin reflects bile excretion.
  • GGT is too sensitive to be a screening test (often elevated without liver disease). Its use: when ALP is isolated and elevated, an elevated GGT means the ALP is from the liver (rather than bone or placenta).

3. Aminotransferases: AST and ALT

  • Transaminases assess injury over the past 1–2 days; they are cleared efficiently by the reticuloendothelial system. Normal is about 35–40 IU/L (varies by lab); some always circulates because hepatocytes turn over.
ALTAST
Blood half-life~47 h~17 h total; ~87 h for mitochondrial AST
Location in hepatocyteCytosolCytosol (20%) and mitochondria (80%)
Tissue sourcesMainly liver (much less in muscle)Liver, kidney, myocardium, skeletal muscle
  • In most hepatocellular injury (e.g. viral hepatitis) AST rises first (higher activity in hepatocytes), but within 24–48 h ALT exceeds AST because of its longer half-life.
  • Three clues to etiology: AST/ALT ratio, level of elevation, rate of decline.

AST/ALT ratio

  • Normal 0.6–0.8. Most liver disorders: <1. Mild chronic hepatitis (e.g. hepatitis C) is near or just under 1 with low absolute levels.
  • >2: alcohol-induced hepatitis (AST usually <300), drugs and toxins, fulminant Wilson disease (>4), and extrahepatic sources (muscle, heart, kidney).
  • Why in alcohol: alcohol metabolites are toxic to mitochondria, releasing mitochondrial AST (long half-life, so it accumulates) into the circulation.
  • Vitamin B6 (pyridoxine) is a cofactor for both enzymes. Patients with alcohol use disorder are often deficient, so levels can be artifactually low; they rise after B6 treatment.

Limitations

  • Reflect only the past 1–2 days; may not reflect apoptosis (little enzyme release).
  • Magnitude does not correlate with the extent of liver function or dysfunction now or in the future. Normal enzymes do not mean no liver disease.
  • In alcohol injury, AST is frequently much greater than ALT; alcohol shifts mitochondrial AST to the plasma membrane.

4. Disruption of Bile Flow: Bilirubin, Alkaline Phosphatase and GGT

  • Cholestasis: accumulation in liver and blood of substances normally excreted in bile: bilirubin and bile salts.
  • Enzymes released from damaged hepatocytes by reduced or obstructed bile flow: alkaline phosphatase (the main marker of cholestasis) and GGT.

Bilirubin pathway

  1. Produced from breakdown of senescent red cells / heme (spleen, bone marrow); travels to the liver bound to albumin.
  2. Taken up by the hepatocyte (diffusion or transporter).
  3. In the smooth ER it is conjugated to glucuronic acid by glucuronyl transferase.
  4. Conjugated bilirubin is secreted into the bile canaliculus by the ATP-binding transporter MRP2. This secretion is the rate-limiting step.

Bilirubin: what it can and cannot tell you

  • Jaundice is clinically apparent when serum bilirubin exceeds 3 mg/dL. Obstruction of the bile ducts must be complete or virtually complete for bilirubin to change dramatically.
  • Bilirubin level does not distinguish hepatocellular from cholestatic disease (viral hepatitis, autoimmune hepatitis and drug toxicity can all raise it, as can bile duct injury or obstruction).
  • Bili-albumin: when bilirubin is high, conjugated bilirubin binds covalently to albumin. Half-life is several weeks (like albumin) vs hours to days for ordinary conjugated bilirubin. So jaundice resolves slowly compared with other liver tests; counsel patients that enzymes can improve while they remain jaundiced.

Alkaline phosphatase

  • A heterogeneous group of enzymes in liver, bone, placenta, kidney. Highly concentrated at the canalicular membrane, so it is sensitive to bile flow changes.
  • It is released into blood when bile acids solubilize it (GPI-anchored) or when biliary pressure rises, and bile acid retention increases its synthesis (inducible), giving the large rises in cholestasis. In pure hepatocellular injury, synthesis is not enhanced, so ALP stays modest.
  • Function unknown; possibly detoxifies lipopolysaccharide.
  • Rapidly rises in biliary obstruction (stones, ascending cholangitis, tumor), sometimes >10× ULN; degree is highly variable with duration and extent of cholestasis.

5. Hepatocellular vs Cholestatic vs Infiltrative Patterns

  • Hepatocellular: primary injury to hepatocytes. Cholestatic: primary injury to bile ducts or disruption of bile flow. Infiltrative: liver invaded by a non-hepatic substance (neoplasm, amyloid). There is overlap, especially between cholestatic and infiltrative.
  • AST, ALT and ALP are the tests that make the distinction; the key is the proportion of AST/ALT to ALP elevation.
Test patternHepatocellularCholestaticInfiltrative
AST, ALT higher than ALPTypical––
ALP higher than AST, ALT–Typical
ALP elevated with near-normal AST, ALT–TypicalTypical

Worked example from the lecture

  • AST 120, ALT 130 (normal ≤40) and ALP 130 (normal ≤120): AST/ALT are ~3× ULN but ALP is only marginally above normal → hepatocellular pattern.

Magnitudes

  • Acute hepatocellular (viral, autoimmune hepatitis): AST/ALT can reach 10–15× ULN, ALP only ~1.5–2×.
  • Cholestatic: reversed; AST/ALT ~2× ULN, ALP 5–10× ULN.
  • Bilirubin is similar in both and does not help. PT is usually abnormal only with extensive hepatocyte loss, so is seen earlier in hepatocellular disease.

7. Disease-Specific Tests and the Stepwise Approach

Screening biochemical tests are not specific for etiology. They classify the disease and estimate reserve; then order disease-specific tests.

  • Examples from the lecture: cholestatic pattern → think AMA for PBC; hepatocellular pattern after travel to Mexico → IgM anti-HAV.

Stepwise approach to abnormal liver tests

  1. Exclude non-hepatic causes (muscle trauma, nephrotic syndrome, malnutrition, etc.).
  2. Classify the pattern: hepatocellular vs cholestatic (vs infiltrative).
  3. Acute vs chronic.
  4. Estimate reserve liver function (albumin, PT).
  5. Only then think about specific etiologies and the differential, combining tests with other data.
