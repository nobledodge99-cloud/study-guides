1. Abdominal Pain: Definitions and Pathophysiology

ScopeDr. Bell (OB/GYN) gives a general approach to abdominal pain (specific diseases come in case sessions), then normal pancreatic function and benign pancreatic disease (pancreatic cancer is a separate lecture). His repeated message: history, exam, clinical judgment; "look at the patient." Red marks his one highlighted slide item.
  • Common in adults and children: ~1.5% of office visits and ~5% of ED visits. At least 10% have a severe or life-threatening cause that may need immediate surgery: never treat it as inconsequential.
  • Chronic: weeks to months. Acute: no standard definition; sudden onset but may have been present for several days (late presentation is more common in the elderly (stoic) and immunosuppressed).

When presenting to an attending, use RUQ, LUQ, RLQ, LLQ, while keeping the regional etiologies in mind.

2. Evaluation: History and Physical Examination

Most important aspect of the evaluationHistory and physical examination. Do not order a CT before you have even examined the patient (radiation exposure; tests can mislead).

History mnemonics

COLDERROLDCART
Character, Onset, Location, Duration, Exacerbation, Radiation, ReliefOnset, Location, Duration, Character, Aggravating/Associated, Relieving, Timing/Treatment (plus Severity)

OLDCART is the one in the MCOM lab manual. Elicit these before ordering a bunch of tests.

Physical examination (slide order)

  1. Vital signs: severe pain may cause tachycardia and even hypotension.
  2. Inspection of the whole patient, not just the abdomen. A patient who is smiling and joking while describing severe pain: be skeptical of a severe cause. Then the abdomen: distension, bruising, lesions, bluish periumbilical discoloration (intra-abdominal or intramuscular bleeding).
  3. Auscultation before palpation: bruit (aneurysm); hyperactive bowel sounds may mean early obstruction; absent bowel sounds do not tell you much.
  4. Palpation: light then deep; tenderness and rigidity. Trick for a patient who "jumps off the table": press with the stethoscope while "listening"; no response suggests no serious cause.
  5. Percussion: fluid, hyperresonance (distended bowel).
  6. Pelvic/rectal: pelvic is important in women with pelvic pain but can mislead: cervical motion tenderness is not always reliable (induced by how hard you move the cervix) and is limited by body habitus (BMI). Rectal for rectal bleeding, melena, severe constipation. Not every patient needs every test: use clinical judgment.

3. Laboratory and Imaging Evaluation

Labs

  • CBC: almost always; WBC can mislead: 30-40% of acute appendicitis have WBC <10,000. Hemoglobin/hematocrit matter when bleeding is suspected in a patient who does not look ill: follow serial values for a drop.
  • CMP (liver tests), amylase/lipase (pancreatic disease), urinalysis (pyuria or hematuria).
  • hCG in all women of childbearing age. Qualitative = pregnant or not; quantitative is important to rule out ectopic pregnancy or miscarriage: in early pregnancy hCG should roughly double every 48 hours (he says rise of at least 75%); a smaller rise suggests an abnormal pregnancy. Do not rely on a single value (lab error); repeat.

Imaging

StudyUse
UltrasoundPoint-of-care if the formal study is not available; transvaginal preferred for the pelvis
CTOften ordered first in the ED, but can be wrong ("rabbit hole")
Flat plate and upright abdomenKidney or calcium stones; ovarian dermoid (may show a tooth); upright film: free air from a perforated viscus
MRIStone in a pregnant woman; usually suggested by radiology
His case: imaging can misleadA 16-year-old awoke at 1 AM with acute abdominal pain (pain waking a patient is concerning). Mild distress, near-normal vitals. CT and trans-abdominal ultrasound both read hemoperitoneum; hematocrit 38 → 35.4 → 34.8 (possibly hydration). On exam she was laughing and talking, BP 110/78, pulse 88, soft flat abdomen with only mild tenderness. Diagnostic laparoscopy found only ~100 cc of blood from a ruptured ovarian lesion with no active bleeding; she went home next morning. Lesson: "If the tests are wrong, go look at the patient."

4. Quadrant Algorithms and Special Populations

Algorithms are provided for your use but can lead you down the wrong track: look at the patient, and do not delay a surgeon or gynecologist waiting hours for imaging if the patient may be unstable.

RUQ pain

Evaluation of RUQ abdominal pain (slide 12) (click to enlarge)
  • Pulmonary symptoms → pulmonary embolism or pneumonia (tachypnea, hypoxia → chest film, helical CT/D-dimer). Urinary symptoms → UTI or nephrolithiasis (costovertebral/suprapubic tenderness → UA: pyuria → UTI/pyelonephritis; hematuria → nephrolithiasis → CT). Colic → hepatobiliary cause (ultrasound; if nondiagnostic consider nephrolithiasis).

RLQ pain

Evaluation of RLQ abdominal pain (slide 13) (click to enlarge)
  • Fever or pain migrating from the periumbilical area to the RLQ → peritonitis or appendicitis → CT with IV contrast. Otherwise assess psoas sign, rigidity, rebound, guarding, right-sided rectal pain: positive → CT; negative → urine, colon or pelvic examination.

LLQ pain

Evaluation of LLQ abdominal pain (slide 14) (click to enlarge)
  • History of fever or diverticular disease → CT with oral and IV contrast or empiric treatment of diverticulitis. Otherwise assess distension, tenderness, rectal bleeding: positive → CT; negative → urinary or gynecologic evaluation.

Special populations

Evaluation in special populations (slide 15) (click to enlarge)
  • Women of childbearing age: pregnancy test first. Positive → pelvic transvaginal ultrasound for ectopic pregnancy or other pregnancy complications. Negative → consider a genitourinary cause, then general work-up.
  • Older or frail: low risk (stable vitals, few comorbidities) → consider UTI or diverticulitis, general work-up. High risk (unstable vitals, significant comorbidities) → think sepsis, perforated viscus, ischemic bowel (also aortic aneurysm): CT and consider hospitalization.

5. The Normal Pancreas and Exocrine Insufficiency

Pancreas anatomy (slide 16) (click to enlarge)
  • The pancreatic duct (plus accessory duct) empties into the duodenum at the major and minor duodenal papillae, close to where the common bile duct joins (ducts of liver, gallbladder and pancreas converge).
FunctionRole
ExocrineDigestive gland: secretes digestive enzymes for digestion of fats and other food
EndocrineRegulates blood sugar via insulin and glucagon; endocrine disease is mostly diabetes

Exocrine insufficiency: causes

AdultsChildren
Acute pancreatitis; chronic pancreatitis; diabetes; autoimmune pancreatitisCystic fibrosis (~90%); Shwachman-Diamond syndrome (rare hereditary)

Clinical features

  • Abdominal pain (pancreatitis can cause severe abdominal and back pain), plus malabsorption, malnutrition, avitaminosis, weight loss, diarrhea, flatulence.
  • Why: without enzymes, fats (and other nutrients) are not absorbed.

6. Benign Pancreatic Disease, Risk Factors and Treatment

DisorderKey points from the lecture
Chronic pancreatitisMost common; inflammatory; can be asymptomatic or severe, even fatal. Classic cause: 6-12 years of heavy alcohol; thought to involve pancreatic enzymes digesting normal pancreatic tissue, eventually causing total pancreatic failure
Acute pancreatitisSudden severe pain; males > females; most common age 30-40 (slide; the audio says 34-40)
Pancreatic cystsSerous (benign) or mucinous (mucin-producing; premalignant, more serious)
IPMN (intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasia)Precursor to pancreatic cancer (serious if invasive); lesions in the ampullary region can be removed endoscopically or by open surgery
Hereditary diseaseCystic fibrosis, Shwachman-Diamond
NeoplasmCovered in the pancreatic cancer lecture

Inciting factors

  • Poor health, genetic predisposition, improper diet, alcohol and tobacco, and possibly obesity (suspected rising with obesity; correlation not yet proven).

Treatment

  • Lifestyle change first: stop alcohol and tobacco, better diet.
  • Enzyme supplementation (chronic medication) when damage is significant; insulin if diabetes develops.
  • Surgery: total pancreatic failure/disease needs a Whipple (removes part of the stomach, much of the duodenum/intestine and the pancreas): complex, many complications, so refer to high-volume centers. Selected IPMN/ampullary lesions: endoscopic or open ampullectomy-type removal.

Pancreatitis: acute vs chronic at a glance

Pancreatitis is inflammation of the pancreas, fundamentally caused by autodigestion of its own tissue by prematurely activated enzymes. Both acute and chronic conditions typically present with severe epigastric pain that radiates to the back.

AcuteChronic
PathophysiologyAutodigestion of pancreatic parenchyma by prematurely activated pancreatic enzymes (especially trypsin). Involves liquefactive necrosis of the pancreas and peripancreatic fat necrosisIrreversible chronic inflammation, fibrosis, atrophy, and dystrophic calcification of the pancreatic parenchyma. Often secondary to recurrent episodes of acute pancreatitis
Major causesGallstones and alcohol (most common). Mnemonic “I GET PP SMASHED”*Alcohol (most common in adults); genetic predisposition (e.g., cystic fibrosis, SPINK1/PRSS1 mutations); can also be idiopathic
Clinical presentationAcute onset of severe epigastric abdominal pain that often radiates to the backMay present with epigastric pain radiating to the back; late-stage disease with signs of pancreatic insufficiency**
DiagnosisAt least 2 of 3: acute epigastric pain radiating to the back; serum amylase or lipase > 3× the upper limit of normal; characteristic imaging findingsClinical features and imaging. CT or MRCP shows atrophy, fibrosis, and dystrophic calcification; contrast studies may reveal a “chain of lakes” pattern from dilated ducts
Key serum markersSerum amylase and lipase elevated (> 3× normal)Serum amylase and lipase may or may not be elevated
ComplicationsSystemic: shock, ARDS (acute respiratory distress syndrome), DIC (disseminated intravascular coagulation), renal failure, hypocalcemia (saponification in fat necrosis). Local: pancreatic pseudocyst, walled-off necrosis (formerly abscess), hemorrhage, infectionPancreatic insufficiency (malabsorption, steatorrhea); diabetes mellitus (destruction of islets); pancreatic pseudocysts; increased risk of pancreatic carcinoma
Pancreatic pseudocyst***Can occur as a complication 4–6 weeks after an acute episodeCan also occur as a complication, similar to acute pancreatitis

*I GET PP SMASHED: Idiopathic, Gallstones, Ethanol, Trauma (e.g., seatbelt injury in children), Posterior duodenal ulcer rupture, Pancreas divisum, Steroids, Mumps, Autoimmune disease, Scorpion sting, Hypercalcemia or hypertriglyceridemia, ERCP (endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography), Drugs (sulfa drugs, nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors, protease inhibitors, diuretics). **Steatorrhea, fat-soluble vitamin deficiencies, and diabetes mellitus. ***A collection of enzymes and necrotic tissue surrounded by granulation (fibrous) tissue, not epithelium. MRCP = magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography.
