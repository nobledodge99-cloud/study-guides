1. Why the Mouth Matters and the Approach

How this guide is builtA condensed version of the clinical Oral Complaints self-directed learning module, using both the slides and the narration. Red marks what the lecturer specifically called out as important for boards or critical to know. For specific disease processes and pathology, see the oral pathology lecture.

Why does a physician worry about oral health? The mouth is critical for screening for systemic conditions and is commonly associated with other gastrointestinal (GI) or alimentary tract issues. “If the eyes are the window to the soul, the mouth is the window to the GI tract and beyond.” Oral health affects systemic health and vice versa (the “systemic connection”: heart disease, diabetes).

YearMilestone
Early 1900sTowns in the American West had children with brown tooth staining but almost no dental caries. The cause was high natural fluoride in the water, which stains but makes tough, resilient enamel. Too much fluoride is dental fluorosis.
1945Grand Rapids, Michigan became the first community to add fluoride (1 part per million) to its municipal water supply
1976Lesch proposed that bacterial infections contribute to dental caries and periodontal disease, and possibly to systemic disease
2000First Surgeon General’s report on oral health: disparities between racial and ethnic groups and barriers to accessing oral health care

The approach scheme

Approach to oral complaints: the three branches
BranchWhat to ask yourself
F. InfectiousIs the driver infectious? Many of these are self-limiting or resolve quickly with treatment, so decide this early.
B. AnatomicIs it congenital (born with it), a neoplasm (possibly cancer), or iatrogenic (something we caused)?
G. SystemicDon’t miss the association with systemic disease: leukemia and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)/acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) are the scary ones; Crohn disease and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) are the common ones with other GI involvement.
Key principleThe history and physical exam are key to a proper differential. Investigative studies (labs, biopsy, imaging) come only as needed.

2. History and Physical Exam

History of present illness (HPI): 7 characteristics of the symptom

CharacteristicOral examples
Onset/timingAcute = infection, trauma, medication effect. Chronic = systemic disease, neoplasm
LocationLips, teeth, gums, tongue, palate, mucosa
QuantitySize, number, enlarging
QualityColor, texture (soft, hard), flat, raised, ulcerated
Alleviating/aggravatingFoods, behavior (teeth grinding), trauma
Associated symptomsPain, bleeding, pus, fever
SettingWhen eating or drinking (hot/cold may indicate dental caries)

The toothbrush test

The patient says…Think…
They don’t brush their teethInfection, poor dental hygiene and caries much higher on the list
They brush and the lesion doesn’t hurtSomething more anatomic or insidious, such as cancer or a premalignant lesion
It hurts when brushingGo through the standard HPI: duration, quality, change over time, alleviating/aggravating factors, associated symptoms

The rest of the history

ElementWhy it matters
AllergiesMay cause rashes or burning sensations in the mouth
MedicationsMany have side effects that appear in the mouth
Past medical historyDiabetes, HIV, Sjögren syndrome, Crohn disease, anemia
Past surgical historyOral procedures, head and neck surgery (cancer), dental work (wisdom teeth extraction), significant history of dental caries
Tobacco and alcoholBoth are well-known risk factors for oral cancer
Drugs“Meth mouth”: methamphetamine reduces salivation (stimulant effect), is accompanied by poor dental hygiene and sugar cravings, producing severe caries and poor dentition
Sexual historyRisk for sexually transmitted infections: herpes simplex virus (HSV), human papillomavirus (HPV), HIV, gonorrhea
Social determinantsCritical to overall oral dentition and health
Review of systemsSymptoms of systemic disease
Oral gonorrheaSmall, flat, salmon-red or bright red lesions in the posterior oropharynx are concerning for bacterial infection and here are consistent with gonorrhea. You will not get this diagnosis without a thorough sexual history.

Physical exam

  • Visual and palpatory exam to identify location, size, consistency, color, bleeding, discharge and tenderness of the lips, teeth, gums, tongue, mucosa, palate, lymph nodes, salivary glands, facial bones and temporomandibular joint (TMJ), and cranial nerves.
  • Elevate the tongue. Oral lesions frequently masquerade on the inferior surface of the tongue or in the buccal mucosa beneath it. Use a tongue depressor to explore, elevate and widen the buccal and oral mucosa so all surfaces are seen.
  • Use musculoskeletal skills on the TMJ: look for restrictions and range of motion.
  • Do a thorough systemic exam if it could be a broad-reaching health condition.

3. Anatomic: Congenital Findings

FindingKey points
Cleft lip and cleft palateFailure of palatal fusion (the upper palate fails to fuse). 4th most common birth defect in the United States, about 1 in 700 births; higher rates in people of Asian, Latino or Native American descent. Cause unknown, likely genetic plus environmental (intrauterine exposure to medications or viruses); greater risk if a sibling, parent or relative is affected
MicrognathiaAbnormally small lower jaw (mandible), so the tongue is disproportionately large for the oral cavity. Caused by many inherited disorders and syndromes. Can cause feeding problems and upper respiratory obstruction in the neonate; if so, surgical osteotomy to lengthen the jaw. Primarily cosmetic in older children
MicrostomiaCongenital or acquired reduction in the size of the oral opening, severe enough to compromise nutrition and quality of life. Cause usually found from the history. Examples: Freeman-Sheldon syndrome (congenital); commissure burn from biting an electrical cord with scarring (secondary microstomia)
Torus palatinusCommon developmental exostosis (bony outgrowth) of the hard palate, appears in adults around age 30, more common in women, always midline. Benign; rarely needs treatment; occasionally removed because it interferes with fitting dentures
Torus mandibularisSimilar bony outgrowth; always on the lingual surface of the mandible near the bicuspids. Now thought less likely congenital and more due to extra pressure on the teeth (clenchers)
MnemonicTaurus, as in the bull: hard like bullhorns. Anytime you hear “torus,” think bony outgrowth.

4. Anatomic: Neoplasms and Pre-neoplasms

Benign findings that can fool you

LesionKey points
Oral papillomaBenign tumor induced by human papillomavirus (HPV). Looks concerning but is not dangerous; filiform or flat papular
Oral fibromaCheek biters or smokeless tobacco users: scarring response to chronic trauma of the buccal mucosa
Morsicatio buccarumPlaque or ulcer from cheek biting
Oral hemangiomaCould this be melanoma? Deep vascular bluish hue without other pigment change, and it may blanch because of its vascular nature

Premalignant

LesionKey points
LeukoplakiaAsymptomatic white patch (leuko = white, plakia = plaque) from chronic irritation: infection, alcohol, tobacco or friction (e.g., braces or hardware). Can occur on mucosa, tongue, palate or floor of the mouth. Premalignant: can transform into squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) (about 20%)
Hairy leukoplakiaBoard association: white, filiform lesion that may be due to Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) in immunosuppressed people, such as those with HIV infection
ErythroplakiaAsymptomatic red macule or patch (“red leukoplakia”), often not irritating or painful. Same risk factors as leukoplakia but high risk of malignant transformation to SCC (over 50%): refer immediately

Malignant

CancerKey points
Squamous cell carcinomaCan present on any oral surface. Risk factors: tobacco and alcohol
Salivary gland carcinomaRare. Risk factors: tobacco, radiation, viral infections, environmental and industrial exposure (hair dressers, rubber manufacturing, nickel compounds). Symptoms: pain, swelling or mass in the gland, difficulty swallowing, and dry mouth (xerostomia) because the gland no longer makes saliva
The case: look beyond the textbookA 19-year-old college student had recurrent tongue ulcers that did not resolve after her stressful exams. She was treated with topical and oral pain medicine, then the lesions grew and spread with extreme tiredness, dry swollen lips, tonsil aches, abnormal feeling in the face and inability to stick out her tongue. She was worked up for hemochromatosis and given antibiotics before a biopsy showed squamous cell carcinoma of the tongue. Principle: oral lesions are often benign and self-limiting but can be a harbinger of other things; a non-healing, enlarging lesion needs a biopsy.

5. Anatomic: Iatrogenic (Medications and Post-surgical)

Oral side effects of medications

Oral problemDrug(s)
Xerostomia (dry mouth)Antihistamines (diphenhydramine) and anticholinergics (oxybutynin)
Dysgeusia (taste disturbance)Clarithromycin, metronidazole
Tooth discolorationTetracycline
Gingival overgrowthPhenytoin (anti-seizure)
Fungal infection (oral candidiasis)Corticosteroids (also antibiotics)
Mucositis and ulcersChemotherapy
Tooth decayAmphetamines (meth mouth: decreased saliva plus toxic exposure of the teeth)
TetracyclineIntrinsic yellow staining if exposed before the teeth are developed; the enamel stains permanently. Do not use in children or in pregnancy.
Fluoride and stainingExcess fluoride (dental fluorosis) causes brown staining but produces tough, caries-resistant enamel. The 1900s “brown mouth” towns are the classic example.
  • Phenytoin: large, heaped-up gums where the tooth barely seems to emerge from the hypertrophic gum tissue.
  • Oral thrush from antibiotics or corticosteroids: less irritating-appearing against the mucosa.

Post-surgical and post-intervention problems

ProblemKey points
Oro-antral fistulaAbnormal communication between the maxillary sinus (antrum) and the oral cavity. 50% follow removal of upper molars. Other causes: sinus surgery, tumors, osteomyelitis. Presents with acute or chronic maxillary sinusitis
Amalgam tattoo (focal argyrosis)Most common pigmentation of the oral cavity. Asymptomatic; silver filling particles leach into the surrounding mucosa, giving a black or blue-gray lesion. Hard to distinguish from melanoma, so refer for biopsy to rule out melanoma
PiercingsPain, infection, or fistulization with a permanent tract (especially tongue or cheek) leaking saliva

6. Infectious Oral Complaints

Pathogenesis: stages of poor oral health

StageWhat happens
1. PlaqueBacteria, acid, food debris and saliva combine to form plaque within about 20 minutes after eating, on the outer enamel (often below the gum line)
2. TartarIf plaque is not removed, it mineralizes into tartar
3. Gingivitis, then periodontal diseasePlaque and tartar irritate the gums (gingivitis), ultimately chronic inflammation between the tooth and the gum (periodontitis)
4. Dental cariesPlaque acid erodes the enamel, creating holes in the tooth (cavities, tooth decay)
5. Tooth abscessInfection tracks down the tooth canal to the apical region; bacteria are hard to clear from the pocket (the reason a root canal is needed)
6. Osteomyelitis and/or neck infectionSpread to adjacent bone (jaw or maxilla) or to deep neck soft tissues (Ludwig angina)

Dental infections

ConditionKey points
GingivitisRisk factors: poor oral hygiene, general illness, pregnancy, uncontrolled diabetes, rough edges of fillings, ill-fitting or unclean mouth appliances, injury, GERD. Symptoms: swollen, painful, easily bleeding gums (even with a toothbrush), anorexia. Exam: swollen gums, bright red or red-purple. Prevented with regular dental cleanings
Dental cariesTooth pain or achy feeling, especially after sweet, hot or cold food and drink, but not guaranteed to have symptoms. Exam: pits or holes in the teeth. A contributor to poor child growth and development (painful teeth reduce eating) and vice versa
Abscessed toothCaused by trauma or caries. Painful, possibly swollen tooth, face or jaw; gum redness, swelling, pus, cavity or broken tooth tender to palpation or even chewing. X-rays confirm. May progress to osteomyelitis or spread to soft tissue (Ludwig angina: extensive swelling, pain and airway compromise)

Thrush versus leukoplakia

Oral thrush (Candida)Leukoplakia
NatureFungal infectionPremalignant lesion from chronic irritation
OnsetAcuteChronic, develops slowly and persists
SettingImmune compromise, HIV, uncontrolled diabetes, recent antibiotics or corticosteroidsTobacco, alcohol, friction, infection
AppearanceWhite plaques or hairy white appearance on tongue, palate or mucosa; not on a friction surface (e.g., superior palate); can be scraped offWhite patch, often on friction surfaces
Practical testTrial of nystatin (swish and spit, or swish and swallow if it involves the posterior pharynx); clears the infectionIf lesions do not resolve, think it was not thrush

Other infectious presentations

ConditionKey points
Geographic tongueBenign migratory glossitis of unknown cause. Loss of filiform papillae gives a patchy, map-like look with clear margins; lesions change location, pattern and color. No treatment if there is no pain
Coxsackie virus (hand-foot-and-mouth disease)Know this one for boards. Oral lesions plus lesions of the hands or feet with fever and possibly sore throat; usually young children (listless, decreased appetite, significant fever). Salmon-red or deep red petechial lesions on the posterior palate; check the buccal mucosa, tongue and palate. Check the hands and feet for papules progressing to vesicles
Herpes simplex labialis (cold sores)Know this one for boards. Classically herpes simplex virus (HSV)-1, now equally HSV-2. Grouped vesicles with clear or yellowish fluid, usually outside the mouth (on the lips). A prodrome of neuralgia or tingling precedes the lesions. Oral antivirals started at symptom onset modestly decrease pain and healing time
TonsillitisSeveral viruses or streptococcal bacteria. Painful, swollen, red tonsils; usually no cough; exudates (streptococcus typically); cervical lymphadenopathy; fever; nausea or headache may accompany. Tonsillectomy only in severe recurrent cases; refer to ear, nose and throat (ENT) for recurrent or chronic tonsillitis, especially with behavioral problems or nighttime breathing difficulty

Tonsil hypertrophy grading

GradeDescription
0Tonsils surgically removed
1Barely seen; hidden just behind the tonsillar pillars
2–3Progressively larger
4Tonsils completely occlude and touch each other in the back of the throat

7. Systemic Disease with Oral Findings

ConditionOral findings and key points
Crohn diseaseCritical one to know. Inflammatory bowel disease that can affect any part of the GI tract from mouth to anus. Recurrent, painful sores of the lips or mouth often mistaken for other conditions. Exam: lip swelling, angular cheilitis (exquisitely painful with wide mouth opening; the corners fissure and bleed) or aphthous ulcers. If recurrences occur despite treating the first suspected diagnosis, think Crohn disease
GERDLowers the pH of the oral cavity. History of heartburn or reflux. Exam: gingivitis, erosion of dental enamel and dentin, tooth decay. Consider in poor dentition with heartburn
Sjögren syndromeChronic, systemic, inflammatory autoimmune disorder with lymphocytic infiltration of exocrine glands. Sicca symptoms: dry eyes and dry mouth (affects speech clarity), joint and muscle pain. Findings: parotomegaly, keratoconjunctivitis sicca, dry lobulated tongue. Investigations: anti-SS-A/anti-SS-B serology, labial or parotid gland biopsy, ophthalmology exam. “A wet mouth is a healthy mouth”
HIV/AIDSKaposi sarcoma: cancer from lymphatic endothelial cells; usually painless purple, red or brown blotches, including in the mouth. Oral candidiasis: opportunistic infection, can be painful, white plaques on the tongue or mucosa. (Hairy leukoplakia: see section 4)
Vitamin B12 deficiencyAtrophic glossitis (red, shiny, swollen tongue), pernicious anemia, numbness, weakness, unsteady gait, dementia. Risk factors: vegan diet, gastritis, gastric bypass surgery. A blood test confirms low B12
Aphthous ulcers (canker sores)Not cold sores and not HSV. Inside the mouth, uncomfortable, can happen in anyone; most often stress and self-limiting. Predisposing: familial tendency, trauma, hormonal factors, food hypersensitivity, immunosuppression (autoimmune disease, methotrexate, chemotherapy, steroids), stress. Scary causes: celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, Behçet syndrome, acute HIV, neutropenia, micronutrient deficiencies. Vitamin B12 deficiency was proposed in celiac patients, but supplementation did not reduce recurrences; some improved after eliminating gluten
LeukemiaNeoplastic disorder of white blood cells. May present with gingival hyperplasia and friable, bleeding gums. Check a complete blood count (CBC)

8. High-Yield Summary

If you see…Think…
Painless oral lesion in someone who brushes; tobacco/alcoholAnatomic or insidious: neoplasm or premalignant lesion
White patch that persists, cannot be explained by infectionLeukoplakia (premalignant, about 20% to SCC)
Painless red patchErythroplakia (over 50% to SCC): refer immediately
White hairy lesion in an HIV patientHairy leukoplakia (EBV)
White plaques, acute, after antibiotics or steroidsThrush: trial of nystatin; if not resolved, not thrush
Blue vascular lesion that blanchesHemangioma (not melanoma)
Gray-blue spot beside a silver fillingAmalgam tattoo: biopsy to rule out melanoma
Hard, midline palatal bump in an adult womanTorus palatinus
Tooth abscess with floor-of-mouth swelling and airway troubleLudwig angina
Fever, young child, posterior palate petechiaeCoxsackie (hand-foot-and-mouth): check hands and feet
Tingling then grouped vesicles on the lip borderHerpes simplex labialis: antiviral at onset
Recurrent painful mouth ulcers and angular cheilitis despite treatmentCrohn disease
Enamel erosion with heartburnGERD
Dry mouth, parotid enlargement, dry eyesSjögren syndrome: anti-SS-A/B, gland biopsy
Red shiny tongue, numbness, unsteady gaitVitamin B12 deficiency
Swollen, friable, bleeding gums not helped by hygieneLeukemia: CBC
Salmon-red posterior oropharynx lesionsGonorrhea: ask the sexual history
Xerostomia / dysgeusia / gingival overgrowth / stainingAntihistamine or anticholinergic / clarithromycin or metronidazole / phenytoin / tetracycline
Mouth sores that keep returning and growingBiopsy: look beyond the textbook
