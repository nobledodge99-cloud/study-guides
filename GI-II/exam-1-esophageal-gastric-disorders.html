1. Upper GI Studies

How this guide is builtA condensed version of Dr. Kinder’s clinical lecture on esophageal and gastric disorders, built from the slides and the narration, organized around his five cases. Questions test the principles of each case rather than the specific case details.
StudyKey points
Barium swallowUsually the first-line test for dysphagia because it is the least invasive
Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD)Suspected esophageal dysphagia, odynophagia, diagnosis of peptic ulcer, work-up for gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), upper gastrointestinal (GI) bleed, foreign body, evaluation of the first portion of the small bowel
Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP)MRI without contrast to evaluate the bile ducts, mainly for choledocholithiasis of the common bile duct; also used to work up primary sclerosing cholangitis. Disadvantage: no intervention, only diagnostic
Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP)Good for interventions: drainage, stone removal, sphincterotomy. 15% get post-ERCP pancreatitis, decreased by giving intravenous indomethacin during the procedure
ManometryMeasures pressures. Good for solid and liquid dysphagia; helps diagnose achalasia
Gastric emptying studyPatient eats a tracer meal (eggs) and the time to leave the stomach is measured. Good for diagnosing gastroparesis
pH monitoringLooks for acid reflux; still the gold standard for diagnosing GERD

2. Approach to Dysphagia

Case 1: a 55-year-old man with food sticking in his chest for 5–6 months, both solids and fluids, regurgitation, occasional postprandial vomiting, bad breath, chest pressure and 20 lb of weight loss, no choking or coughing right after eating. This is esophageal dysphagia; the test to order is a barium swallow.

3. Esophageal Motility Disorders

Achalasia (bird’s beak on barium swallow)

Achalasia
AgeMost common between 30 and 60
MechanismLack of relaxation of the lower esophageal sphincter (LES) and loss of esophageal peristalsis; thought to be autoimmune destruction of the innervation of the lower esophagus
Typical symptomsDysphagia to both liquids and solids, regurgitation, chest pain
Atypical symptomsHeartburn, weight loss, aspiration pneumonia
DiagnosisBarium swallow is the first step; EGD to exclude other causes; esophageal manometry
TreatmentBotulinum toxin injections (relax the LES); pneumatic dilation of the LES (5% risk of perforation); myotomy (cutting the LES muscle); esophagectomy as the last option

Pseudoachalasia (achalasia-like secondary disorders)

CauseKey points
Chagas diseaseInfectious disease of South and Central America caused by Trypanosoma cruzi, transmitted by the triatomine bug. Cardiovascular: arrhythmias, cardiomyopathy, thromboembolism (stroke). GI: megaesophagus (looks just like achalasia) and megacolon
Paraneoplastic syndromeFrequently with small cell lung cancer: antineuronal nuclear antibodies (ANNA-1) destroy the myenteric plexus, simulating achalasia
Lambert-Eaton syndromeAntibodies against presynaptic voltage-gated calcium channels

Diffuse esophageal spasm (DES)

DES
EtiologyUnknown; thought related to deficiency of nitric oxide
SymptomsIntermittent chest pain, dysphagia
DiagnosisBarium swallow may be normal or show the classic corkscrew; manometry confirms
TreatmentCalcium-channel blockers (diltiazem), nitrates (isosorbide), tricyclic antidepressants (imipramine), botulinum toxin. Treat all patients with a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) because almost all have some GERD

Esophageal dysmotility in systemic disorders

DiseaseEsophageal involvement
SclerodermaUp to 90%; loss of peristalsis in the distal two thirds; main symptom is GERD; treat with PPI and lifestyle changes. CREST (calcinosis, Raynaud phenomenon, esophageal dysmotility, sclerodactyly, telangiectasia) includes esophageal dysmotility
DermatomyositisStriated muscle of the oropharynx and proximal esophagus: oropharyngeal or esophageal dysphagia
Crohn disease1 to 2% of patients: ulceration, strictures, fissures, esophagobronchial fistulas, mediastinal abscesses

4. Mechanical Causes: Eosinophilic Esophagitis, Rings, Webs, Strictures, Zenker

Case 2: a 35-year-old with childhood asthma, solids-only dysphagia for months and a meat bolus impaction; EGD shows multiple concentric rings with normal mucosa. Solids only means mechanical obstruction; the condition linked to asthma is eosinophilic esophagitis.

ConditionKey points
Eosinophilic esophagitisChronic inflammation from an aberrant immune or antigenic response to food and aeroallergens. More common in children and adolescents; other allergic disorders; strong genetic predisposition. Children present with dyspepsia; adults with solid food dysphagia, food impaction or chest pain. EGD: scalloped appearance with rings (multiple rings). Immunoglobulin E (IgE) elevated in 2/3. Biopsy: eosinophilic infiltration
RingsConcentric areas of narrowing, usually distal. Schatzki ring = narrowing at the GE junction (congenital, redundant mucosa or GERD). Muscular rings lie several cm above the squamocolumnar junction (mucosa, submucosa and muscle). Gradual progressive dysphagia. Diagnosis: barium swallow or EGD. Treatment: dilation and PPI
WebsThin, eccentric membranous narrowings, most common in the proximal esophagus. Associated with bullous skin disorders, chronic graft-versus-host disease, iron deficiency anemia. Plummer-Vinson syndrome: rare, in females, cervical esophageal web plus iron deficiency anemia
Peptic strictureOlder patients with long-standing reflux. Severe inflammation → fibrosis, scarring, esophageal shortening and loss of compliance. Also from prolonged nasogastric (NG) tube placement. Biopsy and cytology are critical to separate benign from malignant. Treatment: EGD dilation (repeated) and PPI
Zenker diverticulumOutpouching of the upper esophagus. Foul-smelling breath, regurgitates food eaten days earlier, difficulty initiating swallowing solids. Often elderly; definitive treatment is surgery

5. GERD, Barrett Esophagus, Esophageal Cancer and Zollinger-Ellison

GERD

  • Symptoms: heartburn and/or regurgitation; less common cough and wheeze. 70% of noncardiac chest pain is GERD.
Alarm features
New onset at age over 60Anorexia
GI bleedDysphagia
Iron deficiency anemiaOdynophagia
Unexplained weight lossGI cancer in a first-degree relative
Treatment stepDetails
LifestyleWeight loss; elevate the head of the bed; eliminate triggers (caffeine, alcohol, chocolate, spicy foods, high fat, carbonation, peppermint)
MedicationsAntacids, surface agents, histamine-2 receptor blockers, PPIs
SurgeryNissen fundoplication

Esophageal cancer

TypeLocationCauses
AdenocarcinomaLower thirdGERD, Barrett esophagus, obesity. Now the most common because of increased Barrett esophagus
Squamous cell carcinomaUpper two thirdsAlcohol, smoking, human papillomavirus (HPV), nitrates, achalasia

Barrett esophagus

  • Metaplasia of the distal esophagus: squamous epithelium replaced by columnar epithelium, from damage by bile and/or acid.
  • Risk factors: long-standing GERD, smoking, male, older age, obesity. 20% of men with GERD have Barrett on EGD.
  • Diagnosed by EGD and biopsy; associated only with adenocarcinoma.
  • Medications do NOT reverse the epithelial changes or change cancer risk.

Zollinger-Ellison syndrome

  • Gastrinoma: a neuroendocrine tumor that stimulates extra gastrin release, so more acid than normal.
  • Very rare (1 per million) but associated with 1–3% of duodenal ulcers.
  • Diagnosis: gastrin level, multiple ulcers on EGD. The first symptom is diarrhea, not ulcers.

6. Esophagitis and Esophageal Emergencies

Case 3: a human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) patient off antiretrovirals (CD4 count 48) taking vitamin C, iron and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), with painful swallowing, fever, confusion, blurred vision, diarrhea and weight loss. Esophagitis differential: GERD, pill-induced, eosinophilic, caustic injury and infection. The appropriate test is EGD with biopsy (polymerase chain reaction (PCR) sent); histology with “owl eyes” (intranuclear inclusions) = cytomegalovirus (CMV).

Infectious esophagitis

OrganismKey points
Herpes simplex virus (HSV)Mostly immunocompromised, but can occur in immunocompetent hosts. Predominantly distal esophagus
CandidaImmunosuppression (HIV, leukemia), diabetes mellitus, corticosteroids (oral or inhaled), esophageal stasis (advanced achalasia or scleroderma)
CMVExclusively immunocompromised. Owl-eye inclusions. CMV can cause retinitis, esophagitis, colitis, pneumonia and encephalitis

Pill-induced esophagitis and caustic injury

MechanismExample
Caustic acid solutionVitamin C
Caustic alkaline solutionAlendronate
Hyperosmolar solution in contact with the mucosaPotassium chloride (KCl)
Direct drug toxicityTetracycline
  • Predisposing factors: strictures, a prominent aortic arch compressing the esophagus, improper ingestion of the pill, inadequate fluid intake.
  • Culprits: tetracyclines, clindamycin, penicillin, rifampin; some HIV drugs; bisphosphonates; chemotherapy; NSAIDs and aspirin; quinidine, potassium chloride, ferrous sulfate, ascorbic acid.
  • Caustic injury: drain cleaners, hair relaxers, oven and toilet bowl cleaners, button batteries.

Boerhaave syndrome

Boerhaave syndrome
Case clueAfter 20 minutes of intense vomiting, excruciating chest pain, shortness of breath and dizziness
MechanismSpontaneous rupture of the esophagus from a sudden rise in intraesophageal pressure during forceful vomiting; most commonly the lower third
PresentationVomiting, lower thoracic pain, subcutaneous emphysema. Severe: pleural effusions, tachypnea, abdominal rigidity, fever, hypotension
ImagingComputed tomography (CT): pneumomediastinum (air around the esophagus); X-ray may show pneumomediastinum with a left pleural effusion
OutcomeWithout immediate treatment, mediastinitis; 80% mortality if not urgently treated
Esophageal emergencies
Boerhaave syndromeFull-thickness tear
Mallory-Weiss tearSmall, superficial tear (mucosal layer; from emesis or a swallowed sharp object)
OthersIatrogenic perforation, foreign body impaction, tracheoesophageal fistula, bleeding esophageal varices

7. Dyspepsia, Gastritis and Peptic Ulcer Disease

Case 4: a 45-year-old woman with epigastric pain and nausea after meals for months. History: Hashimoto thyroiditis; medications: levothyroxine, diclofenac, Tums. Pale, epigastric tenderness. Labs: hemoglobin 8.2, mean corpuscular volume (MCV) 110, platelets 125, mildly elevated aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), normal bilirubin and lipase; CT: no cancer, normal gallbladder, mild hepatomegaly. EGD: gastritis with small erosions.

Differential for dyspepsia

CategoryExamples
Food or drug intolerance (indigestion)Overeating, eating too quickly, high-fat foods, eating during stressful situations, medications
Functional dyspepsia
Esophageal or gastric dysfunctionGERD, peptic ulcer disease, gastritis (H. pylori-induced), gastroparesis, lactose intolerance, malabsorptive conditions, parasitic infection (Giardia, Strongyloides, Anisakis)
Pancreatic diseaseChronic pancreatitis, pancreatic cancer
Biliary tract diseaseCholecystitis, cholelithiasis

Helicobacter pylori

H. pylori
PresentationSimple gastritis or peptic ulcer disease
AssociationsGastric cancer and MALT (mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue) lymphoma
DiagnosisEGD with biopsy, stool antigen test, urea breath test (patient must be off antacids for 2 weeks)
Treatment10–14 days of triple therapy: PPI + clarithromycin + amoxicillin

Causes of chronic gastritis

TypeCauses
NoninfectiousAlcohol; chemical (NSAIDs, aspirin, bile reflux); autoimmune; celiac disease; radiation; ischemic; Crohn disease, sarcoid, Wegener granulomatosis, eosinophilic granuloma
StressMechanical ventilation (over 48 hours), trauma, burns, shock, sepsis, liver and kidney disease, central nervous system injury, multiorgan failure, coagulopathy (platelets under 50,000). Treatment is prophylaxis: enteral feeding, H2 blockers, PPIs

Peptic ulcer disease

  • H. pylori is the most common cause, especially if the ulcer is duodenal.
  • NSAIDs and alcohol cause the rest and are more likely to cause gastric ulcers. Smoking exacerbates ulcer symptoms.
  • Diagnosis: EGD, and always biopsy non-healing ulcers.
  • Treatment: stop offending agents, treat H. pylori, sucralfate to coat the stomach, PPI for acid suppression.

8. Upper GI Bleeding

Case 5: a 75-year-old man with syncope on standing, fatigue, 25 lb weight loss, black stools for 6 weeks, a six-pack of beer daily for 40 years and active smoking; blood pressure (BP) 95/70, heart rate 110.

Definition and presentation
Upper GI bleedEsophagus, stomach and duodenum above the ligament of Treitz; presents with hematemesis and melena. If fast and extensive, may present with hematochezia
Lower GI bleedHematochezia

Causes

CauseKey points
Peptic ulcer (gastric or duodenal), gastric or duodenal erosions, erosive esophagitis, alcoholic gastritisCommon causes
Mallory-Weiss tearTear in the mucosal layer of the esophagus secondary to emesis or swallowing something sharp
Esophageal and gastric varicesUnderlying liver cirrhosis; usually accompanied by encephalopathy and spontaneous bacterial peritonitis
Portal hypertensive gastropathy“Snake skin” or “mosaic” stomach: dilated vessels, usually fundus and body, with or without varices
Dieulafoy lesionCongenital: a large-caliber, tortuous vessel in the stomach that erodes with eventual GI bleed
Hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (Osler-Weber-Rendu)Rare autosomal dominant disorder of abnormal blood vessels in skin, mucous membranes, lungs, liver, brain; arteriovenous malformations can cause GI bleeding
Boerhaave syndromeFull-thickness esophageal tear; may result in GI bleeding
Upper GI tumors and angiomas

Interpreting the case labs

FindingInterpretation
Hemoglobin 6.4, MCV 65Microcytic anemia, likely iron deficiency from GI bleeding
Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) 120, creatinine 3.1Chronic kidney disease or acute kidney injury
AST 220, ALT 100 (2:1)Alcohol-related hepatitis
Next stepEGD: showed a large gastric tumor

Treatment of upper GI bleeding

StepActions
Step 1: always stabilize firstBlood or intravenous fluids to improve blood pressure and get hemoglobin above 7.0; start IV PPI; platelets and fresh frozen plasma if needed; octreotide IV for bleeding varices
Step 2: once stable, EGDVariceal banding; ulcer clipping and injection
