1. GI Tract Regulation: Hormones and Nerves

Hormones by function

2. The Cephalic Phase and Deglutition

Cephalic phase

Sight or smell of food → medulla oblongata → vagus nerves → acetylcholine onto parietal, ECL, chief and G cells → acid, histamine, pepsin, lipase and gastrin released before the food arrives.

Deglutition (swallowing)

StepAirwayEsophagus
Normal (breathing)Open (glottis, nasopharynx)Closed: upper esophageal sphincter (UES) contracted
SwallowClosed (epiglottis, palate raised)Open: UES relaxes; peristalsis pushes the bolus to the lower esophageal sphincter (LES), which relaxes
  • Pharynx and top 1/3 of esophagus (skeletal muscle): voluntary. Lower 2/3 (smooth muscle): involuntary.
  • UES protects against regurgitation; LES protects against acid reflux. Swallowed air causes bloating, which can be dangerous in infants.
ClinicalAchalasia: lower esophagus paralyzed and the LES cannot open (food cannot enter the stomach; dilated esophagus). GERD: LES always open, gastric reflux damages the esophagus. Barrett's esophagus: severe complication of chronic GERD (cancer risk).

3. The Gastric Phase and the Small Intestine

Parietal cell: regulation and mechanism

Stimulate acidInhibit acid
Vagus nerve (ACh), gastrin, histamineSomatostatin, secretin, GIP
  • H+/K+ ATPase (apical): H+ exchanged for K+; primary active.
  • Cl−/HCO3− exchanger (basolateral): brings Cl− in, sends HCO3− to blood; secondary active. Cl− then leaves into the lumen.

Vagovagal reflex

Food stretches the stomach → stretch receptors → dorsal vagal complex → vagus releases ACh onto parietal, chief and G cells → acid, pepsinogen and gastrin.

Small intestine

Neutralizes acid and carries out about 90% of digestion and absorption. Two duodenal hormones matter most:

SecretinCCK
Cell / triggerS cells; low pH, fatty acidsI cells; fatty acids, small peptides
StimulatesPancreatic HCO3− (neutralizes H+, activates pancreatic enzymes)Pancreatic enzymes (trypsinogen, amylase, lipase) + HCO3−; gallbladder (bile)
InhibitsGastric H+ secretionGastric H+ secretion and gastric emptying
Memory hookAcid neutralizationFat digestion
One-liner summaryParasympathetic (ACh) stimulates; gastrin = protein digestion (H+, pepsin); secretin = acid neutralization; CCK = fat digestion; somatostatin and the sympathetic system inhibit.
Clinical: peptic ulcer diseaseZollinger-Ellison syndrome (gastrin-secreting tumor) and Helicobacter pylori (destroys the mucus layer). Treatment for H. pylori: antibiotics + omeprazole (blocks the H+/K+ ATPase proton pump while the infection is cleared).

ClinicalType 1 / type 2 diabetes: no insulin / insulin insensitive. Acute pancreatitis: the pancreas digests itself. Exocrine pancreatic insufficiency: failure of digestive enzymes → maldigestion, steatorrhea, "pseudo-starvation".

Polymers must be hydrolyzed to monomers (pancreatic enzymes) before absorption. Water and sodium transport depend on the Na+/K+ ATPase.

Carbohydrate absorption (lecture slide) (click to enlarge)
ClinicalLactose intolerance: lactase deficiency.

Protein

  • Individual amino acids: co-transport with Na+. Di- and tri-peptides: co-transport with H+. Small intact peptides: transcytosis.
  • Enterocyte → blood: facilitated diffusion, then to the liver via the hepatic portal vein.
Clinical: Hartnup diseaseDefective neutral amino acid transporter for tryptophan (SLC6A19). Pellagra-like skin eruptions, photosensitivity, ataxia and CNS involvement, diarrhea, aminoaciduria. Treat with a high-protein diet, nicotinamide, and sun avoidance.

Fat

  1. Emulsified by lecithin and bile salts
  2. Hydrolyzed by pancreatic lipase (free fatty acids + monoglyceride)
  3. Grabbed by micelles and carried into the enterocyte
  4. Packaged into chylomicrons
  5. Absorbed into blood (via lymph)

ClinicalSteatorrhea (fat in feces) = insufficient fat digestion/absorption (e.g., pancreatic insufficiency, bile salt deficiency).

6. Water and Electrolyte Absorption

RuleWater follows sodium. Every apical Na+ co-transporter (SGLT-1, amino acid, NHE3) is secondary active and depends on the basolateral Na+/K+ ATPase, which pumps Na+ out to the blood; Cl− and water follow.
IonMechanism (lumen → blood)
K+Small intestine: passive (paracellular). Distal colon: active.
Iron (as Fe2+, ferrous)Apical DMT-1 (divalent metal transporter 1) → bound by ferritin → basolateral ferroportin → carried by transferrin in blood
Ca2+Apical Ca2+ transporter → calbindin (D28K) inside the cell → basolateral Ca2+ pump (primary active) and Na+/Ca2+ exchanger
Cl−Passive and voltage-dependent (nutrient-coupled Na+ uptake makes the lumen negative; Cl− follows). Active: Cl−/HCO3− exchanger (DRA), electroneutral, with NHE3 (Na+/H+ exchanger), which raises intracellular pH
ClinicalWhipple disease: infection of the small intestine → malabsorption. Cholera: increased electrolyte (Cl−) secretion → water follows → severe watery diarrhea.

7. The Large Intestine

  • Na+ enters through the epithelial Na+ channel (ENaC) and leaves via the basolateral Na+/K+ ATPase.
  • Cl− follows Na+ (electrical gradient); Na+/Cl− absorption drives water absorption (dehydrates feces, prevents body dehydration).
  • Aldosterone stimulates Na+ absorption by acting on ENaC and the Na+/K+ pump (↑ salt retention).
  • Distal colon actively absorbs K+ (see section 6).
