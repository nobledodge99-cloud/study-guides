1. Biliary Anatomy, Bile and Neurohormonal Control

How to use this lectureHer message: anatomy is a must. If you know where bile and bilirubin flow, where they get blocked, and which cell or organ is irritated, you can derive the labs, symptoms and complications of every disorder. Her five practice questions (answer slide) are woven in as collapsed reveals.

The main track

  • Right and left hepatic ducts (intrahepatic ductal system) → common hepatic duct → joined by the cystic duct from the gallbladder → common bile duct (runs through the pancreas) → empties into the duodenum through the sphincter of Oddi. The pancreatic duct joins at the ampulla, so pancreatic enzymes share the exit.
  • Sphincter of Oddi is really a collection of sphincters, regulated mainly by CCK.

Bile

  • Made in the liver; mostly water plus bile salts, bilirubin, cholesterol, phospholipids, electrolytes. Normally alkaline: neutralizes acidic chyme.
  • Functions: fat digestion, neutralizing chyme, and eliminating toxins and bilirubin.
  • Secretin acts on cholangiocytes to drive CFTR chloride channels and bicarbonate exchange (she names cGMP; secretin signaling is usually taught as cAMP). Cystic fibrosis → thick bile, stasis, more gallstones.

  • Stasis is a key gallstone risk factor. Causes: vagotomy (no gallbladder contraction), diabetic autonomic neuropathy (hypomotility), high sympathetic tone (critical illness, stress; also acalculous cholecystitis).
  • Opioids raise sphincter tone → can cause biliary colic.

2. Cholelithiasis (Gallstones)

  • Types: most are cholesterol stones (majority in the US); mixed; pigmented (from breakdown of red blood cells, i.e., hemolysis).
  • Principle: anything that causes stasis or an imbalance in bile composition raises stone risk. Cholesterol supersaturation: ↑ cholesterol secretion (obesity, estrogen, rapid weight loss), ↓ bile salts or lecithin, gallbladder hypomotility.
  • "Five F's": female, fat (obesity), forty (age >40), fertile, family history. Others: hemolysis (pigment stones), diabetes (autonomic neuropathy → stasis), liver disease / portal hypertension, cystic fibrosis.
  • Most patients are asymptomatic. Symptoms arise from blockage.

Presentation and labs

  • Cholelithiasis itself: no Murphy sign; labs may be entirely normal (bile flows freely).
  • Biliary colic: transient RUQ pain when a stone briefly blocks the duct then passes, often after a fatty meal (CCK contracts the gallbladder against the stone).

Diagnosis and treatment

  • RUQ ultrasound: bright echoes with posterior shadowing (keywords). Thickened wall points to something else (cholecystitis).
  • Asymptomatic: no treatment. Recurrent/problematic symptoms: laparoscopic cholecystectomy (surgery has risks). Jaundice and pain after surgery → think bile duct injury.
  • Complications: acute cholecystitis (cystic duct), choledocholithiasis → cholangitis (common bile duct), gallstone pancreatitis (ampulla).
Q1: 43-year-old woman, RUQ pain to right shoulder after a fatty meal, deep-inspiration tenderness, gallstones without wall thickening or pericholecystic fluid. Mechanism?A. Cholesterol supersaturation of bile → stone formation (symptomatic cholelithiasis / biliary colic). A stone transiently obstructs the cystic duct, pressure rises, but there is no inflammation or infection. Wrong: B = PBC (chronic, fatigue/pruritus); C = acute cholecystitis (persistent pain, fever, wall thickening); D = cholangitis (Charcot triad); E = cholangiocarcinoma (painless jaundice, weight loss).

3. Acute Cholecystitis

  • Most common cause: a gallstone blocking the cystic duct → trapped bile, ↑ pressure, venous/lymphatic congestion, wall ischemia, bile salt (lysolecithin) irritation, then possible secondary infection. Acalculous cholecystitis also exists: critically ill or post-operative patients (ischemia, high sympathetic tone, stasis).
  • Pain: sustained RUQ pain (longer than biliary colic), often radiating to the right shoulder or back. Epigastric pain radiating to the back suggests pancreatitis. Nausea/vomiting, fever.
  • Murphy sign: deep palpation under the right costal margin while the patient inhales; inspiration stops (catches). Highly suggestive of acute cholecystitis.
  • Ultrasound: thickened gallbladder wall (± pericholecystic fluid) plus stones.
  • Labs: leukocytosis; otherwise often normal LFTs, because only the cystic duct is blocked and the gallbladder is "walled off" (bile still reaches the intestine).

Treatment

  • NPO (rest the gallbladder, likely surgery), IV fluids (normal saline or lactated Ringer), pain control (opioids such as morphine or hydromorphone; ketorolac mentioned), antibiotics, and cholecystectomy.
  • First-line antibiotics: ceftriaxone + metronidazole (ceftriaxone covers gram-negatives and some gram-positives but not anaerobes; metronidazole covers anaerobes; empiric broad coverage). Alternative: piperacillin-tazobactam. Penicillin allergy: a fluoroquinolone (she says "levothyroxine," almost certainly levofloxacin).
  • Complications: gangrene, perforation (rare) → peritonitis, abscess, sepsis. Emphysematous cholecystitis (Clostridium perfringens) is dangerous.
Q2: 28-year-old obese woman, acute RUQ pain with nausea and vomiting, wall thickening and pericholecystic fluid, no duct dilation. Initiating event?A. Gallstone obstruction of the cystic duct (acute calculous cholecystitis). Wrong: B = Clostridium is a possible later secondary infection (emphysematous), not the initiating event; C = PBC; D = acalculous cholecystitis (critically ill/post-op); E = pancreatitis.

4. Choledocholithiasis, Ascending Cholangitis and Gallstone Pancreatitis

Her analogyCholelithiasis is to acute cholecystitis as choledocholithiasis is to ascending cholangitis. Same stones, different location, different labs.

Choledocholithiasis (stone in the common bile duct)

  • Stones usually migrate from the gallbladder (occasionally form in the duct). Extra risks: prior stones, prior cholecystectomy, bile duct strictures, prior surgery, infection.
  • Bile cannot reach the intestine → conjugated (direct) bilirubin backs up → jaundice + RUQ/epigastric pain, nausea/vomiting. Pale stools, dark urine.
  • Labs: ↑ ALP and GGT (cholangiocyte irritation), ↑ direct bilirubin; mild ↑ AST/ALT if prolonged.
  • Imaging: RUQ ultrasound first (dilated bile ducts, sometimes the stone); MRCP (non-invasive, highly sensitive) if unclear; ERCP = gold standard for treatment (and visualization), then cholecystectomy to prevent recurrence.
  • Most common ERCP complication: pancreatitis; monitor afterwards.
Q3: 69-year-old man, jaundice, pale stools, dark urine, markedly ↑ conjugated bilirubin and ALP, dilated intra- and extrahepatic ducts. Mechanism?A. Blockage of bile flow distal to the common hepatic duct (obstructive/post-hepatic jaundice; stone or pancreatic head tumor). Conjugation continues but bile cannot exit; conjugated bilirubin is water-soluble so urine is dark; no bilirubin reaches the gut so stools are pale; ↑ALP/GGT from cholangiocyte injury. Wrong: B and E = Crigler-Najjar/severe hepatitis (indirect); C = hemolysis (indirect, normal-colored urine); D = Gilbert (mild indirect).

Acute ascending cholangitis (medical emergency)

  • Obstruction + stasis → bacteria ascend from the duodenum → infection and inflammation of the biliary tree. Most common cause: choledocholithiasis. Others: strictures (prior surgery), tumors, instrumentation (ERCP), PSC. Higher risk: elderly, immunosuppressed.
  • Charcot triad: fever + jaundice + RUQ pain. Reynolds pentad adds hypotension and altered mental status (septic shock, ICU level care). (The audio says "hypertension"; the slide key says hypotension.)
  • Labs: leukocytosis, ↑ direct bilirubin (already conjugated), ↑ ALP/GGT, mild ↑ AST/ALT; blood cultures (bacteremia).
  • Imaging: ultrasound (dilated ducts, wall thickening); MRCP for diagnosis; ERCP = gold standard for treatment (drainage).
  • Organisms: gram-negatives (E. coli, Klebsiella) and enterococci/anaerobes → broad empiric antibiotics plus fluids (normal saline or lactated Ringer).
  • Complications: sepsis/septic shock, liver abscess, renal failure, death.
Q4: 55-year-old man, fever, jaundice, RUQ tenderness, ↑ ALP and direct bilirubin, blood cultures grow E. coli. Pathophysiology?B. Bacterial infection ascending through an obstructed common bile duct (acute ascending cholangitis). Wrong: A = cholecystitis (no jaundice or bacteremia); C = hemolysis (prehepatic); D = PBC (chronic, afebrile); E = cirrhosis (ascites, varices, coagulopathy).

Gallstone pancreatitis

  • A stone lodged at the ampulla of Vater (where pancreatic duct and common bile duct join) blocks pancreatic secretions: ductal hypertension, premature trypsinogen → trypsin activation, autodigestion.
  • Epigastric pain radiating to the back; ↑ lipase and amylase; mild ↑ conjugated bilirubin from partial biliary obstruction.
  • Ranson criteria: tool for severity and prognosis of acute pancreatitis (criteria not required).
Q5: 46-year-old woman with gallstones, epigastric pain to the back, ↑ lipase, amylase and conjugated bilirubin. Link between stones and presentation?A. Stone migration into the common bile duct causing reflux/obstruction of pancreatic enzymes (gallstone pancreatitis). Wrong: B = cholecystitis; C = viral (mumps, Coxsackie); D = autoimmune IgG4 (painless jaundice, diffuse enlargement); E = alcohol sphincter spasm (history favors mechanical).

5. Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis vs Primary Biliary Cholangitis

Both injure cholangiocytes through an immune-mediated process, release TGF-β, deposit type I and III collagen and end in fibrosis and biliary cirrhosis (like stellate-cell-driven cirrhosis in the liver). They differ in where and why.

  • PSC is a risk factor for cholangitis and cholangiocarcinoma; PBC is a cirrhosis/HCC story.

6. Biliary Atresia

  • Neonatal disorder. The ducts form normally, then an immune-mediated or viral insult (cause not agreed) triggers a T-cell response: ↑ TNF-α and TGF-β → collagen deposition, fibrosis, obliteration of the ducts → cirrhosis.
  • Presentation: persistent jaundice in the first weeks to months of life; bile has nowhere to go; liver enlarges/becomes cirrhotic; ↑ ALP and GGT (cholangiocyte irritation), direct hyperbilirubinemia.
  • Treatment: Kasai procedure (hepatoportoenterostomy; she says "portal enterostomy") bypasses the blocked ducts so bile drains into the intestine; extreme cases need liver transplant. Complication: cirrhosis.
