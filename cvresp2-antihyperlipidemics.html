1. Lipid Physiology & Lipoprotein Review

Why Lipids Need Lipoproteins

  • Lipids (triglycerides, cholesterol, phospholipids) are hydrophobic and cannot circulate freely in aqueous plasma
  • Triglycerides — three fatty acids esterified to a glycerol backbone; primary energy storage form
  • Cholesterol — a sterol; structural component of membranes and precursor for steroid hormones, bile acids, and vitamin D; exists free and esterified
  • Phospholipids — amphipathic; form membrane bilayers and stabilize the lipoprotein surface monolayer

Lipoprotein Structure

Every lipoprotein is a sphere: a hydrophobic core of triglycerides and cholesteryl esters, surrounded by a surface monolayer of phospholipids, free cholesterol, and apolipoproteins. Apolipoproteins play structural, enzymatic, and receptor-binding roles.

Exogenous Pathway

  • Dietary triglycerides/cholesterol absorbed by enterocytes → packaged into chylomicrons (ApoB-48) → enter circulation via lymph
  • Lipoprotein lipase (LPL), endothelium-bound and activated by ApoC-II, hydrolyzes chylomicron triglycerides into free fatty acids + glycerol for tissue use/storage
  • Cholesterol-enriched chylomicron remnants (containing ApoE) are cleared by the liver via receptor-mediated uptake

Endogenous Pathway

  • Liver assembles triglycerides + cholesterol into VLDL (ApoB-100)
  • Stepwise LPL-mediated hydrolysis: VLDL → IDL → LDL
  • IDL may be taken up directly by the liver or further processed by hepatic lipase into LDL
  • LDL (ApoB-100) is ~70–75% cleared by hepatic LDL receptors — the major pharmacologic target in hypercholesterolemia

Reverse Cholesterol Transport (HDL)

  • Cholesterol efflux: free cholesterol transfers from peripheral cells (especially macrophages in plaques) onto nascent HDL (ApoA-I)
  • LCAT esterifies this free cholesterol into cholesteryl esters, which migrate into the HDL core → mature HDL
  • HDL delivers cholesteryl esters to the liver two ways: directly via SR-B1, or indirectly via CETP (transfers esters to VLDL/IDL/LDL, which are then cleared by LDL receptors)
  • HDL cholesterol efflux capacity is emerging as a more reliable marker of cardioprotection than HDL-C concentration alone

📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Approximately what percentage of circulating LDL is cleared via hepatic LDL receptors, making this pathway the major target in hypercholesterolemia treatment?

Explanation: Roughly 70–75% of circulating LDL is cleared by hepatic LDL receptors, which is why upregulating these receptors (statins, ezetimibe, PCSK9 inhibitors, bempedoic acid, bile acid sequestrants) is the central strategy in LDL-lowering pharmacotherapy.

2. Which apolipoprotein serves as the essential cofactor that activates lipoprotein lipase, allowing hydrolysis of chylomicron and VLDL triglycerides?

Explanation: ApoC-II is the obligate activating cofactor for lipoprotein lipase; without it (as in ApoC-II deficiency), triglyceride-rich lipoproteins cannot be hydrolyzed, producing severe hypertriglyceridemia.

3. HDL can deliver cholesteryl esters to the liver directly, without destroying the HDL particle, via which receptor?

Explanation: SR-B1 (scavenger receptor class B type 1) allows the liver to selectively extract cholesteryl esters from HDL directly, remodeling and recycling the particle — the 'direct' route of reverse cholesterol transport.

2. Pathophysiology & Classification of Dyslipidemia

What Is Dyslipidemia?

  • Abnormal levels of cholesterol, triglycerides, or both — a major modifiable risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD)
  • Types: hypercholesterolemia (↑LDL-C), hypertriglyceridemia (↑TG), mixed dyslipidemia (both)
  • Severe hypertriglyceridemia (TG >500 mg/dL, especially >1,000 mg/dL) → sharply increased risk of acute pancreatitis

Primary (Genetic) Dyslipidemias

DisorderDefectKey Feature
Familial hypercholesterolemia (FH)Mutations in LDL receptor, ApoB, or PCSK9Markedly ↑LDL-C, premature ASCVD; homozygous FH can exceed 500 mg/dL, presenting in childhood
Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)]Apo(a) covalently linked to ApoB-100Independent risk factor for CAD and aortic stenosis; not on standard lipid panels; impairs fibrinolysis, promotes inflammation
Familial combined hyperlipidemia↑ApoB and VLDL overproductionVariable phenotype (high LDL, high TG, or both) within the same family
Familial dysbetalipoproteinemiaRemnant particle accumulation (often ApoE2/E2)Roughly proportional ↑cholesterol and ↑TG
Familial chylomicronemia syndromeLPL deficiency or ApoC-II defectsExtreme hypertriglyceridemia, recurrent pancreatitis

Secondary (Acquired) Dyslipidemias

  • Uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, obesity, hypothyroidism, chronic kidney disease, nephrotic syndrome, excessive alcohol intake
  • Medications: glucocorticoids, antiretrovirals, atypical antipsychotics
  • Every dyslipidemia workup should screen for these secondary causes, which often require simultaneous management

Atherogenesis: The Mechanistic Story

LDL infiltrates the endothelium → oxidative modification → oxidized LDL triggers inflammation → monocytes differentiate into macrophages → macrophages engulf oxidized LDL via scavenger receptors → foam cells (hallmark of early plaque) → ongoing inflammation + smooth muscle proliferation → fibrous cap → plaque progression → unstable plaque rupture → thrombosis/acute coronary syndrome.
  • Triglyceride-rich lipoproteins (VLDL and their remnants) are also pro-atherogenic, though less so than LDL
  • When TG >500–1,000 mg/dL, LPL-mediated clearance saturates → chylomicron/VLDL accumulation → hydrolysis in pancreatic capillaries generates cytotoxic free fatty acids → local pancreatic inflammation/injury

📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A patient's triglyceride level is 1,100 mg/dL. The primary immediate clinical concern is:

Explanation: Triglycerides above 500 mg/dL (especially beyond 1,000) saturate LPL-mediated clearance, causing chylomicron/VLDL accumulation and pancreatic capillary hydrolysis that generates cytotoxic free fatty acids — precipitating acute pancreatitis, the priority concern at this level.

2. Lipoprotein(a) is structurally unique among LDL particles because it contains which additional covalently linked protein?

Explanation: Lp(a) is an LDL particle with Apo(a) — a protein homologous to plasminogen — covalently attached to ApoB-100. This structural similarity to plasminogen is thought to impair fibrinolysis and promote both atherogenesis and thrombosis.

3. Which cell type internalizes oxidized LDL via scavenger receptors to form the lipid-laden foam cells characteristic of early atherosclerotic plaque?

Explanation: Monocytes that have migrated into the subendothelial space differentiate into macrophages, which internalize oxidized LDL through scavenger receptors (unregulated by feedback), becoming cholesterol-laden foam cells.

3. Non-Pharmacologic Management

Lifestyle modification is the cornerstone of dyslipidemia management for every patient — sufficient alone for mild abnormalities, and an essential adjunct even when drug therapy is required.

Dietary Goals

  • Reduce saturated fat to <7% of daily calories; eliminate trans fats
  • Limit dietary cholesterol to <200 mg/day
  • Replace saturated fats with mono- and polyunsaturated fats (olive oil, nuts, fish)
  • Increase soluble fiber (oats, legumes, fruits) — reduces LDL-C by interfering with bile acid reabsorption
  • Plant sterols/stanols (fortified foods) competitively inhibit intestinal cholesterol absorption → can lower LDL-C by 5–15%
  • Reduce total calories, avoid simple sugars (especially fructose), and minimize alcohol — all promote hepatic VLDL synthesis and worsen hypertriglyceridemia

Evidence-Based Dietary Patterns

PatternEmphasisEvidence
Mediterranean dietVegetables, whole grains, legumes, nuts, fish, olive oilReduces cardiovascular events even in high-risk populations
DASH dietFruits, vegetables, low-fat dairy; ↓sodium and saturated fatOriginally for blood pressure; also improves lipid profile

Other Lifestyle Interventions

  • Aerobic exercise ≥150 min/week — modestly ↑HDL-C, ↓TG, improves insulin sensitivity
  • Weight loss of even 5–10% body weight improves LDL-C, TG, and HDL-C
  • Smoking cessation — smoking lowers HDL-C, promotes LDL oxidation, and independently causes endothelial dysfunction
  • Alcohol moderation — especially important in hypertriglyceridemia, since alcohol stimulates hepatic triglyceride synthesis
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A patient adopts a diet rich in fortified foods containing plant sterols. Through which mechanism do these lower LDL-C?

Explanation: Plant sterols and stanols structurally resemble cholesterol and competitively inhibit its intestinal absorption, lowering LDL-C by roughly 5–15% when consumed regularly in fortified foods.

2. Which lifestyle factor most directly promotes hepatic VLDL synthesis and can worsen hypertriglyceridemia?

Explanation: Alcohol stimulates hepatic triglyceride synthesis and VLDL secretion; along with excess calories and simple sugars (especially fructose), it is a key dietary driver of hypertriglyceridemia and should be moderated.

3. Increasing soluble fiber intake (oats, legumes, fruits) lowers LDL-C primarily by which mechanism?

Explanation: Soluble fiber binds bile acids in the intestinal lumen, interfering with their reabsorption. This mimics (on a smaller scale) the mechanism of bile acid sequestrant drugs, forcing increased hepatic conversion of cholesterol to bile acids and upregulating LDL receptors.

4. HMG-CoA Reductase Inhibitors (Statins)

Statins are first-line pharmacologic therapy for dyslipidemia and the cornerstone of ASCVD risk reduction.

Mechanism & Effects

  • Competitive inhibitors of HMG-CoA reductase, the rate-limiting enzyme in hepatic cholesterol synthesis
  • ↓Intracellular cholesterol → upregulates hepatic LDL receptor expression → ↑clearance of circulating LDL
  • Pleiotropic effects: improve endothelial function, stabilize plaque, reduce vascular inflammation
  • Reduce LDL-C by ~20–60%; also lower triglycerides and modestly raise HDL-C

Statin Intensity Classification

IntensityExpected LDL-C ↓Example Regimens
High-intensity≥50%Atorvastatin 40–80 mg; rosuvastatin 20–40 mg
Moderate-intensity30–49%Atorvastatin 10–20 mg; rosuvastatin 5–10 mg; simvastatin 20–40 mg; pravastatin 40–80 mg
Low-intensity<30%Simvastatin 10 mg; pravastatin 10–20 mg (rarely used today)

Therapeutic Uses & Pharmacokinetics

  • Primary and secondary ASCVD prevention, familial hypercholesterolemia, mixed dyslipidemia with elevated TG
  • Most undergo significant first-pass hepatic uptake; best taken in the evening to align with nocturnal cholesterol synthesis — except atorvastatin, rosuvastatin, and pitavastatin (long half-lives, dosed any time)
  • Lovastatin and simvastatin are prodrugs with absorption enhanced by food
  • Many are CYP3A4 substrates (lovastatin, simvastatin, atorvastatin) — susceptible to interactions; pravastatin and rosuvastatin are not significantly CYP-metabolized

Adverse Effects, Contraindications & Interactions

  • Myopathy — myalgia to rhabdomyolysis; risk factors include high dose, interacting drugs, advanced age, genetic variants; usually resolves with discontinuation; rarely persists as immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy requiring immunosuppression. Rosuvastatin and pravastatin are often better tolerated in patients with prior statin myalgia
  • Hepatotoxicity — mild transaminase elevation is not itself a reason to stop unless persistently >3× ULN; routine LFT monitoring is not recommended (check if symptomatic)
  • Small increase in fasting glucose/new-onset diabetes risk — ASCVD benefit still outweighs this risk
  • Contraindicated in pregnancy and active liver disease; discontinue when planning pregnancy, avoid in lactation; may be held during serious illness/major surgery/trauma
  • Drug interactions: avoid red yeast rice (contains statin-like compounds); CYP3A4 inhibitors (macrolides, azole antifungals, grapefruit juice) raise levels of CYP3A4-metabolized statins → ↑myopathy risk; when combining with a fibrate, fenofibrate is preferred over gemfibrozil (lower myopathy risk)
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Which two statins have long half-lives that allow dosing at any time of day, unlike most other statins which should be taken in the evening?

Explanation: Atorvastatin, rosuvastatin, and pitavastatin have long half-lives, so unlike most statins (best taken in the evening to align with nocturnal cholesterol synthesis), they can be dosed at any time of day.

2. A patient on simvastatin is started on a macrolide antibiotic. The most important concern with this combination is:

Explanation: Simvastatin is a CYP3A4 substrate; macrolides (and other CYP3A4 inhibitors like azole antifungals or grapefruit juice) raise statin plasma levels, increasing the risk of myopathy up to rhabdomyolysis.

3. When combination therapy with a statin and a fibrate is necessary, which fibrate is preferred due to a lower risk of myopathy?

Explanation: Fenofibrate is preferred over gemfibrozil when combined with a statin because gemfibrozil inhibits hepatic OATP1B1-mediated statin uptake and competes for glucuronidation, raising statin levels and increasing rhabdomyolysis risk.

5. Cholesterol Absorption Inhibitor: Ezetimibe

Mechanism & Effects

  • Selectively inhibits the NPC1L1 transporter at the brush border of the small intestine
  • ↓Intestinal absorption of dietary and biliary cholesterol → ↓cholesterol incorporation into chylomicrons → ↓hepatic cholesterol delivery → compensatory upregulation of hepatic LDL receptors
  • Monotherapy: ↓LDL-C by ~18–20%, with minimal effect on HDL-C/TG

Therapeutic Uses

  • Adjunct to statin therapy for additional LDL-C lowering, or in patients who cannot tolerate high-intensity statins
  • Statin-intolerant patients, alone or combined with bempedoic acid
  • Statin + ezetimibe is synergistic: statins block synthesis, ezetimibe blocks absorption — complementary mechanisms
  • Fixed-dose combinations exist (ezetimibe/simvastatin, ezetimibe/bempedoic acid)

Pharmacokinetics & Adverse Effects

  • Half-life ~22 hours — once-daily dosing, with or without food
  • No significant CYP450 interactions — favorable safety profile with fewer drug-drug interactions than statins
  • Uncommon, generally mild adverse effects; mild transaminase elevation possible; myopathy rare but slightly more frequent when combined with a statin or fibrate

Drug Interactions & Contraindications

  • Fibrates increase ezetimibe plasma concentration
  • Bile acid sequestrants reduce ezetimibe absorption — avoid combining (impaired absorption)
  • Contraindicated in pregnancy and lactation as monotherapy; safety of the combination with a statin in pregnancy is not established and should be avoided
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Ezetimibe lowers LDL-C by inhibiting which specific transporter?

Explanation: Ezetimibe selectively inhibits NPC1L1, the transporter responsible for intestinal (and biliary) cholesterol absorption at the brush border of the small intestine.

2. Which combination should generally be avoided due to impaired absorption of one of the drugs?

Explanation: Bile acid sequestrants reduce ezetimibe absorption; this combination should generally be avoided, whereas fibrates actually increase ezetimibe plasma concentrations.

3. Why is the combination of a statin plus ezetimibe considered synergistic for LDL-C lowering?

Explanation: Statins reduce endogenous hepatic cholesterol synthesis while ezetimibe reduces intestinal cholesterol absorption — two distinct, complementary mechanisms that together produce greater LDL-C lowering than either alone.

6. PCSK9-Targeted Therapies

PCSK9 Monoclonal Antibodies: Alirocumab & Evolocumab

  • Fully human monoclonal antibodies that bind circulating PCSK9, a hepatic protease that normally binds LDL receptors on hepatocytes and directs them to lysosomal degradation
  • By inhibiting PCSK9, LDL receptor recycling and surface expression increase, enhancing clearance of circulating LDL-C
  • ↓LDL-C by ~45–60% when added to statin therapy; ~47–58% as monotherapy; up to 60–70% in FH or statin-intolerant patients
  • Also modestly reduce triglycerides and lipoprotein(a)

Therapeutic Uses & Pharmacokinetics

  • Heterozygous or homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia
  • Established ASCVD requiring additional LDL-C lowering despite maximally tolerated statin ± ezetimibe
  • Sometimes used in documented statin intolerance, though not currently FDA-approved for that indication without ASCVD
  • Administered subcutaneously every 2 weeks or monthly; high cost and need for refrigeration/injection limit use to high-risk populations

Adverse Effects & Contraindications

  • Most common: injection site reactions, nasopharyngitis, influenza-like symptoms; rare hypersensitivity
  • Do not increase myopathy or hepatotoxicity risk, and have no CYP450-mediated interactions — favorable in polypharmacy
  • Rare reports of neurocognitive effects (no established causal link; long-term data ongoing)
  • Contraindicated in pregnancy; not recommended in lactation (LDL receptor pathway is important in fetal development)

siRNA Therapy: Inclisiran

Inclisiran is a small interfering RNA (siRNA) that suppresses hepatic PCSK9 synthesis at the mRNA level — a fundamentally different mechanism than the antibodies, which block circulating PCSK9 protein.
  • Administered subcutaneously every 6 months — the major practical advantage over monthly/biweekly antibody dosing
  • Lowers LDL-C by ~50%; approved for heterozygous FH or ASCVD requiring additional LDL-C lowering
  • Well tolerated: injection site reactions and bronchitis reported infrequently; no significant drug interactions; no dose adjustment needed in mild-moderate renal/hepatic impairment
  • Contraindicated in pregnancy; not recommended in lactation
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. PCSK9 normally promotes degradation of which cellular structure, thereby reducing LDL clearance?

Explanation: PCSK9 binds LDL receptors on the hepatocyte surface and directs them toward lysosomal degradation. Inhibiting PCSK9 (with antibodies or siRNA) allows more LDL receptors to recycle back to the surface, enhancing LDL clearance.

2. What is the key mechanistic difference between inclisiran and alirocumab/evolocumab?

Explanation: Inclisiran is a siRNA that silences PCSK9 mRNA translation in the liver, reducing PCSK9 production at its source, whereas alirocumab and evolocumab are monoclonal antibodies that bind and neutralize PCSK9 protein already in circulation.

3. A major practical advantage of inclisiran over the PCSK9 monoclonal antibodies is:

Explanation: Inclisiran is dosed subcutaneously only once every 6 months, compared to the every-2-week or monthly dosing required for alirocumab and evolocumab — a significant adherence advantage, even though both classes remain costly and are contraindicated in pregnancy.

7. ATP-Citrate Lyase Inhibitor: Bempedoic Acid

Mechanism

  • Oral prodrug activated in the liver by very long-chain acyl-CoA synthetase 1 (ACSVL1) into bempedoic acid-CoA, its active form
  • Active metabolite inhibits ATP-citrate lyase (ACL), a cytosolic enzyme upstream of HMG-CoA reductase that converts citrate to acetyl-CoA (a precursor for both fatty acid and cholesterol synthesis)
  • ↓Hepatic cholesterol synthesis → ↑LDL receptor expression → enhanced LDL-C clearance — the same downstream effect as statins, but one step further upstream
Key distinguishing feature: ACSVL1 (the activating enzyme) is expressed only in the liver, not skeletal muscle — so bempedoic acid does not interfere with muscle cholesterol synthesis, giving it a lower risk of myopathy compared to statins.

Efficacy & Therapeutic Uses

  • Combined with ezetimibe: LDL-C reduction approaches ~30%
  • Triple therapy (statin + ezetimibe + bempedoic acid): LDL-C reduction up to ~65%
  • FDA-approved as add-on therapy for adults with heterozygous FH or established ASCVD requiring additional LDL-C lowering beyond maximally tolerated statins; also useful in statin-intolerant patients
  • Administered orally once daily, alone or as a fixed-dose combination with ezetimibe

Adverse Effects, Interactions & Contraindications

  • Hyperuricemia (may increase gout risk via competition for renal tubular transporter OAT2); small risk of tendon rupture
  • Mild elevations in serum creatine kinase, creatinine, and hepatic transaminases
  • Lower myopathy risk than statins, but caution when co-administered with higher doses of simvastatin or rosuvastatin (bempedoic acid can increase statin exposure)
  • Contraindicated in pregnancy (inhibition of cholesterol synthesis may adversely affect fetal development)
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Bempedoic acid's lower risk of myopathy compared to statins is best explained by which fact?

Explanation: Bempedoic acid is a prodrug activated by ACSVL1, an enzyme expressed only in the liver and not in skeletal muscle. Because the active drug is never generated in muscle tissue, it spares muscle cholesterol synthesis and carries a lower myopathy risk than statins.

2. Bempedoic acid inhibits ATP-citrate lyase, an enzyme positioned where relative to HMG-CoA reductase in the cholesterol synthesis pathway?

Explanation: ATP-citrate lyase converts citrate to acetyl-CoA, a step upstream of HMG-CoA reductase in the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway. Inhibiting it reduces the acetyl-CoA precursor pool, decreasing cholesterol synthesis similarly to statins but via an earlier step.

3. Which adverse effect should prompt monitoring in a patient with a history of gout who is started on bempedoic acid?

Explanation: Bempedoic acid can cause hyperuricemia by competing for the renal tubular transporter OAT2, which may increase the risk of gout flares in susceptible patients.

8. Niacin

Mechanism & Effects

  • Niacin (nicotinic acid, vitamin B3) at pharmacologic doses inhibits lipolysis in adipose tissue via GPR109A-mediated signaling → ↓free fatty acid flux to the liver → ↓hepatic VLDL synthesis
  • Also enhances lipoprotein lipase activity and delays HDL catabolism
  • Effects: ↓LDL-C and ↓TG, ↑HDL-C, and ↓lipoprotein(a)
  • Most effective available agent for raising HDL-C (up to 30–40%); lowers TG 35–50% at doses of 2–6 g/day; additional LDL-C lowering of ~25% at higher doses

The Outcomes Problem

Despite favorable lipid changes, large outcome trials showed no added cardiovascular benefit when niacin was combined with statins — leading the FDA to withdraw approval for that combination in 2016. Niacin is no longer recommended as adjunctive therapy in patients already on statins.

Adverse Effects

  • Flushing (prostaglandin D2-mediated) — the most frequent complaint; mitigated by low-dose aspirin 30 minutes before dosing, gradual titration, or extended-release formulations
  • Pruritus, acanthosis nigricans (uncommon); GI intolerance, especially at higher doses or with peptic ulcer disease
  • More serious: hepatotoxicity (especially with sustained-release formulations), hyperuricemia, hyperglycemia (insulin resistance)
  • Monitor: liver enzymes, fasting glucose, uric acid

Formulations & Current Role

  • Crystalline (immediate-release) and prescription extended-release niacin are FDA-approved for dyslipidemia
  • OTC sustained-release and "flush-free" formulations lack FDA approval, show variable efficacy, and carry higher hepatotoxicity risk at lipid-lowering doses
  • May still be considered in statin-intolerant patients with mixed hyperlipidemia or elevated Lp(a)
  • Maximum dose should not exceed 6 g/day; initiate low with meals, titrate gradually, and educate patients about expected flushing
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Why did the FDA withdraw approval for adding niacin to statin therapy in 2016?

Explanation: Large cardiovascular outcome trials showed that adding niacin to statin therapy improved lipid parameters (HDL-C, TG, LDL-C) but produced no additional reduction in cardiovascular events, leading the FDA to withdraw the combination indication in 2016.

2. Niacin-induced flushing is mediated by which substance, and how can it be mitigated?

Explanation: Niacin-induced flushing is mediated by prostaglandin D2 release; taking low-dose aspirin 30 minutes before the niacin dose, gradual titration, or using extended-release formulations can reduce this common and often treatment-limiting side effect.

3. Niacin remains a reasonable option in which of the following clinical scenarios?

Explanation: Given the lack of added cardiovascular benefit with statins and its adverse effect profile, niacin's remaining niche is in statin-intolerant patients with mixed hyperlipidemia or elevated lipoprotein(a) — not as routine statin adjunct therapy.

9. Fibrates

Mechanism & Effects

  • Gemfibrozil and fenofibrate are PPAR-α agonists that modulate lipid metabolism
  • Upregulate lipoprotein lipase (LPL) and enhance fatty acid oxidation in liver and muscle → increased clearance of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins (VLDL, chylomicrons)
  • Suppress ApoC-III (an inhibitor of lipolysis) and increase synthesis of ApoA-I and ApoA-II → modestly raise HDL-C

Therapeutic Uses

  • Primary indication: severe hypertriglyceridemia to reduce pancreatitis risk
  • Also used in dysbetalipoproteinemia and mixed hyperlipidemia
  • No consistent ASCVD benefit when added to statin therapy for most patients — FDA withdrew the fenofibrate add-on indication for ASCVD prevention in 2016; subgroup analyses suggest possible benefit in patients with type 2 diabetes and atherogenic dyslipidemia
  • Fibrates remain a cornerstone in managing chylomicronemia syndrome and severe hypertriglyceridemia

Choosing Between Fibrates

Fenofibrate is preferred over gemfibrozil when combined with a statin. Gemfibrozil inhibits hepatic statin uptake via OATP1B1 and competes for glucuronidation enzymes, raising statin levels and increasing rhabdomyolysis risk — gemfibrozil should generally be avoided with statins.

Pharmacokinetics, Adverse Effects & Interactions

  • Absorbed more efficiently with food; dosing adjustments required in renal impairment; avoid in significant hepatic or renal dysfunction
  • Fenofibrate may have some benefit in nonalcoholic fatty liver disease
  • Adverse effects: myopathy, cholesterol gallstones, GI upset, transaminase elevations, rare hematologic abnormalities; cholelithiasis risk higher in women, obese patients, and Native Americans
  • Potentiate warfarin by displacing it from albumin — requires INR monitoring and dose adjustment
  • Contraindicated in pregnancy, children, and pre-existing gallbladder disease
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Fibrates lower triglycerides primarily by which mechanism?

Explanation: As PPAR-alpha agonists, fibrates upregulate LPL activity and suppress ApoC-III (an LPL inhibitor), together enhancing clearance of triglyceride-rich VLDL and chylomicrons.

2. A patient on atorvastatin requires fibrate therapy for severe hypertriglyceridemia. Which fibrate is preferred, and why?

Explanation: Gemfibrozil inhibits OATP1B1-mediated hepatic statin uptake and competes for glucuronidation, raising statin plasma levels and rhabdomyolysis risk. Fenofibrate lacks this significant interaction and is preferred when a fibrate must be combined with a statin.

3. A patient taking warfarin is started on fenofibrate. What is the most important monitoring consideration?

Explanation: Fibrates displace warfarin from albumin-binding sites, potentiating its anticoagulant effect and increasing bleeding risk; INR should be monitored closely with warfarin dose adjustment as needed.

10. Bile Acid Binding Resins

Mechanism & Effects

  • Cholestyramine, colestipol, colesevelam are non-absorbable, positively charged anion-exchange resins
  • Bind negatively charged bile acids in the intestinal lumen, preventing reabsorption and increasing fecal bile acid excretion
  • Depletes the bile acid pool → stimulates hepatic conversion of cholesterol to bile acids via upregulation of cholesterol 7α-hydroxylase → reduces hepatic cholesterol stores → compensatory upregulation of hepatic LDL receptors
  • Reduce LDL-C by 15–25%; no effect in homozygous FH (patients lack functional LDL receptors to upregulate)

Therapeutic Uses

  • Mild-to-moderate LDL-C elevations, especially in statin-intolerant patients, children, and pregnant women where other agents may be contraindicated
  • Also occasionally leveraged to treat digitalis toxicity and cholestatic pruritus (bile salt accumulation)
  • Second-line therapy overall, particularly when statin use is limited

The Triglyceride Trap

Bile acid resins may stimulate increased hepatic VLDL production, potentially worsening triglyceride levels. They are contraindicated in severe hypertriglyceridemia and should be used cautiously with moderately elevated TG.

Adverse Effects & Drug Interactions

  • Constipation, bloating, dyspepsia, diarrhea (increase dietary fiber, hydrate); rare: folic acid malabsorption, gallstone formation, vitamin K deficiency → hypoprothrombinemia (monitor INR, especially with concurrent warfarin)
  • Impair absorption of many oral drugs: warfarin, digoxin, thiazides, levothyroxine, tetracyclines, statins (except pravastatin/rosuvastatin), iron, fat-soluble vitamins — take other medications at least 1 hour before or 3–4 hours after the resin
  • Contraindicated in biliary obstruction, chronic constipation, diverticulitis, and severe hypertriglyceridemia

Colesevelam — the Newer, Better-Tolerated Option

  • Better tolerated; does not significantly bind concomitant oral agents — preferred in patients with polypharmacy
  • Modestly improves glycemic control in type 2 diabetes by increasing GLP-1 secretion
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Bile acid sequestrants would be expected to have minimal LDL-lowering effect in a patient with which condition?

Explanation: Bile acid sequestrants work by upregulating hepatic LDL receptors in response to depleted bile acid/cholesterol stores. Patients with homozygous FH lack functional LDL receptors, so this compensatory upregulation cannot occur and the drugs are ineffective.

2. Which patient population is bile acid sequestrant therapy particularly well suited for, given the safety profiles of other lipid-lowering agents?

Explanation: Because they are not systemically absorbed, bile acid sequestrants are a preferred option in pregnancy (and in children and statin-intolerant patients) where other lipid-lowering therapies may be contraindicated or unstudied.

3. A patient taking cholestyramine and levothyroxine together should be counseled to:

Explanation: Bile acid resins impair absorption of many oral drugs, including levothyroxine. Other medications should be taken at least 1 hour before or 3–4 hours after the resin dose to avoid reduced absorption.

11. HoFH Agents & Omega-3 Fatty Acids

Lomitapide (MTP Inhibitor)

  • Oral inhibitor of microsomal triglyceride transfer protein (MTP), essential for assembling triglycerides into VLDL (liver) and chylomicrons (intestine)
  • Reduces VLDL and LDL production — lowers LDL-C by up to 50% in homozygous FH, as an adjunct to maximally tolerated statin therapy
  • Must be taken ≥2 hours after food or on an empty stomach; requires a strict low-fat diet and fat-soluble vitamin supplementation/monitoring
  • Significant hepatotoxicity risk (elevated LFTs, hepatic steatosis) — requires regular liver monitoring and is only available through an FDA REMS program
  • Metabolized by CYP3A4 — contraindicated with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors; embryotoxic — negative pregnancy test required before starting, effective contraception throughout therapy

Evinacumab (ANGPTL3 Inhibitor)

  • Fully human monoclonal IgG4 antibody that inhibits ANGPTL3, a hepatic protein that normally inhibits both lipoprotein lipase and endothelial lipase
  • By neutralizing ANGPTL3, evinacumab enhances LPL and endothelial lipase activity → reduces triglycerides, LDL-C, and HDL-C
  • LDL-C reduction is independent of the LDL receptor pathway — particularly valuable in HoFH patients with absent/severely defective LDL receptors
  • FDA-approved as adjunct for HoFH; reduces LDL-C by ~47% when added to maximal standard therapy (including statins, ezetimibe, PCSK9 inhibitors, lomitapide, apheresis)
  • Administered as an IV infusion every 4 weeks; half-life ~17–25 days; metabolized via nonspecific proteolysis
  • Adverse effects: hypersensitivity reactions, embryo-fetal toxicity (contraindicated in pregnancy); common: nasopharyngitis, influenza-like illness, dizziness, nausea; no clinically significant drug interactions identified

Omega-3 Fatty Acids

  • Primarily EPA and DHA; icosapent ethyl is a highly purified EPA-only formulation; OTC fish oil varies widely in EPA/DHA content and dose
  • Mechanism: reduce VLDL triglyceride synthesis/secretion by decreasing fatty acid substrate availability and limiting lipolysis
  • Used as adjunctive therapy for severe hypertriglyceridemia to reduce pancreatitis risk (icosapent ethyl FDA-approved at 4 g/day total, divided doses with food)
  • Icosapent ethyl has demonstrated cardiovascular benefit — guidelines recommend it in diabetic/high-risk patients with persistently elevated TG despite statin therapy
EPA vs. DHA: DHA-containing products may modestly increase LDL-C; EPA-only formulations like icosapent ethyl typically do not raise LDL-C significantly — a key distinguishing point.
  • Adverse effects: nausea, dyspepsia, fishy aftertaste, arthralgia; can prolong bleeding time (monitor with anticoagulants); icosapent ethyl associated with a slight increase in atrial fibrillation/flutter
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Evinacumab lowers LDL-C through a mechanism that is notable for being independent of which pathway, making it uniquely useful in homozygous FH?

Explanation: By inhibiting ANGPTL3, evinacumab enhances lipoprotein lipase and endothelial lipase activity, clearing triglyceride-rich lipoproteins upstream of LDL formation independent of hepatic LDL receptors — crucial for HoFH patients with absent or defective LDL receptors.

2. A patient with homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia is prescribed lomitapide. Which monitoring/safety measure is essential given its major risk?

Explanation: Lomitapide carries a significant hepatotoxicity risk (elevated liver enzymes, hepatic steatosis), requiring regular liver function monitoring; it is available only through an FDA REMS program for this reason.

3. Compared to DHA-containing omega-3 formulations, EPA-only formulations like icosapent ethyl have which distinguishing lipid effect?

Explanation: DHA-containing omega-3 products can modestly increase LDL-C, while EPA-only formulations such as icosapent ethyl typically do not raise LDL-C significantly — an important distinction when selecting a triglyceride-lowering omega-3 product.

12. 2026 Guidelines & Drug Selection

What Changed in 2026

  • Lifestyle modification remains foundational; statins remain first-line pharmacologic therapy
  • PREVENT-ASCVD equations replace the older pooled cohort equations for primary prevention — estimate both 10-year and longer-term risk, beginning at age 30
  • Greater emphasis on identifying additional risk: measure lipoprotein(a) at least once in adulthood; selectively use ApoB and coronary artery calcium (CAC) scoring to refine decisions

Escalation Pathway

Statins remain first-line. When statin therapy alone does not achieve the LDL-C goal, add ezetimibe, bempedoic acid, or a PCSK9-targeted therapy based on the patient's risk and treatment needs — in that general order of typical practice, though selection is individualized.

Severe/Persistent Hypertriglyceridemia

  • Statins remain foundational for overall ASCVD risk reduction even when TG is elevated
  • Triglyceride-lowering therapy (fibrates, prescription omega-3s) becomes a priority specifically to prevent pancreatitis when TG is very high (especially ≥1,000 mg/dL)

Pediatric & Pregnancy Considerations

  • Drug treatment generally avoided in pregnancy, lactation, and children
  • Pharmacologic therapy may be initiated for familial hypercholesterolemia typically after age 8, depending on LDL-C level and other risk factors
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. According to the 2026 guideline framework, a patient with a calculated 10-year PREVENT-ASCVD risk of 7% should generally:

Explanation: A 10-year risk of 5% to <10% falls into the range where the guidelines recommend initiating lipid-lowering therapy (as opposed to the 3-<5% range, where therapy is only 'considered').

2. A patient has established ASCVD and is classified as very-high-risk. What is the target LDL-C goal under the 2026 guidelines?

Explanation: Very-high-risk patients with established ASCVD have the most stringent LDL-C target of <55 mg/dL, compared to <70 mg/dL for high-risk primary prevention and <100 mg/dL for borderline/intermediate-risk primary prevention.

3. A patient on statin therapy has persistently very high triglycerides (>1,000 mg/dL). According to current guidance, the treatment priority should shift toward:

Explanation: When triglycerides are very high (especially ≥ 1,000 mg/dL), the immediate priority shifts to triglyceride-lowering therapy (fibrates, prescription omega-3 fatty acids) to reduce the risk of acute pancreatitis, while statins remain in place for overall ASCVD risk reduction. Bile acid sequestrants would be contraindicated here since they can worsen hypertriglyceridemia.

The Core Take-Home Logic

Predominant LDL-C elevation → statin first → add ezetimibe → add bempedoic acid or a PCSK9-targeted therapy.
Predominant severe hypertriglyceridemia → statin remains foundational for ASCVD risk, but add a fibrate or prescription omega-3 (icosapent ethyl) specifically to prevent pancreatitis.
HoFH unresponsive to standard therapy → consider lomitapide (MTP inhibitor) or evinacumab (ANGPTL3 inhibitor), both of which work even without functional LDL receptors.
Statin-intolerant → ezetimibe, bempedoic acid, PCSK9-targeted therapy, or bile acid sequestrant, depending on the predominant lipid abnormality.
