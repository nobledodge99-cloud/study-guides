1. Why Order a Lab Test?

Before reflexively ordering a test, Dr. Wilde frames five questions every clinician should be able to answer. If you can't answer these, step back and learn about the test before using it routinely:

  1. Why is the test being ordered?
  2. What are the consequences of not ordering the test?
  3. How good is this test at discriminating between health and disease?
  4. How are the results interpreted?
  5. How will the results influence patient management and outcome?

Four Sore-Throat Cases: Same Presentation, Different Testing Decisions

Case 1 — 13yo boy Rapid strep negative, no formal culture sent, no antibiotics given. One month later: rheumatic fever with polyarthritis, weight loss, and heart failure requiring two valve replacements. Lesson: the rapid strep antigen test only detects Group A strep — a negative rapid test still requires a throat culture to catch other pathogenic strep, or true disease can be missed entirely.
Case 2 — 15yo girl Rapid strep negative, culture sent and returned positive for Group C strep three days later. Started on amoxicillin, full recovery — just a few days' delay in appropriate treatment.
Case 3 — 17yo boy Rapid strep negative, culture sent (also negative), started empirically on amoxicillin for a "presumed strep" picture that was actually mononucleosis. Amoxicillin (aminopenicillin) in a patient with mono classically triggers a diffuse morbilliform rash — even though he had tolerated amoxicillin before.
Case 4 — 16yo girl Rapid strep positive, treated appropriately with amoxicillin — but died overnight from a rapidly progressive retropharyngeal abscess that compromised her airway. Lesson: correct testing and correct treatment don't guarantee a good outcome — disease can progress faster than the interval between visits allows for.

Four Primary Reasons to Order a Lab Test

  • Screening — identify potential disease in asymptomatic individuals, often based on risk factors, exposures, or family history.
  • Diagnosis — confirm or rule out disease in a symptomatic patient.
  • Monitoring — assess treatment efficacy, evaluate for side effects, track disease progression.
  • Research — better understand disease mechanisms and treatment options.
Pearl: the same test can serve different purposes in different contexts — hemoglobin A1c was originally approved only for monitoring known diabetics but is now also used as a screening tool.
Section 1

Q1. A 16-year-old male has a negative rapid antigen strep test but no throat culture is sent, and he is not treated. One month later he develops migratory polyarthritis and new heart failure. This outcome is best explained by which limitation of the test used?

A negative rapid antigen test only rules out Group A strep with reasonable confidence — it does not detect other strep species and, in a clinically concerning picture, should be backed up with a throat culture. Missing that step here allowed untreated Group A strep to progress to rheumatic fever.
Section 1

Q2. A patient with an unremarkable exam requests a colon cancer screening test as part of a routine wellness visit — she has no GI symptoms. Which of the five pre-test questions from this lecture is LEAST relevant to this specific decision?

The lecture's five pre-ordering questions are all clinically oriented (purpose, consequences of omission, discriminatory power, interpretation, and impact on management) — none of them concern the technical laboratory methodology itself, which is a separate consideration from clinical test selection.

2. Screening vs. Diagnostic Tests

Screening tests identify potential disease in asymptomatic people. Diagnostic tests confirm or rule out disease in symptomatic people, or follow up an abnormal screening result.

Section 2

Q3. A 58-year-old asymptomatic man's PSA rose from 1.0 ng/mL to 2.0 ng/mL over one year — both values fall within the "normal" reference range for his age. What is the most appropriate next step?

Even when the absolute PSA remains within the normal range, a doubling within one year (PSA velocity) raises concern for malignancy and warrants further workup — the rate of change matters as much as the number itself.
Section 2

Q4. A 45-year-old asymptomatic woman undergoes a screening mammogram that shows a suspicious mass. Which of the following best describes the next test in this sequence?

Once a screening test is abnormal, the patient is no longer "asymptomatic/average risk" for that finding — subsequent breast ultrasound, diagnostic mammography, and biopsy are diagnostic tests aimed at confirming or excluding disease.

3. Reading a Real Lab Panel

The lecture walks through actual patient panels to show how the same tests serve different purposes depending on context.

64-Year-Old Male

  • Basic Metabolic Panel (BMP): fasting glucose, BUN/creatinine, GFR, electrolytes — assesses renal function and bone/electrolyte status.
  • Lipid panel: patient already had diagnosed hyperlipidemia and was on a statin — this panel is monitoring how well the medication is controlling cholesterol, not screening.
  • Hepatic function panel: ordered alongside the statin to make sure the medication isn't causing hepatotoxicity — again, monitoring.
  • Hemoglobin A1c: 5.7% — his first-ever above-normal value, falling in the prediabetes range. Management: lifestyle modification, recheck in ~3 months to assess progression.
  • PSA: 4.4 ng/mL — just above the "high" cutoff, but this had been rising slowly (0.1–0.2/year) rather than jumping suddenly, which is reassuring. He also had a digital rectal exam (increases sensitivity of the workup). Plan: refer to urology for ongoing monitoring given the now-abnormal value.
  • Vitamin D and CRP: obtained but are not routine screening tests, so they are often not covered by insurance — the physician should flag potential out-of-pocket cost to the patient before ordering.

48-Year-Old Female

BMP, lipid panel, and HbA1c were obtained on an obese patient with multiple cardiovascular risk factors. She was started on a cholesterol-lowering medication and on metformin for prediabetes because of her risk profile and likelihood of progression — an example of using labs to guide preventive management, not just to label a diagnosis.

37-Year-Old Female

TSH with free T4/T3 obtained for ongoing diagnosis and monitoring of thyroid treatment — an example of the same panel serving both a diagnostic and a monitoring role over time.

Section 3

Q5. A 60-year-old man on a statin for known hyperlipidemia has a lipid panel and hepatic function panel ordered at his follow-up visit. What is the primary purpose of these tests in this context?

In a patient with an established diagnosis already on treatment, repeat lipid and hepatic panels serve to monitor how well the statin is controlling cholesterol and to watch for hepatotoxicity — this is monitoring, not screening or new diagnosis.
Section 3

Q6. A patient's hemoglobin A1c returns at 5.7% for the first time, with no prior abnormal values. What does this result indicate and what is the appropriate next step?

An HbA1c of 5.7% falls into the prediabetes range as presented in this lecture's case. The appropriate initial management is lifestyle modification with a recheck in roughly three months to assess progression, not immediate pharmacologic therapy.

4. Shotgun vs. Rifle: Test Selection

Case A 4-year-old is brought to the ED with an upper respiratory infection, a fever of 102.2°F, and a generalized seizure lasting ~2 minutes.

Shotgun Approach

Ordering everything available (blood draw, arterial stick, nasal swab, throat swab, stool test, PPD, x-rays, EKG, etc.) without a clear differential — "casting a wide net." May be justified in some contexts, but tests should be driven by history, physical exam, and differential diagnosis, not reflexive over-ordering.

Rifle Approach

If exam reveals nuchal rigidity and severe photophobia in addition to fever and seizure, concern shifts specifically to meningitis. A targeted lumbar puncture is performed, analyzing cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) for:

  • White blood cells, red blood cells
  • Total protein and glucose
  • Gram stain
  • Cultures for bacteria, virus, fungi
  • Rapid PCR for meningococcal sequence
Pearl: before every workup ask yourself — am I doing a shotgun approach because I genuinely don't know what's going on, and if so, will I actually be able to interpret every result I get back? Go back to the five pre-ordering questions from Section 1.
Section 4

Q7. A 4-year-old with fever and a generalized seizure is found on exam to have nuchal rigidity and photophobia. Which single test set represents the most appropriate "rifle" approach?

Nuchal rigidity and photophobia point specifically toward meningitis, so the targeted (rifle) workup is a lumbar puncture with full CSF analysis — this directly tests the leading diagnosis rather than casting a wide, undirected net.
Section 4

Q8. Before ordering a broad panel of tests on a patient with a vague presentation, the lecture recommends the clinician ask which key self-check?

The lecture's caution about the shotgun approach centers on whether the ordering clinician can meaningfully interpret every result obtained — ordering a test you can't interpret doesn't help the patient and may generate confusing incidental findings.

5. Properties of a Useful Diagnostic Test

Validity = does the test actually indicate who has the disease and who doesn't (is it telling you what you need to know)? Reliability = consistency — does repeating the test give the same result each time?

Four Properties

  1. Reproducibility: methodology is described in enough detail to be reliably reproduced across different sites, temperatures, and settings.
  2. Accuracy and precision established: accuracy = hitting the true center of the target (correctness); precision = hitting the same spot every time (consistency). A test can be accurate but imprecise (right on average, scattered results) or precise but inaccurate (consistently wrong).
  3. Reference interval appropriately established: a reference interval captures ~95% of a healthy population — meaning ~5% of healthy people will still fall outside "normal" limits and show an abnormal result despite being disease-free.
  4. Sensitivity and specificity reliably established against a gold standard: no test is 100% sensitive or 100% specific — a cutoff must be chosen balancing the two.
Section 5

Q9. A lab test consistently returns the same numeric value of 8.2 on repeated runs of the same sample, but the true value is actually 6.0. Which property does this test demonstrate?

Getting the same wrong answer every time is the classic definition of precise-but-inaccurate: reliability (consistency) is high, but validity (correctness) is poor.
Section 5

Q10. A reference interval is defined to capture 95% of a healthy population. What does this imply about a healthy patient's chance of an "abnormal" result on any single test?

Because the reference range is constructed to encompass 95% of healthy individuals, by definition about 5% of healthy people will fall outside it and receive an "abnormal" result despite having no disease.

6. Incidence & Prevalence: the Bathtub Analogy

Incidence = number of new cases of a disease/condition in a specific time period (usually a year), divided by the population initially disease-free. It's a direct measure of disease risk — usually reported per 100,000 people.

Prevalence = the total number of cases alive with the disease, either over a period (period prevalence — better reflects overall disease burden, since it captures both new cases and deaths between two dates) or at one moment (point prevalence — only counts those currently alive with disease). Usually reported as a fraction of the total population.

The Bathtub

  • The bathtub = the total population being assessed.
  • The water in the tub = prevalence (how full the tub is right now).
  • The faucet dripping in = incidence (new cases entering, plus recurrences).
  • The drain = ways out of the disease state: recovery/cure, or death.
Applying the Bathtub to Real Diseases
ConditionIncidencePrevalenceWhy
InfluenzaHighLowVery contagious (fills fast) but high cure rate, low mortality (drains fast too)
EbolaHighLowHighly contagious but almost universally fatal — the water leaves via the drain (death) almost as fast as it enters
Alzheimer's diseaseLowHighRelatively uncommon new-onset rate, but no cure and not highly fatal on its own — cases accumulate over time (tub stays full, drains slowly)
Section 6

Q11. Using the bathtub analogy, a disease with a very high cure rate and low mortality, but that is extremely contagious, would be expected to show which incidence/prevalence pattern?

Like influenza — the "faucet" runs fast (high incidence, very contagious) but the "drain" runs just as fast because most people recover quickly and few die, so the "tub" (prevalence) never gets very full.
Section 6

Q12. Which measure best reflects the overall disease burden or "load" carried by a community over the course of a year, since it captures both new cases and those who died during that period?

Period prevalence spans two dates and includes everyone who had the disease during that window, including people who subsequently died — giving a fuller picture of community disease burden than a single-moment point prevalence.

7. Sensitivity & Specificity

Sensitivity = the proportion of people with the disease who test positive (probability that, if disease is present, the test is positive). Highly sensitive tests are used for screening — cast a broad net so you don't miss anyone — at the cost of more false positives.

Specificity = the proportion of people without the disease who test negative (probability that, if disease is absent, the test is negative). Highly specific tests are used to confirm a positive screening result.

Mnemonics: SnNout — a highly Snensitive test, when Negative, rules out disease. SpPin — a highly Specific test, when Positive, rules in disease.

The 2×2 Table

Disease PresentDisease Absent
Test PositiveTrue Positive (TP)False Positive (FP)
Test NegativeFalse Negative (FN)True Negative (TN)

Sensitivity is also called the true positive rate; specificity is the true negative rate.

Sensitivity = TP / (TP + FN)
Specificity = TN / (TN + FP)

No test is 100% sensitive or 100% specific — shifting a diagnostic cutoff to increase sensitivity (fewer false negatives) always increases false positives, and vice versa; there is always a trade-off.

Section 7

Q13. A highly sensitive screening test returns a negative result in a patient with a low pre-test probability of disease. What is the most appropriate interpretation?

By the SnNout rule, a highly sensitive test that comes back negative is good at ruling disease out, because a sensitive test rarely misses true disease (few false negatives).
Section 7

Q14. In a study of 200 patients with confirmed pneumonia and 300 without, a chest imaging test correctly identifies 180 of the pneumonia patients as positive. What is the sensitivity of this test?

Sensitivity = TP / (TP + FN) = 180 / 200 = 90%. Only the diseased population (200 patients) is used in this calculation — the 300 disease-free patients are irrelevant to sensitivity.
Section 7

Q15. A car alarm is set to trigger at the slightest vibration, going off even when a pedestrian brushes past the car. This scenario is analogous to which test property in the lecture?

The car alarm analogy illustrates that a very high sensitivity setting catches every possible signal (never misses a real burglar) but at the cost of frequent false alarms — the same trade-off as a highly sensitive lab test.

8. PPV, NPV & Likelihood Ratios

Sensitivity and specificity describe the test itself and guide which test to order. Predictive values describe how to interpret a result you already have in a specific patient.

Positive Predictive Value (PPV) = TP / (TP + FP)
Negative Predictive Value (NPV) = TN / (TN + FN)

PPV = probability that a positive test correctly indicates disease. NPV = probability that a negative test correctly indicates absence of disease.

Prevalence drives PPV/NPV: higher disease prevalence increases PPV and decreases NPV (and vice versa) — even though sensitivity and specificity themselves do not change with prevalence. Example: a D-dimer has a high NPV and is excellent for ruling out pulmonary embolism when negative, but a positive D-dimer is much less useful on its own, since many conditions besides PE can elevate it.

Likelihood Ratios

The likelihood ratio (LR) combines a test's sensitivity and specificity with its result to update the pre-test probability of disease into a post-test probability — and unlike PPV/NPV, it is not affected by prevalence, which is its main utility.

  • LR 0–1: disease is unlikely
  • LR > 1: disease is more likely
  • LR = 1: equivocal, not helpful
Section 8

Q16. A D-dimer test is ordered in a low-risk patient with suspected pulmonary embolism and returns negative. Which statement best reflects the correct interpretation?

D-dimer's strength is its high NPV — a negative result is reassuring for ruling out PE. Its low specificity means a positive result is far less useful, since many non-PE conditions can also elevate D-dimer.
Section 8

Q17. As disease prevalence in a population increases, what happens to a test's positive predictive value and negative predictive value, assuming sensitivity and specificity stay constant?

Because more of the tested population truly has disease as prevalence rises, positive results are more likely to be true positives (PPV rises), while negative results are more likely to include a missed true case (NPV falls).
Section 8

Q18. A clinician wants a test-result metric that will not be distorted by moving between a low-prevalence outpatient clinic and a high-prevalence referral center. Which metric should they rely on?

Unlike PPV and NPV, the likelihood ratio is not affected by disease prevalence, making it a stable way to update pre-test probability across settings with different baseline disease rates.

9. Putting It Together: HIV Screening

Case 100 people are tested for HIV using ELISA — a test that is very sensitive but not very specific. Everyone who tests positive on ELISA is then retested with a more specific confirmatory test (historically Western blot, now more sensitive gold-standard confirmatory assays), which almost never falsely shows HIV. Patients with a false-positive ELISA can then be confidently reassured they do not have HIV because of the accuracy of the confirmatory test.

Why not just use the specific test first? The specific/confirmatory test usually comes with higher cost, higher risk, and more limited access, while the sensitive screening test is cheap, low-risk, and widely available (e.g., in clinics, remote settings). This mirrors why we don't perform colposcopy or cardiac catheterization on everyone as a first-line screen — we screen broadly with a sensitive, low-risk test, then confirm narrowly with a specific test only in those who screen positive.

Clinical caution: never diagnose and disclose a serious disease ("you have HIV," "you have cancer") based on the initial sensitive screening test alone — confirmatory testing must occur first.
Section 9

Q19. Why is a highly sensitive but less specific test (like ELISA) typically used as the first-line screening tool rather than going straight to the highly specific confirmatory test?

The two-tier screen-then-confirm strategy exists because sensitive screening tests are cheap, low-risk, and widely accessible, while highly specific confirmatory tests tend to be costlier, riskier, or less available — reserving the confirmatory test for the smaller group who screen positive is more efficient.
Section 9

Q20. A patient screens positive on an initial sensitive ELISA test for HIV. What is the most appropriate next step before informing the patient of a diagnosis?

Because the initial screen is sensitive but not very specific, a positive result must be confirmed with a highly specific test before disclosing a diagnosis, since a meaningful fraction of initial positives will be false positives.
