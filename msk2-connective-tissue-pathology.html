Connective Tissue Diseases — The Big Picture

Over 200 diagnoses, split into two broad groups

Connective tissue diseases cause inflammation, damage, or structural issues in the tissues that support the body (tendons, ligaments, cartilage, blood vessels, and other structures connecting/supporting organs). They break into two broad categories:

GroupMechanism
Autoimmune disorders (Part 2 of this series)The immune system mistakenly attacks its own connective tissues
Genetic/inherited disorders (Part 1 — this section)Mutations in genes affecting the structure, production, or function of connective tissue
Framing for this half of the seriesPart 1 covers four genetic/inherited connective tissue disorders in detail — Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, Marfan syndrome, Stickler syndrome, and osteogenesis imperfecta — plus a quick-reference table of three additional genetic CTDs (Loeys-Dietz, epidermolysis bullosa, pseudoxanthoma elasticum) at the end.

01 · Ehlers-Danlos Syndrome (EDS) — Overview

EDS — Subtypes & Epidemiology

13 subtypes, but two account for the vast majority of cases
  • 13 subtypes total; common symptoms include hypermobile joints, fragile/stretchy skin, easy bruising, chronic pain, and vascular complications
  • Classic and hypermobile EDS together account for over 90% of all EDS cases
  • ¼ of all EDS patients show aortic aneurysmal disease
  • Vascular EDS (vEDS) represents less than 5% of cases — but is disproportionately responsible for mortality
  • Caused by mutations in genes coding for collagen fibrils, or for proteins involved in processing those collagens — mutation in at least 20 different genes can produce an EDS phenotype
  • Incidence of vascular pathology increases with age; most EDS-related deaths are tied to vascular EDS via arterial rupture
  • Affects all races and ethnicities worldwide
Genetics vs. observed epidemiology — a real discrepancy worth understandingSince most EDS subtypes (including hypermobile EDS) are autosomal dominant, a 1:1 male:female ratio would be expected genetically. In practice, diagnosed cases show a strong female bias — attributed to factors like hormonal influences (estrogen affecting connective tissue laxity/symptom severity), differences in pain perception and symptom reporting, diagnostic bias, and underdiagnosis in males (who may have milder or different presentations). It's also likely that overall EDS incidence is underestimated, since patients with only minimal joint/skin involvement may never seek care in the first place.

02 · EDS History & Physical, and the Beighton Criteria

EDS — History & Physical

Two findings dominate the presentation, and there is no confirmatory lab test
  • Skin hyperextensibility and hyperflexible joints are the two most common presenting signs of EDS
  • Skin: smooth and velvet-like texture, fragility, delayed wound healing, and thin atrophic scars after healing
  • Shoulder dislocation is usually the initial sign of EDS — these dislocations are predominantly atraumatic (or minimal-force), often occurring during daily activities or even sleep, and are frequently painless
  • No supportive laboratory test exists — this is a clinical (and genetic) diagnosis
  • Mental development is generally normal
  • Dental pathologies are commonly observed: hypodontia of permanent teeth (1–6 teeth fail to develop), delayed eruption, and dentin dysplasia; a lack of attached gingiva can be a pathognomonic feature
  • A baseline echocardiogram is recommended for children diagnosed under age 10
  • If EDS is suspected: CT, MRI, and echocardiography can evaluate for cardiovascular concerns (mitral valve prolapse, aortic dilatation); MRI may also show white matter lesions consistent with EDS
Skin exam findings, in detailThe skin is usually white and soft, with underlying vessels sometimes visible. It has a doughy feel, is easily hyperextensible, and immediately returns to its original state after release. Molluscoid pseudotumors — small, spongy outgrowths over scars and pressure points — are commonly found in classic (type I) EDS. Smaller, deep, movable subcutaneous nodules (spherules) are often palpable on the arms and over the tibia and may calcify on radiography. Skin fragility with frequent bruises/lacerations is common, and joint hyperextensibility is variable in degree, most commonly affecting the digital joints.
Piezogenic papules — a distinctive EDS foot findingSubcutaneous fat herniating through the plantar fascia produces blanching white nodules on the sides of the feet that appear only while bearing weight — piezogenic papules.

Beighton Criteria

A quick, low-cost bedside screen for generalized joint hypermobility — not a substitute for genetic testing
  • A 9-point screening tool that assesses generalized joint hypermobility (GJH) using five movements, suggestive of EDS
  • Genetic testing remains the gold standard for diagnosis — but documenting the Beighton score is still worthwhile, including to support insurance payment for further work-up
What the five maneuvers assessThe classic Beighton exam scores (1 point each, bilateral items scored per side, for a maximum of 9): passive dorsiflexion of the pinky finger beyond 90°, passive apposition of the thumb to the flexor forearm, hyperextension of the elbow beyond 10°, hyperextension of the knee beyond 10°, and forward flexion of the trunk with knees straight so palms rest flat on the floor (this last item is scored once, not per side).

03 · EDS Complications & Treatment

Complications

Vascular and organ rupture are the most feared — and can occur without any preceding trauma
  • Vascular and organ rupture — the two most feared and mortal complications of EDS. Most common in the vascular and kyphoscoliotic subtypes, but reported in more common subtypes too. Vascular rupture can occur anywhere arterially, most often in the thorax and abdomen. Organ rupture most often involves the gravid uterus, sigmoid colon, spleen, and liver.
  • Mitral valve prolapse, with or without regurgitation (severe regurgitation is less common, but can become clinically significant under physiologic stress such as pregnancy, presenting with CHF/pulmonary edema)
  • Pregnancy complications: increased risk of cervical incompetence, premature/precipitous labor, significant vaginal/perineal lacerations with vaginal birth, premature rupture of membranes, breech presentation, uterine/bladder prolapse, uterine rupture, and great vessel rupture
  • Surgical interventions carry significant risk from vessel/organ fragility and prolonged healing time, plus increased bleeding risk from capillary fragility
  • Low blood pressure or POTS (postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome)
  • Spontaneous pneumothorax
  • Sleep disturbance and sleep-disordered breathing
Key clinical ruleSudden-onset pain in a patient with suspected or known EDS should prompt emergent medical evaluation regardless of the presence or absence of trauma — vascular/organ rupture can occur spontaneously.

Treatment / Management

  • Multidisciplinary approach focused on preventing disease progression and subsequent complications
  • Thorough, regular/frequent, comprehensive health surveillance
  • Pregnant EDS patients should be followed and managed by a high-risk OB/GYN
  • Regular cardiovascular screening (echocardiography for aortic root and mitral valve abnormalities) to catch structural issues that may need surgical intervention before rupture or heart failure occurs
  • Hypertension should be managed aggressively — it adds strain to already-fragile vasculature and increases complication risk over time

04 · Marfan Syndrome (MFS) — Overview & Cardiology

Marfan Syndrome — Overview

A fibrillin-1 disorder, not a collagen disorder — the outlier of the four genetic CTDs in this lecture
  • Primarily impacts the heart, blood vessels, eyes, bones, and joints, and sometimes the lungs and skin
  • Mutations in the FBN1 gene (chromosome 15), which affects the fibrillin-1 protein essential for connective tissue elasticity and strength
  • Prevalence: 1 in 3,000 to 5,000 individuals
  • Clinical findings and genetic testing are the only diagnostic determinants
Inheritance detail worth rememberingMFS is autosomal dominant — a person needs only one copy of the mutated FBN1 gene to develop the condition. However, in about 25% of cases the mutation occurs spontaneously, with no family history at all.

MFS and Cardiology

The aorta drives both morbidity and mortality — and the complication is preventable with timely surgery
  • Echocardiogram recommended at diagnosis and again at 6 months
  • Aortic root disease (aortic regurgitation, aneurysmal dilatation, dissection) is the primary cause of morbidity and mortality in MFS, in up to 60–80% of patients
  • MFS causes an aortic dissection in 50% of patients under age 40 worldwide
  • Aortic dissection and rupture are preventable — via prophylactic replacement of the ascending aorta, recommended once the aortic sinus diameter reaches 5.0 cm
  • 40–54% will have mitral valve prolapse (frequency increases with age, more prevalent in women); the tricuspid valve is affected in a small percentage
Number to know coldProphylactic ascending aortic root replacement is recommended once the aortic sinus diameter reaches 5.0 cm — this single threshold is what actually prevents the leading cause of death in MFS.

05 · Marfan Syndrome — Orthopedics

MFS — Musculoskeletal Findings

Overgrowth and laxity dominate — but a few joints are paradoxically stiff
  • Excess linear growth of the long bones and joint laxity — patients are taller than the general population with disproportionately long extremities relative to trunk length
  • Positive thumb sign and wrist sign
  • Pectus carinatum or pectus excavatum
  • Severe hindfoot valgus
  • Scoliosis
  • Acetabular protrusion (protrusio acetabuli)
Counterintuitive pointDespite the overall picture of joint laxity, some MFS patients have reduced joint mobility, particularly of the elbow and digits — hypermobility in MFS is not uniform across every joint.
Scoliosis in MFS — different behavior than idiopathic scoliosis50–63% of MFS patients develop some degree of scoliosis, often early in childhood/adolescence, sometimes with atypical curve patterns (e.g., double major curves) that can progress more rapidly. Only about one-third typically require intervention (bracing or surgery). Critically, MFS scoliosis is less responsive to bracing — a brace is only used in skeletally immature patients with curves less than 25°. MFS patients also have a significantly higher rate of surgical complications than non-MFS scoliosis patients.
Acetabular protrusion — the mechanismProtrusio acetabuli is defined as medial protrusion of the acetabulum ≥3 mm beyond the ilioischial (Kohler) line. Hip joint degeneration (osteoarthritis) in MFS is secondary to ligamentous laxity combined with this underlying bony abnormality — not laxity alone.

06 · Marfan Syndrome — Ocular/Pulmonary Findings & Ghent Criteria

Ocular & Pulmonary Findings

  • Annual ophthalmologic evaluation is recommended for all MFS patients, given ectopia lentis (dislocated lens) in 50–80% of patients — requires a maximally dilated slit lamp exam
  • Other ocular findings: flat cornea, retinal detachment, glaucoma, early cataract formation
  • Dural ectasia: abnormal widening/"ballooning" of the dural sac, most commonly in the lumbosacral spine, causing posterior vertebral scalloping — highly prevalent in MFS (63–92% of patients). Presents as low back pain or radicular pain in the buttocks/legs; pain may relate to periosteal pressure, erosion of lumbosacral elements, nerve root traction, or sacral microfractures secondary to osteopenia. (Also seen in vascular EDS.)
  • Emphysematous pulmonary changes: lung bullae predominantly in the upper lobes, predisposing to spontaneous pneumothorax
Practice Question
A patient with Marfan syndrome reports chronic low back pain radiating into the buttocks, with imaging showing posterior scalloping of the lumbosacral vertebral bodies. What is the most likely underlying explanation?

Ghent Criteria

Informational only, per the lecture — know that it exists and what it's for, not the point-by-point detail

The Ghent criteria combine major and minor findings across multiple organ systems (skeletal, ocular, cardiovascular, dural, pulmonary, skin, and family/genetic history) to formally establish a diagnosis of Marfan syndrome. Dr. Langevin was explicit that this is informational only — do not study it in great detail; just be aware of its utility.

The actual takeawayMFS diagnosis and management is a team sport: work with clinical geneticists/genetic counselors, cardiology, ophthalmology, orthopedics, and specialized pediatrics. The Ghent criteria are the formal tool that team uses — you don't need to reproduce it from memory.

07 · Stickler Syndrome — Overview

Stickler Syndrome — Genetics & Epidemiology

The third most common inherited connective tissue disorder
  • A group of genetic conditions affecting several genes that encode for collagen
  • 80% have a variant in COL2A1; the second most common is COL11A1
  • Prevalence: 1 in 7,500 to 9,000 newborns — making it the 3rd most common inherited connective tissue disorder
  • Signs and symptoms vary widely between affected individuals, even within the same family

Symptoms & Physical Features

  • Facial features: cleft palate, flattened face, small lower jaw (micrognathia), recessed chin
  • Eye problems: severe myopia, retinal detachment (a leading cause of blindness in this population), cataracts, glaucoma
  • Musculoskeletal: joint hypermobility, early-onset arthritis, scoliosis in some cases
  • Hearing loss: conductive or sensorineural — common
  • Pierre Robin sequence: a combination of cleft palate, small lower jaw, and tongue-based obstruction
Pierre Robin sequence — the actual mechanismThis is a chain reaction, not three independent findings: a small lower jaw (micrognathia) causes the tongue to sit further back (glossoptosis), which then often prevents the roof of the mouth from closing normally, resulting in a cleft palate. Understanding the sequence (not just the list) is what makes this testable.
Timing of diagnosisStickler syndrome is generally diagnosed during infancy or childhood, often prompted by severe myopia, cleft palate, or Pierre Robin sequence. Individuals with milder symptoms may not be diagnosed until adolescence or adulthood. Cleft palate is itself classified: Class I (soft palate only), Class II (hard and soft palate), Class III (unilateral lip/palate), Class IV (bilateral lip/palate).

08 · Stickler Syndrome — Complications, Diagnosis & Surveillance

Complications by System

Stickler syndrome complications
SystemComplications
Joint & skeletalEarly-onset arthritis (30s–40s); joint hypermobility that may become stiff; scoliosis/kyphosis with back pain; knock knees; hip issues (Legg-Calve-Perthes disease); abnormal bone ends

Other potential complicationsIncreased risk of mitral valve prolapse; learning challenges, often secondary to the vision and hearing impairments rather than a primary cognitive issue.

Diagnosis & Surveillance

  • No single universal test — diagnosis relies on a combination of methods
  • Clinical criteria: a constellation of symptoms, often scored with a point-based system assessing eyes, ears, bones/joints, palate, and family history
  • Genetic testing confirms mutations in collagen genes (COL2A1, COL11A1, COL11A2, COL9A) — helps distinguish types and aids family planning
  • Specialized assessment: ophthalmologists play a key role, particularly in identifying "ocular-only" variants
Surveillance teamOphthalmology (critical): regular, frequent check-ups with a vitreoretinal specialist to screen for cataracts, glaucoma, and retinal detachment. Audiology & ENT: annual hearing evaluations. Orthopedics/Rheumatology: monitoring for scoliosis, joint pain, early-onset osteoarthritis. Cleft/craniofacial team: managing palate issues, feeding, and breathing.

09 · Osteogenesis Imperfecta (OI) — Overview & Types

OI — Overview

"Brittle bone disease" — a type I collagen structural defect
  • Occurrence: 1 in 15,000 to 20,000 births
  • Classified into 11 types, each with subtypes, following varying autosomal patterns and severity — Types I–V are the traditional/classic types; VI–XIX are very rare and involve different genes
  • Quantitative and/or qualitative abnormalities in structurally normal type I collagen; typical mutations in COL1A1 and COL1A2
  • Leads to insufficient osteoid production; both enchondral and intramembranous ossification are affected
  • Thin, poorly organized bony trabeculae and collagen matrix, with scanty spongiosa

10 · Osteogenesis Imperfecta — Diagnosis & Management

Diagnosis

  • Combination of clinical presentation, family history, lumbar spine bone mineral density assessment, bone biochemistry, and characteristic radiographic findings
  • Prenatal ultrasound may detect severe cases; skin biopsy can assess collagen production
  • 4 hallmark features: decreased bone mass with increased fracture risk, blue sclerae, dentinogenesis imperfecta (normal enamel with abnormal dentin), and hearing loss
  • No single confirmatory lab test
Essential safety stepIn any patient presenting with multiple fractures, it is essential to exclude non-accidental trauma as part of the initial evaluation — OI is a genuine mimic of abuse and must be ruled in/out carefully in both directions.

Medical Management

  • Bisphosphonates (IV pamidronate, oral alendronate) — reduce osteoclastic bone resorption, decreasing fracture risk; this is the clinically established mainstay
  • Research-only modalities: stem cell therapy, gene therapy, biologics — gene/cell therapy targeting COL1A1/COL1A2 has shown promise in animal models but isn't widely available; sclerostin antibody (romosozumab) has shown benefit in animal studies without human trials; denosumab has improved bone quality in small studies but isn't approved for routine use; teriparatide increased bone density in a small trial but remains unapproved for this population

Orthopedic Management

  • Goals: improve functional status, prevent deformity and disability, correct existing deformities, monitor for complications
  • Orthotic devices, walking aids, and wheelchairs
  • Fracture management and correction of long bone deformities
  • Prophylactic intramedullary rodding is recommended for children with recurrent long bone fractures — rod selection (telescoping vs. non-telescoping) is based on bone size and skeletal maturity
PrognosisDepends heavily on type — mild forms allow a near-normal lifespan with managed risks, while severe types may lead to significant disability or early mortality due to complications like respiratory failure. Treatment advances have meaningfully improved outcomes.

15 · Labs — Autoantibody Tests

Sensitivity vs. Specificity — the Concept Underlying This Whole Panel

  • Sensitivity: the proportion of actual positive cases (true positives) correctly identified as positive — how well a test detects the condition. A good screening test tolerates some false positives in exchange for catching everyone with the disease (e.g., rapid strep).
  • Specificity: the proportion of actual negative cases (true negatives) correctly identified as negative — how well a test rules out the condition. What you don't want in a specific test is a false negative — someone with disease whose test comes back negative.
Know your lab's assay typeThe classic sensitivity/specificity numbers for these autoantibody tests are based on older radioimmunoassay (RIA) methods. Newer assays such as ELISA are faster and cheaper but carry a higher risk of false positives. Ask your lab which methodology they use, and factor that into the clinical picture — this becomes especially important in rheumatology practice.

17 · Psoriatic Arthritis — Risk Factors & Pathophysiology

Risk Factors & Epidemiology

  • Risk factors: psoriasis and family history; obesity, smoking/alcohol, trauma, infections/stress
  • Affects 0.1–1% of the general population and ~20% of patients with psoriasis
  • 33–50% have a first-degree relative with PsA
  • Onset in the 30s–40s, with roughly equal male/female distribution
  • More severe psoriasis = more likely to develop PsA; incidence increases with time (1.7% at 5 years of psoriasis, 3.1% at 10 years, 5.1% at 20 years, 20.5% at 30 years)
  • Skin disease precedes arthritis in 68% of cases; arthritis and skin disease coincide in 15%; arthritis precedes skin disease in 17%
Predictors of PsA in a psoriasis patientA severe psoriasis phenotype, scalp/intergluteal/perianal psoriasis, nail pitting, low level of education, and uveitis all predict future development of PsA. If a psoriasis patient shows these patterns, educate them to report joint pain early rather than dismissing it as normal aches — earlier treatment limits long-term joint damage.
Triggers vs. causeInfections and lifestyle factors (obesity, smoking, alcohol) do not cause PsA, but in genetically predisposed people they can trigger the chronic inflammatory response that tips PsA into activity.

Pathophysiology

  • CD4+/CD8+ T cells produce IL-17 and TNF-alpha
  • This process occurs mostly at the enthesis — the point where fascia, ligaments, and tendons/joint capsules attach to bone — rather than in the synovium itself, which is the more classic site of inflammation in rheumatoid arthritis
Enthesitis, in mechanistic termsAt the enthesis, collagen fibers normally run in parallel for strength; near the bony attachment, fibers shift to a more perpendicular orientation and calcify into the bone. Proinflammatory cytokines (IL-17, TNF-α) trigger neutrophil recruitment into the synovial fluid, activate synoviocytes, promote local angiogenesis, and simultaneously activate osteoclasts (bone destruction) and osteoblasts (new bone formation) — this dual, simultaneous destructive/reparative process at the enthesis is the pathophysiologic hallmark that distinguishes PsA from RA.

20 · Ankylosing Spondylitis — Epidemiology & Pathophysiology

Epidemiology

  • 0.2–0.5% of the population
  • Affects men:women at roughly 2:1
  • HLA-B27 positive in 90–95%
  • Presents most commonly before age 40 — 80% experience their first symptom before age 30; less than 5% are diagnosed after age 45
  • Cause is unknown; increased risk in relatives of affected patients
  • Twice as likely to experience a vertebral fragility fracture — something to treat aggressively and monitor regularly
Extra-articular manifestationsInflammatory bowel disease (up to 50% of individuals), acute anterior uveitis (25–35%), and psoriasis (~10%). AS is also linked to increased cardiovascular disease risk (believed to stem from chronic systemic inflammation) and pulmonary complications — diminished chest wall expansion and limited spinal mobility predispose to a restrictive pulmonary pattern.

Pathophysiology

  • Infiltrating immune cells: CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes and macrophages
  • TNF-α and TGF-β cause inflammation, fibrosis, and ossification at sites affected by enthesitis
  • The enthesis is the major site of inflammation — the same underlying theme as psoriatic arthritis, just with a different chemical trigger set and a different anatomic distribution (axial spine/SI joints predominating in AS)

  • Back/buttock/hip pain is the most common initial and chronic complaint
  • Flattening of the lumbar lordotic curve

23 · Ankylosing Spondylitis — Treatment & Complications

Treatment / Management

  • Nonmedical: osteopathic manipulation and acupuncture
  • Medications: NSAIDs for pain/inflammation; biologics (e.g., TNF inhibitors like etanercept) for moderate-severe cases; DMARDs like sulfasalazine for peripheral symptoms
  • Lifestyle: smoking cessation (worsens progression), good posture, exercise programs (yoga, Pilates, swimming)
  • Surgery: rare — for severe spinal deformity or hip replacement. Early diagnosis and treatment significantly improve quality of life.
OMM and acupuncture aren't just "soft" adjunctsBoth have shown to reduce pain and improve mobility, and should be used in conjunction with other modalities. Acupuncture combined with disease-modifying medication has shown reduced inflammatory markers (CRP, TNF, IL-6) compared to medication alone, and patients report significantly improved quality of life when OMM and acupuncture are added to their regimen.

Complications to Surveil

  • Chronic pain and disability
  • Aortic regurgitation
  • Pulmonary fibrosis
  • Cauda equina syndrome
  • Mood disorders (depression, anxiety) — patients with AS have a higher incidence of mood disorders related to chronic disease burden; ask about this at every visit if you're following someone regularly

24 · IBD-Associated Spondyloarthropathy — Epidemiology & Pathogenesis

Epidemiology

  • 0.2–0.3% of the general population; 10–20% of IBD patients will develop SpA
  • Crohn's disease > ulcerative colitis for developing SpA
  • Peripheral arthritis and axial involvement both occur; 60–70% are female (notably, IBD itself has a slightly higher male prevalence, ~1.6:1 — the arthritis-associated subgroup skews female)
  • HLA-B27 positive in 50–75% of cases
  • Diagnostic delay: women with IBD-SpA generally experience a longer diagnostic delay (sometimes 9–14 years) compared to men
ASAS-PerSpA study findingA recent large-scale study found no significant overall clinical differences between CD-associated SpA and UC-associated SpA — both groups showed similar musculoskeletal characteristics. However, psoriasis (15.4%) and uveitis (28.2%) were more common in UC-SpA, while CD patients had a higher prevalence of HLA-B27 positivity (51.9% vs. 39.4%).

Pathogenesis

  • IL-23/17 along with TNF-α drive the process
  • Immunologic abnormality initiated in the intestinal mucosa
  • Altered gut microbiota and increased intestinal permeability
  • CD14+ monocyte-like cells (the primary source of IL-23, also present in human enthesis tissue) and CD8+ T cells stimulated in the gut travel to and bind enthesis sites, stimulating inflammation there
The gut-joint axis, in sequenceReduced gut microbiota diversity → increased intestinal permeability → microbial antigens cross into the immune system (recall roughly 80–90% of the immune system resides in the gut) → subclinical gut inflammation triggers CD14+ monocytes and IL-17-producing T cells → these enter the bloodstream → travel to enthesis sites → trigger a cytokine cascade that attacks collagen and recruits neutrophils, producing enthesitis.

25 · IBD-SpA — Clinical Presentation & Diagnosis

Clinical Presentation & Screening

  • Screening all IBD patients for SpA is controversial — two screening questionnaires exist, but both have low specificity and limited usefulness
  • IBD patients complaining of inflammatory back pain, dactylitis, or enthesitis symptoms should be worked up for SpA
The misdiagnosis trapIBD itself is commonly misdiagnosed as irritable bowel syndrome, and the associated SpA is commonly misdiagnosed as fibromyalgia — this is particularly common in young patients and women, contributing to delayed diagnosis, increased disease burden, and poorer prognosis. Remember: fibromyalgia does not cause joint erosion; IBD-SpA does — this distinction should prompt a more aggressive work-up rather than dismissing the pain.

Diagnostic Approach

  • Rheumatologists tend to rely on ASAS (Assessment of SpondyloArthritis International Society) criteria
  • Gastroenterologists often use the Orchard classification instead — Type 1 (arthritis predominantly affecting large joints, often paralleling IBD activity) vs. Type 2 (polyarthritis affecting ≥5 peripheral joints, typically unrelated to IBD activity)
  • Since there's a lot of misdiagnosis and late onset, patients often bounce between rheumatology and gastroenterology, and the two specialties use different diagnostic frameworks — worth being aware of as a generalist coordinating care
  • Labs: ESR/CRP, CMP, CBC (checking for anemia of chronic disease, thrombocytopenia)
  • Imaging: X-ray and/or MRI; ultrasound is useful for enthesitis, and the patella is the most common site rheumatologists check for enthesitis in this context (not the heel, which is more classically checked in other SpA work-ups)

26 · IBD-SpA — Treatment

Treatment Approach

  • Lifestyle/nonpharmacologic: OMM and acupuncture, smoking cessation (very important), exercise and movement — medication is an adjunct, not a substitute, just as with the other spondyloarthropathies
  • No clear treatment consensus exists — both IBD and the associated SpA require therapy, and rheumatologists typically base treatment on PsA/axial SpA recommendations while gastroenterologists apply CD/UC guidelines; both approaches appear similarly efficacious
  • NSAIDs: use COX-2 inhibitors only — nonselective NSAIDs can worsen IBD (and have been shown to elevate fecal calprotectin, a marker of gut inflammation)
  • DMARDs (methotrexate, sulfasalazine, azathioprine) may help peripheral arthritis but have no impact on axial disease
The biologic balancing actChoice of biologic agent requires real care here: IL-17 inhibitors (effective for SpA/PsA) may worsen IBD. Conversely, α4β7 integrin inhibitors (effective for IBD) may induce or worsen SpA. TNF inhibitors and JAK inhibitors are promising options that can help balance both disease processes at once.

27 · Systemic Lupus Erythematosus — Epidemiology & Pathophysiology

Epidemiology — A Disease With Striking Demographic Patterns

  • 72.8 per 100,000; approximately 204,000 U.S. adults affected; most prevalent in Western societies (North America, Western Europe)
  • Ethnic bias (highest to lowest prevalence): Black/African American > Native American/Alaska Native > Hispanic/Latino > Asian/Pacific Islander > White
  • Black/African American patients have the highest overall prevalence (up to 230–287 per 100,000); Black women are 3x more likely to develop lupus than White women and often experience more severe, earlier-onset disease
  • Black and Hispanic patients have higher SLE-related mortality; Black patients often die younger, with mortality occurring up to 13 years earlier than White patients
  • Black, Hispanic, Asian, and Native American patients experience higher rates of lupus nephritis, cardiovascular disease, and more rapid progression of organ damage compared to White patients
  • Women of childbearing age: 9:1 female:male ratio
The migration clue — points to an unidentified environmental triggerBoth Africans and Asians who emigrate to the U.S. or Europe have an increased incidence of developing SLE compared to their relatives who remain in their home country. This pattern strongly suggests an environmental trigger that has not yet been elucidated — genetics alone cannot explain a change in disease incidence within the same person's lifetime after migration.
Environmental triggersInfections, smoking, silica exposure, and hormonal influences (e.g., estrogen) can initiate or exacerbate disease — this is fundamentally an epigenetics story: genes determine what you're coded for, but environmental exposures determine which genes actually get turned on.

Pathophysiology

The cascadeEnvironmental factors + genetically susceptible individual → break in immune tolerance → activation of an autoimmune response → cytokine release, complement activation, and autoantibody production → organ damage. Autoantibodies target DNA and RNA, attacking intranuclear structures. Immune complexes form and deposit in tissue (kidney, skin, vessels), activating complement pathways and recruiting inflammatory cells — a type III hypersensitivity mechanism that leads to tissue damage and vasculitis.

28 · SLE — Organ Pathology

The LE Body (Hematoxylin Body) — the Hallmark of SLE Pathology

A homogeneous globular mass of nuclear material — the classic finding across nearly every affected organ

The LE body (hematoxylin body) is a homogeneous globular mass of nuclear material that stains bluish-purple with hematoxylin. It can be observed in the lungs, kidneys, spleen, heart, lymph nodes, and serous and synovial membranes. It contains immunoglobulins and DNA, and phagocytic engulfment of the LE body produces the classic LE cell.

29 · SLE — History & Physical, Rashes & Labs

Constitutional & Mucocutaneous Findings

  • SLE is a multisystem disease with varied presentations — from mild disease with only mucocutaneous involvement, to severe life-threatening multiorgan disease
  • Constitutional symptoms (seen in >90% of SLE): fatigue, malaise, fever, anorexia, weight loss. More than 40% of SLE patients may have a lupus flare as the cause of fever — but infection must always be ruled out first, given these patients' immunocompromised state
  • 80% of SLE patients have mucocutaneous involvement, in three categories:
    1. Acute cutaneous LE (ACLE): localized, malar, and generalized forms
    2. Subacute cutaneous LE (SCLE): annular and papulosquamous forms
    3. Chronic cutaneous LE (CCLE): classic discoid lupus erythematosus (DLE)
Malar rash — the key distinguishing featureThe classic acute malar ("butterfly") rash spares the nasolabial fold — this holds true across skin tones (Caucasian, Native American, Black, Hispanic patients all show this same sparing pattern) and is what separates it from its mimics. Malar rash differential diagnosis: erysipelas, cellulitis, SLE, rosacea, pellagra, dermatomyositis (rosacea in particular tends to involve the nasolabial fold, unlike SLE). These rashes blanch with pressure; an infectious process typically will not. ACLE lesions usually heal without scarring.
Discoid lupus erythematosus (DLE)The most common form of CCLE — disk-shaped erythematous papules/plaques with adherent scaling and central clearing, an active/raised border, and a real risk of scarring alopecia. Sun exposure flares it up and can worsen the central vascular collapse/necrosis. DLE may occur with or without underlying SLE, and can be localized (head/neck only) or generalized.

Diagnostic Labs

  • ANA is positive in more than 97% of SLE cases, but a positive ANA does not confirm the diagnosis — more than 20% of the healthy general population (especially females or relatives of patients with autoimmune disease) can have a positive ANA. A negative ANA makes SLE significantly less likely.
  • If ANA positive, follow with: (ss)DNA, (ds)DNA (correlates with disease activity and lupus nephritis development), anti-Smith antibodies (less than 30% sensitivity, but 99% specific for SLE), complements C3/C4 (low = consumption, may correlate with activity)
  • Additional labs: CRP/ESR, CMP, CBC, urinalysis/protein-creatinine ratio
  • Renal biopsy is warranted if lupus nephritis is a concern — this stages the disease and directs care from nephrology

30 · SLE — Musculoskeletal Manifestations

Joint & Muscle Involvement

  • 80–90% of SLE patients have MSK involvement, ranging from mild arthralgia to deforming arthritis
  • Any joint may be involved, but the classic pattern is a non-erosive, symmetrical inflammatory polyarthritis predominantly affecting the small joints of the hands, knees, and wrists
  • 10% develop avascular necrosis, typically bilateral hip involvement
  • 10% can develop polymyositis
Jaccoud arthropathy — deformity without erosionResults from joint capsule and ligament laxity, leading to non-erosive deformities of the hands — including ulnar deviation and subluxation of the metacarpophalangeal joints — that can mimic rheumatoid arthritis on exam. The key distinguishing point: on imaging, the joints themselves look essentially normal (no cupping, no erosion) — it's the surrounding soft tissue (ligaments/capsules) that has become so lax that it collapses, not the bone that has been eroded away.

31 · SLE — Treatment & Management

Treatment by Organ System

Treatment is dictated by which organ system is involved and the severity — from minimal to intensive
  • General: patient education, physical/lifestyle measures, emotional support — this is a chronic disease, and the aim of treatment is to prevent organ damage and achieve remission
  • Cutaneous: mild disease → topical corticosteroids or topical calcineurin inhibitors; more significant disease → hydroxychloroquine, methotrexate, systemic steroids
  • Musculoskeletal: hydroxychloroquine, methotrexate, leflunomide, biologics
  • Hematologic: severe refractory cytopenias → IV pulse-dose steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, cyclophosphamide, plasmapheresis, recombinant G-CSF, or splenectomy
  • Cardiopulmonary: hydroxychloroquine, methotrexate, oral steroids
  • Renal: staging with biopsy is key — nephrology-directed care from there
Pregnancy planning in SLEPregnancy should only be attempted once the patient has been in remission for a long stretch (roughly 6–7 months) — a longer remission window is safer. Contraception should be progesterone-only, since estrogen-based contraception can trigger a flare. Hydroxychloroquine is considered safe in pregnancy and is used prophylactically (continuously), not just reactively for flares.

32 · Septic Arthritis — Epidemiology & Microbiology

Epidemiology

An orthopedic emergency — inflammation of the joint secondary to infectious etiology
  • Usually monoarticular; however, polyarticular septic arthritis involving many or smaller joints can also occur (especially with severe sepsis, immune compromise, RA, or multiple comorbidities)
  • 2–6 cases per 100,000 people — uncommon, but must stay high on the differential given the stakes
  • Children > adults; male:female 2:1; incidence peaks between ages 2 and 3
Route of infectionHematogenous seeding from a systemic infection is the most common route; next most common is direct trauma (puncture, surgery/procedure).

33 · Septic Arthritis — History & Physical, Labs & Imaging

History & Physical

  • Classically presents with acute onset monoarticular joint pain, fever, swelling, and a reluctance or refusal to move the affected joint
  • Pain with passive motion of the joint is a key exam finding
  • Fever is present in only 40–60% of cases — a nonspecific finding that should not be relied upon to rule the diagnosis in or out
The classic pediatric pitfallIn children, the hip is the most common joint involved, and a child with hip septic arthritis may present with only referred knee pain or a refusal to weight-bear — not obvious hip pain. Any child under 5 complaining of knee pain and refusing to bear weight should have the hip evaluated (imaged), even though the knee is the stated complaint, precisely so this diagnosis isn't missed.

Labs & Imaging

  • Synovial fluid: culture, Gram stain, crystal analysis, WBC count with differential — a WBC count >50,000/mm³ with >90% neutrophil predominance suggests a bacterial source
  • Blood work: CBC, ESR, CRP, and blood cultures — drawn times 2, both aerobic and anaerobic, from two different sites
  • Imaging: X-ray, ultrasound, MRI

34 · Septic Arthritis — Treatment

Treatment

  • Antimicrobial therapy and joint fluid drainage — Gram stain can help guide initial empiric coverage
  • Empiric coverage: antistaphylococcal agents (nafcillin, oxacillin, or vancomycin); a third-generation cephalosporin (ceftriaxone, ceftazidime, or cefotaxime) is added for IV drug users
  • Blood and synovial fluid cultures should guide definitive, targeted therapy once available
  • Duration: total of 4 weeks — IV for the first half, then compatible orals; extend to 6 weeks for Pseudomonas aeruginosa
If the patient isn't improvingIf no improvement is seen within 5–6 days, the joint should be re-aspirated, and Lyme disease should be ruled out. Also consider fungal infection and reactive arthritis, and use imaging to rule out osteomyelitis. Prosthetic joint infections often require aggressive debridement and/or removal of the prosthesis, with an antibiotic-impregnated cement spacer placed while the infection clears before any re-implantation.
