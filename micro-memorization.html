Oxygen Requirements

Aerobe — grows in presence of atmospheric O₂ (~20% O₂)

Obligate aerobe — requires O₂

Anaerobe — grows in the absence of O₂

Obligate anaerobe — usually killed in presence of O₂ (lacks catalase/superoxide dismutase)

Microaerophile — requires 2–10% O₂

Facultative anaerobe — does not require O₂ but grows better in its presence

Aerotolerant anaerobe — grows with or without O₂

Bacterial Biochemistry — Some Main Points
  • Bacteria need a source of building blocks, energy, and electrons.
  • Energy production can involve aerobic respiration, anaerobic respiration, or fermentation. Aerobic respiration uses O₂ as an exogenous electron acceptor; anaerobic respiration uses another exogenous electron acceptor; fermentation uses an endogenous electron acceptor.
  • Metabolism includes catabolism (breaking things down) and anabolism (building things up).
  • Bacteria that infect us must be able to grow at body temperature, but that doesn't have to be their optimal temperature.
  • Oxygen requirements can include obligate aerobes, obligate anaerobes, facultative anaerobes, aerotolerant, or microaerophiles.
  • Factors such as pH and salt can affect which bacteria grow in which parts of the body.
  • Biochemical pathways can produce molecules that can be virulence factors such as capsules, adhesion molecules, enzymes, toxins, immune evasion factors, and chelators.
  • Biochemical analysis can differentiate between different bacteria — catalase, coagulase, oxidase, citrate, CAMP, and carbohydrate utilization tests are common.
  • Media can include selective and differential types (e.g. TSI tubes, blood agar, chocolate agar, MacConkey agar, and mannitol salt agar).
  • Immune tests can also be used to identify bacteria.

Control of Microorganisms

Sterilization — kills ALL organisms, including endospores.

Disinfection — kills all pathogenic organisms, with the possible exception of endospores.

Sanitization — kills pathogens down to a safe level.

Antisepsis — less potent than disinfectants; can be used on living tissue.

Degerming — procedures (e.g. handwashing) that mechanically reduce microbes on skin/other tissues.

Agents named with a prefix for what's affected (bacteri-, viru-, fungi-, germi-) and a suffix: -cide = kills the organisms, -stat = stops them from growing.

Gram-Positive Identification Algorithm

  • Gram + (purple/blue)
    • Cocci
      • Streptococcus (pairs/chains) — catalase negative
        • Hemolysis α (partial, green) — optochin sensitivity + bile solubility → S. pneumoniae (optochin sensitive, bile soluble) vs viridans strep (no capsule, e.g. S. mutans, S. mitis)
        • Hemolysis β (complete, clear) — bacitracin sensitivity + PYR → Group A / S. pyogenes (bacitracin sensitive, PYR+) vs Group B / S. agalactiae (bacitracin resistant, PYR−)
        • Hemolysis γ (none, grows in bile) — growth in 6.5% NaCl → Enterococcus (E. faecium, E. faecalis) vs nonenterococcus (S. gallolyticus)
      • Staphylococcus (clusters) — catalase positive
        • Coagulase + → S. aureus (also β-hemolytic)
        • Coagulase − — novobiocin sensitivity → S. epidermidis (sensitive) vs S. saprophyticus (resistant)
    • Bacilli (rods)
      • Aerobic → Listeria, Bacillus, Corynebacterium (Listeria/Bacillus are also weakly β-hemolytic)
      • Anaerobic → Clostridium, Cutibacterium (formerly Propionibacterium)
    • Branching filaments
      • Aerobic, weakly acid-fast → Nocardia
      • Anaerobic, not acid-fast → Actinomyces

Gram-Negative Identification Algorithm

  • Gram − (pink)
    • Diplococci — aerobic — maltose fermentation → N. meningitidis (ferments maltose + glucose) vs N. gonorrhoeae / Moraxella (glucose only)
    • Coccobacilli → Haemophilus influenzae, Bordetella pertussis, Pasteurella, Brucella, Francisella tularensis, Acinetobacter baumannii
    • Curved rods — oxidase +
      • Grows at 42°C → Campylobacter jejuni
      • Grows in alkaline media → Vibrio cholerae
      • Produces urease → Helicobacter pylori
    • Bacilli (rods) — lactose fermentation
      • Non-fermenter — oxidase
        • Oxidase + → Pseudomonas, Burkholderia
        • Oxidase − — H₂S production on TSI agar → Salmonella, Proteus (H₂S+) vs Shigella, Yersinia (H₂S−, *pleomorphic rod/coccobacillus)
      • Fast fermenter → E. coli, Klebsiella, Enterobacter
      • Slow fermenter → Citrobacter, Serratia

Important tests are bolded; important pathogens are bold italic. *Yersinia is a pleomorphic rod/coccobacillus.

Viral genetics:
Recombination — exchange of genes between 2 chromosomes by crossing over within regions of significant base sequence homology.

Reassortment — when viruses with segmented genomes (e.g. influenza) exchange genetic material (e.g. 2009 H1N1 pandemic); potential for antigenic shift.

Complementation — when 1 of 2 viruses infecting a cell has a mutation causing a nonfunctional protein; the nonmutated virus "complements" by making a functional protein that serves both viruses.

Phenotypic mixing — simultaneous infection of a cell with 2 viruses produces progeny with a genome from one virus (A) but the surface proteins from the other (B); infectivity of the pseudovirion is determined by the coat (B tropism), but progeny from that infection encode type A capsid.
Viral genomes:
Naked nucleic acids of most dsDNA viruses (except poxviruses/HBV) and (+) strand ssRNA viruses are infectious. Naked nucleic acids of (−) strand ssRNA and dsRNA viruses are NOT infectious (lack the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase needed to transcribe (−) strand to (+)).

DNA viruses: all have dsDNA genomes (like our cells) except Parvoviridae (ssDNA); all are linear except papilloma-, polyoma-, and hepadnaviruses (circular). Mnemonic: "Part of a HHAPPPy virus" (repeato-virus)

RNA viruses: all have ssRNA genomes except Reoviridae (dsRNA); (+) stranded = retro, toga, flavi, corona, hepe, calici, picorna (also = mRNA); (−) stranded = arena, bunya, paramyxo, orthomyxo, filo, rhabdo; Segmented = Bunya, Orthomyxo, Arena, and Reoviruses ("BOAR").

Viral envelopes: generally acquired from the plasma membrane when they exit the cell. Exceptions: herpesviruses acquire their envelope from the nuclear membrane.

Endemic (Systemic Dimorphic) Mycoses

All of the following can cause pneumonia and can disseminate. All are caused by dimorphic fungi (mold cold [25°C] = mold; heat [37°C] = yeast). Only exception is Coccidioides, which is a spherule (not yeast) in tissue. Systemic mycoses can form granulomas (like TB), cannot be transmitted person-to-person (unlike TB), and amphotericin B can be used for local or systemic infection.

Brain Abscess

Most commonly caused by viridans streptococci and Staphylococcus aureus. If dental infection or extraction precedes the abscess, oral anaerobes are commonly involved.

Multiple abscesses are usually from bacteremia (hematogenous spread); single lesions arise from contiguous sites: otitis media/mastoiditis → temporal lobe and cerebellum; sinusitis/dental infection → frontal lobe.

Toxoplasma reactivation is an important cause in AIDS patients.

TORCH Infections

Microbes that may pass from mother to fetus. Transmission is transplacental in most cases, or in special cases (especially HSV-2) via vaginal delivery. Nonspecific signs common to many TORCH infections include hepatosplenomegaly, jaundice, thrombocytopenia, and growth restriction. Other important infectious agents include Streptococcus agalactiae (group B strep), E. coli, and Listeria monocytogenes — all cases of meningitis in neonates.
