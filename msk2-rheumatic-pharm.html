Rheumatoid Arthritis Overview

Chronic, progressive autoimmune disease with persistent synovial inflammation.

Inflammation → synovial proliferation (pannus) → cartilage damage, bone erosion, deformity, pain, stiffness, loss of function.

Treatment Goals

  • Reduce pain, swelling, stiffness
  • Improve physical function & QOL
  • Suppress the underlying inflammatory disease process
  • Prevent/slow progression of joint damage
  • Reduce systemic complications of chronic inflammation

Immune Targets Relevant to RA Pharmacology

Cells amplifying inflammation: T cells, B cells, macrophages, synovial fibroblasts

Pro-inflammatory cytokines: TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1, IL-17

Cytokines promote osteoclast activity via RANKL signaling → bone resorption/erosions. Pannus contributes to cartilage destruction and bone erosion.

➜ These immune/inflammatory pathways create the drug targets for DMARDs, biologics, JAK inhibitors, NSAIDs, and glucocorticoids.

Quick Check — DMARD Categories

1. What is the key mechanistic distinction between biologic DMARDs and JAK inhibitors?
Correct: Biologic DMARDs are large proteins that bind extracellular targets (cytokines, cell-surface receptors). JAK inhibitors are small molecules that cross into the cell and block intracellular JAK-STAT signaling.

NSAIDs & Glucocorticoids in RA (LO #2)

LO2: Describe the roles of NSAIDs, glucocorticoids, and DMARDs in RA treatment, including onset and ability to alter disease progression.

NSAIDs Core

Role: Symptom control only — pain, stiffness, swelling. Do NOT alter disease progression and do not replace DMARD therapy.

Onset: Relatively fast (compared to DMARDs)

Major AEs: GI toxicity, renal impairment, fluid retention/HTN, CV risk

Caution/avoid in: CKD, CVD, HF, PUD, anticoagulant use, uncontrolled HTN

Monitor: Kidney function, BP, GI bleeding risk, CV risk

Glucocorticoids Core

Role: Rapid anti-inflammatory/immunosuppressive effect. Used for flares, bridge therapy while DMARDs take effect, or intra-articular injection for localized joint inflammation.

Onset: Fast

Limitation: Chronic systemic use avoided when possible — toxicity is dose- and duration-dependent; supportive, not foundational, therapy.

Major AEs: Infection risk, osteoporosis, hyperglycemia, HTN, weight gain, mood changes, cataracts/glaucoma, skin thinning, impaired wound healing, adrenal suppression, myopathy

Monitor: Glucose, BP, infection risk, bone health, signs of adrenal suppression

Big-Picture RA Treatment Strategy

  1. Control symptoms when needed (NSAIDs/glucocorticoids)
  2. Start disease-modifying therapy early
  3. Monitor disease activity and safety
  4. Adjust/escalate therapy if goals not met

Key concept: Rapid symptom control ≠ long-term disease modification.

Quick Check — Anti-inflammatory Therapy

1. A patient with RA is started on a DMARD but needs symptom relief for the next few weeks while it takes effect. What is this strategy called?
Correct: NSAIDs or glucocorticoids are commonly used as "bridge therapy" for rapid symptom control while a DMARD's gradual onset (weeks-months) takes effect.

Conventional Synthetic DMARDs (LO #3, #4)

LO3/4: MOA, clinical use, AEs, contraindications, and monitoring for methotrexate, hydroxychloroquine, sulfasalazine, leflunomide.

All csDMARDs are Core Agents — know mechanism, clinical role, major AEs, and key monitoring/safety concerns for each.

Methotrexate Core — Anchor Drug

Role: First-line / anchor csDMARD for many patients; slows progression, reduces joint damage, improves function; used alone or combination.

Mechanism: ↑ extracellular adenosine → ↓ inflammatory cell activation and ↓ cytokine production

Administration: Once weekly (not daily!) dosing; oral or SC; renal elimination → ↑ toxicity with renal impairment

Major AEs: GI upset, stomatitis, fatigue, alopecia, hepatotoxicity, bone marrow suppression/cytopenias, GI ulceration, rare pneumonitis; teratogenic

Counseling: Give with folic acid to reduce toxicity

Monitor: CBC, LFTs, renal function

Hydroxychloroquine Core

Role: Mild RA or combination therapy; favorable safety profile; less potent for preventing erosive joint damage than MTX or leflunomide.

Mechanism: Accumulates in lysosomes → ↑ lysosomal pH → interferes with antigen processing/presentation → ↓ T-cell activation and inflammatory signaling

Major AEs: GI upset, rash, skin hyperpigmentation, retinal toxicity (can be irreversible → ophthalmologic screening baseline + ongoing), QT prolongation caution

Note: Generally safer in pregnancy than many other DMARDs

Sulfasalazine Core

Role: Mild-moderate RA, alone or combo; part of "triple therapy" with methotrexate + hydroxychloroquine.

Mechanism: Prodrug cleaved by colonic bacteria → sulfapyridine (most important component in RA) → ↓ pro-inflammatory cytokine activity, modulates T-cell/immune responses

Major AEs: GI upset, headache, rash, orange urine/sweat discoloration; serious: hepatotoxicity, bone marrow suppression (leukopenia/agranulocytosis), hypersensitivity, hemolytic anemia in G6PD deficiency

Counseling: Folate impairment → folic acid supplementation; report fever, sore throat, bruising/bleeding, jaundice, severe rash

Monitor: CBC, LFTs

Leflunomide Core

Role: Moderate-severe RA; often an alternative to methotrexate when MTX not tolerated/contraindicated.

Mechanism: Active metabolite inhibits dihydroorotate dehydrogenase → ↓ de novo pyrimidine synthesis → ↓ T-/B-cell proliferation → ↓ immune-mediated inflammation

Key PK: Long half-life (enterohepatic recirculation) — toxicity may persist after stopping; cholestyramine washout can accelerate elimination

Major AEs: Hepatotoxicity, diarrhea, HTN, rash, alopecia, bone marrow suppression; teratogenic — avoid pregnancy

Monitor: LFTs, CBC, BP

Rarely Used / Lower-Yield DMARDs Lower-yield

Selected-use immunosuppressants for refractory disease or serious extra-articular involvement — know names/category only:

Azathioprine Cyclophosphamide Cyclosporine Mycophenolate mofetil

Quick Check — csDMARDs

1. A patient on hydroxychloroquine needs which specific type of monitoring not required for the other csDMARDs?
Correct: Hydroxychloroquine accumulates in the retina and can cause irreversible retinal toxicity, so ophthalmologic screening is required at baseline and ongoing.
2. Why is methotrexate dosed once weekly instead of daily for RA?
Weekly dosing is a critical, high-yield counseling point — mistaking it for daily dosing is a classic, dangerous medication error.

Biologic DMARDs

Overview

bDMARDs = protein-based therapies (mostly mAbs or fusion proteins) targeting specific immune components: pro-inflammatory cytokines/receptors, T-cell co-stimulation, or B cells.

Clinical role: Moderate-severe RA when csDMARD response is inadequate; may combine with a csDMARD; avoid combining with another biologic or a JAK inhibitor.

Naming: Many end in –mab. -xi- = chimeric (↑ immunogenicity risk), -u- = human. Biosimilars are highly similar (not identical) to a reference biologic — potential immunogenicity/access/cost/substitution issues.

TNF-α Inhibitors Class-Level

Why target TNF-α: Major pro-inflammatory cytokine — amplifies synovial inflammation, promotes IL-1/IL-6, contributes to pannus formation, cartilage damage, bone erosion.

Mechanism (class): Bind TNF-α → prevent TNF receptor activation → ↓ cytokine production, ↓ immune cell recruitment, ↓ synovial inflammation and joint damage progression.

Clinical use: Moderate-severe RA with inadequate csDMARD response; commonly combined with methotrexate; avoid combining with another biologic or JAK inhibitor.

Class safety — major concern: infection

  • Serious/opportunistic infections; latent TB reactivation → screen before therapy; hepatitis B screening often performed
  • Vaccines: review status before therapy; avoid live vaccines during treatment
  • Injection-site/infusion reactions, demyelinating disease, worsening HF, cytopenias, lupus-like syndrome, possible malignancy risk

Quick Check — Biologic DMARDs

1. Which biologic is a soluble receptor fusion protein rather than a monoclonal antibody?
Correct: Etanercept is a soluble TNF receptor fusion protein, distinguishing it structurally from the four TNF mAbs (adalimumab, infliximab, certolizumab, golimumab).
2. Why is CRP a less reliable marker of infection in a patient on tocilizumab?
Correct: CRP production is driven by IL-6 signaling in the liver, so IL-6 receptor blockade lowers CRP regardless of infection — a key high-yield safety pearl.

Targeted Synthetic DMARDs — JAK Inhibitors

tsDMARDs = small-molecule drugs targeting intracellular signaling pathways. In RA, the main tsDMARDs are JAK (Janus kinase) inhibitors, which block the JAK-STAT pathway that many inflammatory cytokines signal through.

vs. Biologics: Biologic DMARDs = large molecules, extracellular targets. JAK inhibitors = small molecules, oral, act inside the cell, ↓ signaling from multiple cytokines.

Clinical use: Moderate-severe RA inadequately controlled with other therapies; may be used alone or with csDMARDs; do NOT combine with bDMARDs.

Class-level major safety concerns: Serious infections, TB, herpes zoster reactivation; cytopenias; lipid elevations; thrombosis / MACE / malignancy / mortality warning; less common: GI perforation.

Tofacitinib Core — Representative Agent

Role: Oral tsDMARD, moderate-severe RA

Mechanism: Primarily affects JAK1 and JAK3

PK/interactions: Metabolized by CYP3A4 → interactions with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors/inducers; dose adjust with renal/hepatic impairment

Safety: Shares class warnings — serious infection/zoster, cytopenias, lipid elevations, thrombosis/MACE/malignancy warning

Quick Check — tsDMARDs

1. A patient on tofacitinib is prescribed a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor. Why is this significant?
Correct: Tofacitinib is primarily metabolized by CYP3A4, making it susceptible to significant interactions with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors or inducers.

Clinical Takeaways — RA

  • Separate symptom control (NSAIDs/glucocorticoids) from disease modification (DMARDs)
  • Methotrexate = anchor csDMARD; escalation = combination csDMARDs → biologics or JAK inhibitors
  • Match therapy to patient: pregnancy potential, renal/hepatic function, infection history/vaccination status, CV risk, malignancy history, lung disease, HF, demyelinating disease
  • Safety monitoring follows the mechanism: CBC, LFTs, renal function, lipids, eye exams, infection screening, vaccine planning

Gout Overview

Inflammatory arthritis caused by monosodium urate (MSU) crystal deposition. Crystals trigger a strong innate immune response → joint inflammation.

Classic presentation: Acute painful monoarthritis; common site = 1st metatarsophalangeal joint (podagra). Flares = rapid onset pain, swelling, warmth, erythema.

Recurrent/uncontrolled: chronic joint damage and tophi

Risk factors: Male sex, older age, CKD, obesity, alcohol use, diuretics, high-purine or high-fructose intake

Hyperuricemia & Crystal Formation

Uric acid = final product of purine metabolism. Elevated serum urate creates conditions for crystal formation, but hyperuricemia does not automatically mean gout.

Gout develops when crystals form → deposit in joints/tissues → trigger inflammation.

Inflammatory Cascade in Acute Gout

  • MSU crystals recognized by macrophages
  • Crystal ingestion activates the NLRP3 inflammasome
  • ↑ IL-1β → neutrophil recruitment → amplified joint inflammation

➜ This is why colchicine (↓ neutrophil function/inflammasome activation) and IL-1 inhibitors work in gout.

Gout Treatment Strategy (LO #5)

LO5: Differentiate pharmacologic strategies for acute gout flares vs. prevention of recurrent gout.
Acute Flare TreatmentPrevention (Urate-Lowering Therapy)
GoalRapidly ↓ crystal-triggered inflammation, pain, swelling↓ urate burden so new crystals don't form & existing deposits dissolve
Main drugsNSAIDs, colchicine, glucocorticoids (IL-1 inhibitors in refractory cases)Allopurinol, febuxostat, probenecid (pegloticase in severe/refractory)
What they do NOT doDo not lower urate burden or prevent long-term crystal formationDo not primarily treat immediate flare pain

Important Initiation Problem

Starting or changing urate-lowering therapy can precipitate gout flares early in treatment — serum urate changes destabilize existing crystal deposits.

Prophylaxis strategy: Anti-inflammatory prophylaxis when initiating ULT — low-dose colchicine preferred when appropriate; alternatives = NSAID or low-dose prednisone/prednisolone. Typical duration: at least 3–6 months, individualized.

Quick Check — Gout Strategy

1. Why can starting allopurinol trigger an acute gout flare?
Correct: Any therapy that changes serum urate levels quickly (up or down) can destabilize deposits and provoke a flare — hence routine anti-inflammatory prophylaxis (often low-dose colchicine) at ULT initiation.

Acute Gout Flare Treatment (LO #6)

LO6: Mechanisms, indications, and major AEs of colchicine, NSAIDs, glucocorticoids, allopurinol, febuxostat, probenecid.

Treatment choice depends on: kidney function, GI risk, CV risk, diabetes, infection concern, drug interactions, number of joints involved, ability to take oral meds.

NSAIDs Core

Mechanism: Inhibit COX enzymes → ↓ prostaglandin production → ↓ pain/inflammation

Use: First-line acute flare treatment; do NOT lower serum urate or prevent future crystal formation

Safety: Kidney function, GI bleed risk, CVD, HF, BP, age, anticoagulant use

Gout-specific point: Aspirin is generally avoided as gout flare treatment.

Colchicine Core

Role: Acute flare treatment (most effective started early); also used as prophylaxis when starting ULT. Does not lower serum urate, dissolve crystals, or prevent long-term crystal formation.

Mechanism: Binds tubulin → inhibits microtubule polymerization → ↓ neutrophil migration/adhesion/phagocytosis/mediator release; limits NLRP3 inflammasome activation → ↓ IL-1β–mediated inflammation

Key PK: Oral, narrow therapeutic window; CYP3A4 & P-glycoprotein substrate → strong inhibitors ↑ toxicity

Common AEs: Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhea (early warning signs of toxicity)

Serious toxicities: Bone marrow suppression (leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, aplastic anemia), myopathy/neuromyopathy, rhabdomyolysis

High-yield safety: Neuromuscular toxicity risk ↑ with renal impairment and myotoxic drugs (e.g., statins); life-threatening toxicity with strong CYP3A4/P-gp inhibitors

Glucocorticoids Core

Mechanism: Broad anti-inflammatory — ↓ cytokines, ↓ prostaglandin synthesis, ↓ leukocyte migration/immune cell activation

Clinical role: Option for acute flares; useful when NSAIDs/colchicine unsafe or not tolerated (e.g., CKD, GI risk, drug interactions)

Route: Oral/systemic for multiple joints; intra-articular for 1–2 joints (consider septic arthritis before injecting an acutely inflamed joint)

Safety: Short-term — hyperglycemia, ↑BP, mood/sleep changes, appetite increase, fluid retention. Longer-term/repeated — infection risk, adrenal suppression, osteoporosis

IL-1 Inhibitors Selected-use / Specialized

Role: NOT routine first-line — considered when NSAIDs, colchicine, glucocorticoids are ineffective, contraindicated, or not tolerated (refractory flares).

Rationale: MSU crystals activate NLRP3 inflammasome → ↑ IL-1β → neutrophil recruitment/inflammation; IL-1 blockade reduces this.

Agents: Anakinra Canakinumab Rilonacept

Class safety: Infection risk, injection-site reactions, hypersensitivity; avoid combining with other biologic immunosuppressants

Quick Check — Acute Gout Flare Treatment

1. A gout patient on a statin is prescribed colchicine for an acute flare. What is the primary concern?
Correct: Both colchicine and statins carry myotoxic risk; combining them (especially with renal impairment or CYP3A4/P-gp inhibition) raises the risk of myopathy and rhabdomyolysis.
2. Which NSAID is generally avoided specifically for gout flare treatment?
Correct: Aspirin has complex, dose-dependent effects on urate handling and is generally avoided as gout flare treatment.

Urate-Lowering Therapy

Allopurinol Core — Preferred First-Line

Role: Preferred first-line ULT for most patients; promotes gradual dissolution of crystal deposits; prevents tophi, joint damage, uric acid kidney stones.

Mechanism: Purine analog; inhibits xanthine oxidase → ↓ conversion hypoxanthine → xanthine → uric acid → ↓ serum urate

Dosing: Oral; start low and titrate to serum urate goal (typically <6 mg/dL); renal function matters for dosing/safety

Major AEs: Rash/hypersensitivity (report promptly); rare but serious allopurinol hypersensitivity syndrome (SJS/TEN, fever, eosinophilia, hepatitis, kidney injury)

Risk factors for hypersensitivity syndrome: High starting dose, renal impairment, HLA-B*58:01 (test recommended in higher-risk populations: Han Chinese, Korean, Thai, African American ancestry)

Monitor: Serum urate, renal function, LFTs, CBC

Key interaction: Azathioprine/mercaptopurine → ↑ severe bone marrow suppression risk

➜ Gout-specific: starting/increasing allopurinol can precipitate early flares → anti-inflammatory prophylaxis commonly used at initiation.

Febuxostat Core — MTX-tolerance Alternative

Role: Alternative ULT when allopurinol not tolerated, inadequate despite titration, or not advisable. Preventive therapy — not for immediate flare pain.

Mechanism: Selective nonpurine xanthine oxidase inhibitor → ↓ uric acid production → ↓ serum urate

PK: Oral; does not rely primarily on renal elimination (contrast with allopurinol)

Major AEs: ↑ LFTs, nausea, diarrhea, arthralgia, rash/hypersensitivity

Boxed warning: ↑ cardiovascular death risk vs. allopurinol in patients with established CVD.

Key interaction: Contraindicated with azathioprine/mercaptopurine

Probenecid Core

Role: Alternative/add-on when xanthine oxidase inhibitors inadequate/not tolerated. Preventive — not for immediate flare pain; flare risk at initiation.

Mechanism: Uricosuric — inhibits renal tubular urate reabsorption, mainly via URAT1 in the proximal tubule → ↑ urinary uric acid excretion → ↓ serum urate

PK: Oral; requires adequate kidney function to work; generally avoided with uric acid nephrolithiasis history or significant CKD

Major AEs: GI upset, nausea, headache, rash; ↑ risk of uric acid kidney stones → encourage hydration

Key interactions: Can ↑ levels of penicillins and methotrexate (↓ renal tubular secretion); salicylates (aspirin) reduce the uricosuric effect

Pegloticase Specialized — Severe/Refractory Chronic Gout

Role: Specialized ULT — not routine first-line; used for severe or refractory chronic gout.

Mechanism: Recombinant pegylated uricase enzyme → converts uric acid → allantoin → ↓ serum urate and crystal burden

Key safety: Infusion reactions/anaphylaxis; anti-drug antibodies → loss of response & ↑ infusion reaction risk (methotrexate often co-administered to blunt this); contraindicated in G6PD deficiency (hemolysis/methemoglobinemia risk); early flares → use flare prophylaxis

Quick Check — Urate-Lowering Therapy

1. A patient of Han Chinese ancestry is about to start allopurinol. What should be considered before initiation?
Correct: HLA-B*58:01 testing is recommended for higher-risk populations (including Han Chinese, Korean, Thai, and African American ancestry) before starting allopurinol due to increased risk of severe hypersensitivity syndrome/SJS-TEN.
2. Why does probenecid require adequate kidney function to be effective?
Correct: Probenecid works by blocking URAT1-mediated urate reabsorption in the proximal tubule, so impaired renal function undermines the drug's ability to increase urinary urate excretion.
3. Which ULT agent is contraindicated in G6PD deficiency?
Correct: Pegloticase is contraindicated in G6PD deficiency due to risk of hemolysis and methemoglobinemia.
