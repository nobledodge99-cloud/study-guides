OMM Techniques Comparison

Myofascial Release · HVLA · Muscle Energy · Lymphatics · BLT/LAS · Still Technique

High-yield pattern: the gentler, lower-force techniques (MFR, BLT/LAS, and largely Lymphatics) have no true absolute contraindication beyond lack of patient consent — everything else on their list is relative. HVLA stands out with the most genuine absolute contraindications, since it's the highest-force technique and can't be adjusted mid-thrust the way other techniques can be backed off.
Technique Active / Passive Direct / Indirect Target Tissue Absolute Contraindications Relative Contraindications Steps (Brief)
Myofascial
Release (MFR)		 Passive
active variant exists		 Both — direct engages/holds the barrier; indirect moves toward ease and holds Fascia / myofascial planes None beyond lack of consent Acute fracture, open wound/infection at site, anticoagulation/bleeding disorder near site, recent surgery at site Locate the fascial restriction → apply gentle traction, compression, or torsion into the barrier (direct) or toward ease (indirect) → follow tissue as it releases → reassess
HVLA Passive Direct — engages and thrusts through the restrictive barrier Articulation / joint Fracture; joint instability (incl. atlantoaxial instability, Down syndrome); active bone/joint infection; malignancy at site; severe/unstable osteoporosis; acute myelopathy; vertebrobasilar insufficiency (cervical) Anticoagulation, mild–moderate osteoporosis, disc herniation with radiculopathy, spondylolisthesis, post-surgical fusion, pregnancy (region-dependent) Diagnose restrictive barrier → precisely localize the joint to engage that barrier → deliver a quick, small-amplitude, high-velocity thrust through it (often an audible pop) → reassess
Muscle Energy
Technique (MET)		 Active — requires patient's own muscular effort Direct — patient engages the barrier and contracts against it Muscle (via reflex mechanisms — post-isometric relaxation, reciprocal inhibition — to restore joint mobility) Fracture at site; patient unable to voluntarily cooperate/contract (unconscious, severe cognitive impairment); acute muscle/tendon tear Acute severe pain limiting contraction, severe osteoporosis, fragile post-surgical tissue Diagnose barrier → position joint/muscle to engage it → patient performs brief (~3–5 sec) isometric contraction against matched counterforce → relax → move to new barrier → repeat 3–5x → reassess
Lymphatic
Techniques		 Passive
breathing coordination		 N/A — mechanical/pumping rather than barrier-based Lymphatic system, fascial diaphragms (pelvic, respiratory, thoracic inlet) Active untreated infection (risk of dissemination/sepsis); malignancy (risk of promoting spread); DVT (embolism risk); decompensated CHF; fracture at site Recent abdominal/thoracic surgery, pregnancy, severe osteoporosis (direct pressure techniques) Release fascial diaphragms in sequence (pelvic → respiratory → thoracic inlet) → apply pump techniques (thoracic pump, pedal pump) synchronized with respiration to drive lymph centrally
BLT / LAS Passive Classified as indirect for COMLEX, though direct/indirect variants both exist Ligaments (peri-articular) None beyond lack of consent Acute fracture, open wounds, soft tissue/bony infection, abscess, DVT, anticoagulation, neoplasm, recent post-op site, aortic aneurysm Disengage (compress/decompress joint) → Exaggerate (move to position of ease across all planes to find balance point) → Balance (hold until release) — "DEB likes her BLT"
Still
Technique		 Passive Combined — begins indirect/ease, then moves through the restrictive barrier via a curved path Joint/articulation and surrounding myofascial tissue Fracture, joint instability, active infection, malignancy at site, severe osteoporosis (compression component) DVT (if compression used near it), anticoagulation, acute inflammatory conditions, fragile post-surgical tissue Position toward ease → apply compression or traction → move the joint through a curved arc across and past the restrictive barrier → return to neutral → reassess
Note: exact direct/indirect and active/passive classifications for MFR and Lymphatics can vary slightly by textbook/program — cross-check against your course's specific framing if a question hinges on that exact wording.
