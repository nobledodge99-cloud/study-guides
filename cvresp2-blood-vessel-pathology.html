1. Vessel Wall & Endothelium

Arteries carry pressure, veins carry volume. The three-layer plan (intima / media / adventitia) scales up or down depending on the job a given vessel is doing — and every vascular disease in this guide is really a disease of one of those three layers.

Vessel Types by Size

VesselWall FeaturesFunction
Large elastic arteries (aorta, innominate, subclavian)Thick media with abundant elastic lamellae between internal & external elastic laminaeElastic recoil during diastole propels blood forward ("pressure reservoir")
Medium muscular arteries (coronary, renal)Media dominated by smooth muscle, thick wallDistribute blood, regulate flow via vasomotor tone
Small arteries / arterioles (<2 mm)Media = principal component, little adventitiaPrincipal site of physiologic resistance & blood pressure control
Capillaries (7–8 µm)Endothelium only, no mediaGas/nutrient exchange; slow, low-pressure laminar flow
VeinsThin media, more collagen (adventitia), easily compressedCapacitance/volume reservoir, return blood to heart
LymphaticsEndothelial-lined channels onlyReturn interstitial fluid to venous circulation via thoracic duct

The Endothelium

  • Maintains a permeability barrier, regulates coagulation (anticoagulant at baseline), inflammation, and vascular tone
  • Key mediators: nitric oxide & prostacyclin (vasodilators, antithrombotic); ACE (angiotensin I→II); P- and E-selectins/ICAM/VCAM — adhesion molecules for leukocyte rolling/adhesion
  • Endothelial activation: physiologic stimuli (turbulent flow, cytokines, hypoxia) upregulate adhesion molecules and procoagulant factors in a controlled way
  • Endothelial dysfunction: pathologic stimuli — cigarette smoke, hypoxia, acidosis, advanced glycation end-products, hypertension — produce a persistently pro-inflammatory, pro-thrombotic phenotype
Board Tip The "response-to-injury" hypothesis that drives atherosclerosis (Section 2) is built entirely on this endothelial injury/dysfunction concept — learn it once here and it explains the rest of the unit.
Practice Questions

Which vessel layer is the primary determinant of peripheral vascular resistance and is therefore the principal site of blood pressure regulation?

A) Adventitia of large elastic arteries
B) Intima of medium muscular arteries
C) Media of small arteries and arterioles
D) Endothelium of capillaries
Small arteries and arterioles have a media that dominates the vessel wall relative to their diameter; smooth muscle tone here is the chief regulator of total peripheral resistance and thus blood pressure.

Which of the following endothelial-derived mediators acts as a vasodilator and inhibits platelet aggregation?

A) Endothelin-1
B) Nitric oxide
C) Thromboxane A2
D) von Willebrand factor
Nitric oxide (with prostacyclin) is a vasodilator and antithrombotic mediator produced by endothelium; endothelin-1 is a vasoconstrictor, thromboxane A2 promotes platelet aggregation, and vWF mediates platelet adhesion.

A vessel with an endothelial lining but no media layer, functioning purely for gas and nutrient exchange, is best classified as a:

A) Small artery
B) Vein
C) Muscular artery
D) Capillary
Capillaries consist of endothelium alone (no media), enabling slow laminar flow for efficient gas/nutrient exchange.

2. Atherosclerosis Pathogenesis

Arteriosclerosis: The Umbrella Term

"Arteriosclerosis" = thickening and hardening of arteries. Three distinct pathological entities fall under it:

EntityVessel affectedClinical significance
AtherosclerosisElastic & muscular arteriesHigh — #1 cause of CAD, MI, stroke, gangrene
Monckeberg medial calcific sclerosisMuscular arteries (media)Low — incidental, does not narrow lumen
Arteriolosclerosis (hyaline/hyperplastic)Small arteries/arteriolesAssociated with hypertension & diabetes

Response-to-Injury Hypothesis

  • Atherosclerosis is a chronic inflammatory/healing response of the arterial wall to endothelial injury
  • Sequence: injury → endothelial dysfunction → monocyte adhesion & migration into intima (become macrophages) → lipid uptake (foam cells) → smooth muscle migration/proliferation from media into intima → extracellular matrix deposition → fibrofatty plaque
  • Begins in the teens (fatty streaks) — a preclinical process that progresses silently for decades before an event

Major Risk Factors

Modifiable — MajorModifiable — MinorNon-modifiable
Hyperlipidemia (↑LDL, ↓HDL, ↑triglycerides), hypertension, diabetes, smokingObesity, physical inactivity, Type-A personality/stress, high-fat dietIncreasing age, male sex/postmenopausal female, family history, genetic disorders
Board Tip Homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia (LDL-receptor defect) → MI by ~age 20; heterozygous form → disease by ~40. Elevated hs-CRP is an independent marker of vascular inflammation/risk.

Pathogenic Contributors

  • Chronic hyperlipidemia → LDL accumulates in intima → oxidized by free radicals → oxidized LDL is cytotoxic to endothelium and is what macrophages avidly take up
  • Endothelial permeability to lipoprotein, hemodynamic turbulence (branch points), hypertension, and thrombosis all contribute
Practice Questions

A 19-year-old who dies in a motor vehicle accident is found at autopsy to have yellow, minimally raised intimal streaks at arterial branch points. This finding most likely represents which stage of vascular disease?

A) Monckeberg medial calcific sclerosis
B) Early fatty streak — the preclinical phase of atherosclerosis
C) Hyperplastic arteriolosclerosis
D) Cystic medial degeneration
Fatty streaks (lipid-laden macrophages/foam cells) begin forming in the teens, well before clinical disease — atherosclerosis is a decades-long process that begins far earlier than symptoms appear.

Which cell type, upon migrating from the media into the intima and proliferating, contributes to fibrous cap formation in an atherosclerotic plaque?

A) Neutrophil
B) Mast cell
C) Endothelial progenitor cell
D) Smooth muscle cell
Smooth muscle cells migrate from the media into the intima, proliferate, and produce extracellular matrix/collagen to form the fibrous cap; macrophages (derived from monocytes, not smooth muscle) are the primary foam cell precursor.

A 55-year-old with poorly controlled diabetes and a smoking history has markedly elevated LDL. Which modification of LDL is most directly responsible for its uptake by macrophages via scavenger receptors, driving foam cell formation?

A) Oxidation
B) Glycosylation of ApoA-I
C) Esterification by LCAT
D) Cleavage by ACE
Oxidized LDL is taken up avidly by macrophage scavenger receptors (unregulated by feedback), driving foam cell formation — a central pathogenic step in atherosclerosis.

3. Plaque Morphology & Complications

Components of the Atheroma

  • Fibrous cap: smooth muscle cells, macrophages, foam cells, collagen
  • Necrotic core: cell debris, cholesterol clefts (dissolved during processing, leaving clear spaces on H&E), foam cells, calcium
  • Shoulder region: ongoing inflammation and neovascularization

Stable vs. Unstable (Vulnerable) Plaque

Stable PlaqueUnstable/Vulnerable Plaque
Fibrous capThickThin
Lipid coreSmallLarge, necrotic
InflammationMinimalProminent
Major complicationChronic, progressive luminal narrowing → critical stenosis/ischemia (e.g., stable angina)Plaque rupture/hemorrhage → superimposed thrombosis → acute occlusion (MI, stroke)
Critical stenosis is generally cited at >70–90% luminal occlusion — beyond this point fixed flow limitation itself can precipitate ischemia (e.g., stable angina); acute events, however, are usually driven by rupture of a plaque that was not previously flow-limiting.

Complications

  • Rupture with superimposed thrombosis → MI, stroke
  • Hemorrhage into the plaque → sudden expansion/occlusion
  • Calcification → wall rigidity
  • Weakening of the media underneath → aneurysm formation
  • Embolization of plaque/thrombus material
Practice Questions

Which plaque feature is most predictive of acute rupture and thrombotic occlusion, as opposed to gradual flow-limiting stenosis?

A) Thin fibrous cap over a large necrotic lipid core with active inflammation
B) Thick fibrous cap with dense calcification
C) Concentric medial hyperplasia
D) Monckeberg-type calcification of the media
The vulnerable/unstable plaque phenotype — thin cap, large necrotic core, ongoing inflammation — is prone to rupture and superimposed thrombosis, producing acute events like MI even when luminal narrowing was previously mild.

Histologic examination of a ruptured coronary plaque reveals empty, needle-shaped spaces within the necrotic core. These spaces most likely represent dissolved:

A) Calcium deposits
B) Cholesterol crystals
C) Fibrin strands
D) Hemosiderin
Cholesterol clefts form when cholesterol crystals dissolve out during tissue processing, leaving characteristic needle-shaped empty spaces within the necrotic lipid core.

Which histologic feature is most characteristic of a stable atherosclerotic plaque as opposed to a vulnerable one?

A) Thin fibrous cap
B) Large lipid-rich necrotic core
C) Thick fibrous cap with minimal inflammation
D) Extensive neovascularization within the plaque
Stable plaques have a thick fibrous cap and minimal active inflammation, protecting against rupture; vulnerable plaques instead show thin caps, large necrotic cores, and inflammation.

4. Monckeberg & Arteriolosclerosis

Monckeberg Medial Calcific Sclerosis

  • Dystrophic calcification of the media of muscular arteries
  • Does not encroach on the lumen — little to no clinical significance
  • Palpable, "pipestem" vessels; visible on X-ray as calcified rings

Arteriolosclerosis (Associated with Hypertension)

TypeMorphologyClinical Association
Hyaline arteriolosclerosisHomogeneous pink hyaline thickening of the arteriolar wall (leaked plasma proteins + increased matrix)Benign/chronic hypertension, diabetes, elderly; diffuse glomerular scarring in kidney
Hyperplastic arteriolosclerosis"Onion-skinning" — concentric, laminated smooth muscle proliferation; may show fibrinoid necrosis of the wallMalignant/accelerated hypertension (typically >200/120 mmHg); medical emergency
Board Tip Onion-skinning = hyperplastic arteriolosclerosis = malignant hypertension. Fibrinoid necrosis on immunofluorescence shows bright staining for fibrin within the vessel wall.
Practice Questions

A patient presents with a blood pressure of 220/130 mmHg, papilledema, and acute kidney injury. Renal biopsy shows concentric, laminated ("onion-skin") thickening of arteriolar walls with fibrinoid necrosis. What is this lesion called?

A) Hyaline arteriolosclerosis
B) Monckeberg medial calcific sclerosis
C) Fibromuscular dysplasia
D) Hyperplastic arteriolosclerosis
Onion-skinning with fibrinoid necrosis is the histologic signature of hyperplastic arteriolosclerosis, the vascular lesion of malignant/accelerated hypertension.

An incidental finding on abdominal X-ray shows calcified, pipestem-appearing femoral arteries in an asymptomatic 70-year-old, with no luminal narrowing. This is most consistent with:

A) Monckeberg medial calcific sclerosis
B) Hyaline arteriolosclerosis
C) Hyperplastic arteriolosclerosis
D) Fibromuscular dysplasia
Monckeberg medial calcific sclerosis causes dystrophic calcification of the muscular artery media without encroaching on the lumen, making it typically an incidental, clinically insignificant finding.

A 60-year-old with long-standing type 2 diabetes and hypertension undergoes renal biopsy showing homogeneous, pink hyaline thickening of the arteriolar walls. This finding is most associated with:

A) Malignant hypertension
B) Vasculitis
C) Amyloid deposition
D) Chronic benign hypertension and diabetes
Hyaline arteriolosclerosis — homogeneous pink hyaline thickening from leaked plasma proteins — is classically seen in chronic (benign) hypertension and diabetes, contributing to nephrosclerosis.

5. Blood Pressure Regulation & Definitions

Blood Pressure Categories (mmHg)

CategorySystolicDiastolic
Normal<120and <80
Elevated ("pre-hypertension")120–129and <80
Hypertension Stage 1130–139or 80–89
Hypertension Stage 2≥140or ≥90
Hypertensive crisis/emergency>180and/or >120

Determinants of Pressure

Blood pressure = Cardiac Output (CO) × Total Peripheral Resistance (TPR). Every mechanism of hypertension in this guide funnels through one or both of these two variables.
  • ↑CO: ↑heart rate, ↑sodium/water retention (aldosterone, ANP dysregulation)
  • ↑TPR: vasoconstrictors (angiotensin II, catecholamines), structural wall thickening reducing lumen caliber

Epidemiology & Consequences

  • Affects ~30% of US adults; risk increases with age; higher prevalence/severity in African-Americans
  • Most important risk factor for cardiovascular disease over age 45; patients are usually asymptomatic (found incidentally)
  • Untreated: ~50% die of heart disease/CHF, ~1/3 die of stroke
  • End-organ damage: left ventricular hypertrophy/heart failure, multi-infarct dementia, aortic dissection, renal failure (nephrosclerosis), retinopathy
Practice Questions

The two physiologic variables that directly determine arterial blood pressure are:

A) Stroke volume and heart rate only
B) Cardiac output and total peripheral resistance
C) Renin and aldosterone only
D) Blood volume and vessel compliance only
BP = CO × TPR. Every hormonal or structural mechanism of hypertension acts by raising one (or both) of these two variables.

A patient's blood pressure is 128/78 mmHg on a routine visit. According to current classification, this reading is best categorized as:

A) Normal
B) Hypertension stage 1
C) Elevated blood pressure
D) Hypertensive crisis
Systolic 120–129 with diastolic <80 defines the "elevated" (formerly pre-hypertension) category, distinct from normal (<120/<80) and stage 1 (130–139/80–89).

Chronic, poorly controlled hypertension most directly contributes to which of the following end-organ complications via arteriolar and capillary bed injury?

A) Acute pancreatitis
B) Nephrosclerosis and renal failure
C) Peptic ulcer disease
D) Cholelithiasis
Chronic hypertension damages renal arterioles (hyaline arteriolosclerosis), producing nephrosclerosis with progressive renal impairment — one of the classic end-organ complications alongside LVH, dementia, and dissection.

6. Primary vs. Secondary Hypertension

Primary (Essential/Idiopathic) Hypertension

  • Accounts for ~90–95% of all hypertension
  • Multifactorial: genetic + environmental (stress, obesity, smoking, inactivity, heavy salt intake)
  • Core defect: impaired renal sodium excretion at a given pressure → fluid overload/↑CO, with compensatory but pathologic peripheral vasoconstriction resetting the pressure "set point" upward

Secondary Hypertension — "RENAL" Mnemonic

LetterCategoryExamples
RRenalRenal artery stenosis (↑renin), chronic kidney disease — most common secondary cause
EEndocrinePrimary hyperaldosteronism (most common endocrine cause), Cushing syndrome, pheochromocytoma, hyperthyroidism
NNeurologic↑Intracranial pressure
AAorticCoarctation of the aorta
L"Labor"/Latrogenic-LifestylePregnancy (preeclampsia), psychogenic stress, post-operative state, drugs

The RAAS Pathway

  • ↓Renal perfusion → juxtaglomerular (JG) cells (modified smooth muscle near the glomerulus) release renin
  • Renin converts angiotensinogen (liver) → angiotensin I
  • ACE (lung/vascular endothelium) converts angiotensin I → angiotensin II
  • Angiotensin II: direct vasoconstriction (↑TPR) + stimulates adrenal cortex to release aldosterone → renal Na⁺/water retention (↑CO)
Board Tip Other secondary endocrine mimics of hyperaldosteronism: Liddle syndrome (ENaC gain-of-function), 11β-HSD2 deficiency/licorice ingestion (impaired cortisol→cortisone conversion lets cortisol activate mineralocorticoid receptors), and congenital adrenal hyperplasia (11β-hydroxylase or 17α-hydroxylase deficiency) — all converge on excess distal nephron sodium reabsorption.
Practice Questions

A 45-year-old with resistant hypertension is found to have an abdominal bruit and unilateral renal artery stenosis on imaging. Which enzyme, produced in response to the resulting hypoperfusion, initiates the cascade responsible for this patient's hypertension?

A) Angiotensin-converting enzyme
B) Aldosterone synthase
C) Renin
D) 11-beta hydroxylase
Renal artery stenosis causes hypoperfusion sensed by juxtaglomerular cells, which release renin — the rate-limiting first step that cleaves angiotensinogen to angiotensin I and initiates the RAAS cascade (renovascular hypertension, the "R" in RENAL).

A 30-year-old woman with resistant hypertension is found to have hypokalemia and metabolic alkalosis. Plasma aldosterone is elevated with suppressed renin. This is most consistent with which cause of secondary hypertension?

A) Primary hyperaldosteronism
B) Pheochromocytoma
C) Renal artery stenosis
D) Coarctation of the aorta
Elevated aldosterone with suppressed renin, hypokalemia, and metabolic alkalosis is classic for primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn syndrome), the most common endocrine cause of secondary hypertension.

Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE), which converts angiotensin I to angiotensin II, is primarily produced by which tissue?

A) Adrenal cortex
B) Juxtaglomerular cells
C) Hepatocytes
D) Pulmonary vascular endothelium
ACE is expressed predominantly on pulmonary (and other vascular) endothelium, where circulating angiotensin I is converted to the active vasoconstrictor angiotensin II.

7. Hypertensive Vascular Disease

Benign vs. Malignant Hypertension

Benign (Chronic) HypertensionMalignant Hypertension
Frequency~90–95% of hypertensives~5% — minority, but a medical emergency
Blood pressureStage 1–2, stable over yearsOften >200/120 mmHg, rapid rise
Vessel lesionHyaline arteriolosclerosisHyperplastic arteriolosclerosis + fibrinoid necrosis
CourseCan develop on top of pre-existing benign HTNRenal failure, retinal hemorrhage/papilledema, encephalopathy; death within 1–2 years if untreated

Vessel-Specific Effects of Chronic Hypertension

  • Large elastic arteries: accelerates atherosclerosis
  • Arterioles: hyaline arteriolosclerosis (pink hyaline material replacing the wall) → nephrosclerosis, diffuse glomerular scarring
  • Muscular arteries: medial thickening from increased collagen, elastic tissue, smooth muscle, fibroblasts
Board Tip Funduscopic exam ("look in the eyes") is a bedside window into malignant hypertension — retinal hemorrhage and papilledema reflect identical arteriolar pathology occurring systemically.
Practice Questions

Which of the following best distinguishes malignant hypertension from benign essential hypertension?

A) It represents a small minority of cases but is a medical emergency with rapid, severe pressure elevation and fibrinoid necrosis of arterioles
B) It is more common overall and rarely causes end-organ damage
C) It exclusively affects large elastic arteries
D) It is defined by Monckeberg medial calcific sclerosis
Malignant hypertension is the minority form (~5%) but is a true emergency — pressures often exceeding 200/120 with fibrinoid necrosis/hyperplastic arteriolosclerosis and rapid end-organ failure if untreated.

Fibrinoid necrosis of the arteriolar wall with intense immunofluorescence staining for fibrin is the pathologic hallmark of which condition?

A) Hyaline arteriolosclerosis
B) Monckeberg sclerosis
C) Fibromuscular dysplasia
D) Malignant hypertension
Malignant hypertension produces hyperplastic arteriolosclerosis with fibrinoid necrosis, which stains strongly for fibrin on immunofluorescence — distinguishing it from the benign hyaline form.

Chronic hypertension accelerates which vascular pathology in large elastic and muscular arteries?

A) Cystic medial degeneration exclusively
B) Atherosclerosis
C) Monckeberg medial calcific sclerosis
D) Vasculitis
Chronic hypertension is a major risk factor that accelerates atherosclerosis in large elastic and muscular arteries, in addition to its distinct effects on arterioles (hyaline/hyperplastic arteriolosclerosis).

8. Aneurysms & Aortic Dissection

Definitions

TermDefinition
True aneurysmLocalized dilation involving all three wall layers (intima, media, adventitia); may be fusiform (circumferential) or saccular (localized outpouching)
False (pseudo-) aneurysmBreach in the wall with a contained extravascular hematoma — not bounded by all three layers
DissectionBlood tracks through a tear into the media, splitting the wall along its length
Arteriovenous fistulaDirect artery-to-vein connection bypassing capillaries — may shunt large volumes → high-output cardiac failure

Two Most Common Causes of Aortic Aneurysm

  • Atherosclerosis — greatest factor in abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA); weakens the media by compressing/compromising its nutrient supply
  • Hypertension — most associated with ascending aortic aneurysm and with cystic medial degeneration
AAA rupture risk scales sharply with size: <4 cm minimal risk (observe); 4–5 cm ~1%/yr; 5–6 cm ~11%/yr; >6 cm ~25%/yr. Surgery is generally indicated above ~5–5.5 cm.

Connective Tissue Disorders & Aneurysm

Loeys-Dietz syndromeTGF-β receptor/SMAD3 mutations — increased TGF-β signaling; aneurysms rupture at smaller sizes → aggressive course

Other causes: tertiary syphilis (thoracic aortic aneurysm from obliterative vasa vasorum arteritis), cystic medial degeneration (hypertension), vasculitis.

Aortic Dissection — DeBakey Classification

TypeInvolvementNote

  • Begins as an intimal tear → blood dissects the media → can rupture externally or re-enter the lumen
Practice Questions

A 60-year-old smoker with known abdominal atherosclerosis presents with a pulsatile, tender periumbilical mass. Which type of aneurysm is most likely, and what is its most common underlying cause?

A) Thoracic aortic aneurysm; cystic medial degeneration
B) Berry aneurysm; congenital wall defect
C) Mycotic aneurysm; bacterial seeding
D) Abdominal aortic aneurysm; atherosclerosis
AAA is classically associated with atherosclerosis, presents as a pulsatile abdominal mass, and carries rupture risk that rises steeply with diameter.

A 25-year-old tall, thin man with a history of lens dislocation presents with acute chest pain radiating to the back. Imaging reveals an ascending aortic aneurysm with dissection. The underlying connective tissue defect most likely involves:

A) Fibrillin-1
B) Type III collagen
C) TGF-beta receptor 2
D) An elastin cross-linking enzyme
Marfan syndrome, caused by fibrillin-1 mutations, predisposes to cystic medial degeneration and ascending aortic aneurysm/dissection; classic features include tall stature, lens dislocation, and joint hypermobility.

A dissection confined to the descending aorta, distal to the origin of the left subclavian artery, is classified as which type in the Stanford system?

A) Stanford A
B) DeBakey I
C) Stanford B
D) DeBakey II
Dissections isolated to the descending aorta (distal to the left subclavian) are Stanford type B (equivalent to DeBakey III) and are often managed medically, unlike ascending (Stanford A) dissections which usually require surgery.

9. Venous Disease

Varicose Veins

TypeMechanismExample
PrimaryInherent weakness of the vessel wall (~43% familial)Lower extremity varicose veins
Secondary↑Back-pressure from venous obstruction, or wall weakening from inflammationEsophageal varices (portal hypertension/cirrhosis), hemorrhoids
  • Dilated, tortuous veins with poorly supported, often incompetent valves
  • Esophageal varices are invisible at autopsy (collapse after death) but can cause massive, fatal hemorrhage in life

Venous Thrombosis (DVT) & Virchow Triad

Virchow Triad: (1) Endothelial injury — trauma, surgery, catheters; (2) Stasis — immobility, prolonged travel, cardiac failure; (3) Hypercoagulability — pregnancy, malignancy, Factor V Leiden, oral contraceptives.
  • 90% of DVTs occur in the deep veins of the lower extremity (popliteal and above)
  • Insidious — few clear symptoms; requires a high index of clinical suspicion
  • Migratory thrombophlebitis (Trousseau sign): associated with visceral malignancy (pancreas, colon, lung) via tumor-driven hypercoagulability
  • Complication: pulmonary embolism — a large embolus lodging at the bifurcation is a saddle embolus, causing acute hypoxia and sudden death

Superior/Inferior Vena Cava Obstruction

  • IVC obstruction more common — classically from renal cell carcinoma tumor thrombus extending up the renal vein/IVC
  • Produces marked lower extremity edema, distended superficial abdominal collateral veins, and proteinuria (renal vein involvement)
Practice Questions

A patient with newly diagnosed pancreatic adenocarcinoma develops recurrent, migrating episodes of superficial vein tenderness and redness in different limbs over several weeks. This finding is best known as:

A) Virchow triad
B) Trousseau sign (migratory thrombophlebitis)
C) Peau d'orange
D) Raynaud phenomenon
Trousseau sign describes migratory superficial thrombophlebitis associated with an underlying hypercoagulable malignancy — classically pancreatic, colon, or lung cancer.

A bedridden postoperative patient develops unilateral calf swelling, warmth, and tenderness. Which component of Virchow triad is most directly implicated by prolonged immobility?

A) Endothelial injury
B) Hypercoagulability
C) Stasis
D) Hyperviscosity
Immobility produces venous stasis, one of the three components of Virchow triad (along with endothelial injury and hypercoagulability) that predisposes to venous thrombosis.

Esophageal varices most commonly arise as a complication of which underlying process?

A) Portal hypertension from cirrhosis
B) Primary venous wall weakness
C) Superior vena cava obstruction
D) Deep vein thrombosis
Esophageal varices are secondary varicosities resulting from portal hypertension (classically cirrhosis), which raises pressure in the portosystemic collateral veins.

10. Lymphatic Disease

Primary Lymphatic Disease

  • Congenital (rare): head/neck cystic hygroma, congenital lymphedema, cystic hygroma associated with Turner syndrome
  • Familial (Milroy disease): hereditary lymphatic hypoplasia, chronic limb swelling

Secondary Lymphatic Disease

  • Causes: filarial parasites, metastatic tumor obstructing lymphatics, irradiation, trauma, surgery (e.g., axillary lymph node dissection for breast cancer)
  • Increased interstitial fluid → interstitial fibrosis → skin becomes thickened, resembles peau d'orange (orange peel) or ulcerates
  • Extreme, disfiguring lower extremity lymphedema = elephantiasis (classically parasitic — filariasis)
Practice Questions

A woman develops chronic swelling and thickened, dimpled ("peau d'orange") skin of her arm several years after axillary lymph node dissection for breast cancer. This is best classified as:

A) Primary congenital lymphedema
B) Milroy disease
C) Secondary lymphedema
D) Cystic hygroma
Surgical lymph node dissection is a classic cause of secondary (acquired) lymphedema, producing chronic interstitial fibrosis and the characteristic peau d'orange skin change.

A newborn is found to have a large, multiloculated cystic neck mass composed of dilated lymphatic channels. This finding is most strongly associated with which chromosomal condition?

A) Trisomy 21
B) Turner syndrome
C) Klinefelter syndrome
D) Trisomy 13
Cystic hygroma (a congenital lymphatic malformation) in the neck is classically associated with Turner syndrome (45,X).

Filarial infection causing obstruction of lymphatic channels can result in massive, disfiguring lower extremity swelling known as:

A) Peau d'orange
B) Cystic hygroma
C) Lymphangitis
D) Elephantiasis
Chronic lymphatic obstruction from filariasis produces elephantiasis — massive, disfiguring lymphedema of the lower extremities.

Board Tip: "Pauci-immune" means little to no immune complex/antibody deposition is seen on immunofluorescence at the vessel wall despite active vasculitis — this describes the ANCA-associated vasculitides (GPA, MPA, EGPA), where injury is thought to be driven by ANCA-activated neutrophils rather than by immune complex deposition.
Practice Questions

Which term describes vasculitis with minimal immunoglobulin or complement deposition on immunofluorescence, despite active vessel wall inflammation, as is characteristically seen with ANCA-associated vasculitides?

A) Pauci-immune
B) Immune complex-mediated
C) Anti-basement membrane
D) Granulomatous
"Pauci-immune" (literally "few immune") describes vasculitis lacking significant immunoglobulin/complement deposits — the hallmark of ANCA-associated small vessel vasculitis (GPA, MPA, EGPA), distinguishing it from immune complex-mediated disease.

Which vasculitis is characterized by direct invasion of the vessel wall by an infectious organism, as opposed to immune-mediated injury?

A) Giant cell arteritis
B) Polyarteritis nodosa
C) Kawasaki disease
D) Syphilitic aortitis
Syphilitic (luetic) aortitis results from direct spirochetal invasion of the vasa vasorum and aortic wall — an example of infectious rather than immune-mediated vasculitis.

A vasculitis classified as affecting "small vessels" typically involves which of the following?

A) Aorta and its major branches
B) Arterioles, capillaries, and venules
C) Coronary arteries exclusively
D) Only large elastic arteries
Small-vessel vasculitides (e.g., GPA, MPA, EGPA, IgA vasculitis) affect arterioles, capillaries, and postcapillary venules, in contrast to large-vessel (aorta/branches) or medium-vessel (named muscular arteries) disease.

Board Tip Both are granulomatous, giant-cell-containing large-vessel vasculitides — age is the key discriminator (>50 = GCA, <50 = Takayasu).

Polyarteritis Nodosa (PAN)

  • Medium-vessel, segmental necrotizing vasculitis; lesions all at the same stage (unlike MPA)
  • No granulomas, ANCA-negative; classically associated with hepatitis B
  • Spares the lung; involves kidney, heart, GI tract, skin, peripheral nerves — mononeuritis multiplex (wrist/foot drop), palpable purpura
  • Angiography: multiple small aneurysms ("beads on a string")

Kawasaki Disease

  • Children, medium vessel — classically coronary arteries
  • Segmental necrosis, acute inflammation; predisposes to thrombosis and coronary artery aneurysm
  • Also known as mucocutaneous lymph node syndrome

Buerger Disease (Thromboangiitis Obliterans)

  • Young/middle-aged heavy smokers — strong association, especially Jewish, Japanese, and Indian populations
  • Segmental, thrombosing, acute and chronic inflammation of medium/small arteries of the extremities with microabscesses; may extend to adjacent veins/nerves
  • Angiography: corkscrew collateral vessels
  • Presents with severe distal ischemia, ulceration, gangrene — often affects the fingers used to hold cigarettes; smoking cessation is essential to halt progression

Raynaud Phenomenon vs. Raynaud Disease

  • Raynaud disease (primary): exaggerated vasospasm of small arteries/arterioles in young, otherwise healthy females in response to cold/emotion; no underlying structural disease; benign
  • Raynaud phenomenon (secondary): arterial insufficiency of the extremities from an underlying disease — SLE, scleroderma, atherosclerosis, Buerger disease — must be excluded before diagnosing primary Raynaud disease
  • Classic white → blue → red color sequence: white (vasospasm/ischemia) → blue (cyanosis from stasis/deoxygenated blood) → red (reactive hyperemia on rewarming)

Behçet Disease

  • Vasculitis affecting vessels of nearly all sizes, arteries and veins; classic triad: recurrent oral ulcers, genital ulcers, and uveitis
  • Associated with HLA-B51; higher incidence along the ancient Silk Road (Japan/China to Turkey/Iran); leading cause of blindness in some of these regions

16. Borderline & Malignant Vascular Neoplasms

Kaposi Sarcoma — Four Clinical Forms

FormPopulationCourse
Chronic (Classic)Older males of Eastern European/Mediterranean/Ashkenazi Jewish descentIndolent, distal extremity plaques/nodules; not HIV-associated
Lymphadenopathic (African/endemic)Young children in equatorial AfricaAggressive, involves lymph nodes/viscera; same geography as Burkitt lymphoma
Transplant-associatedSolid organ transplant + immunosuppressionCan be widely metastatic; may regress if immunosuppression is reduced

Histology: spindle cell proliferation forming slit-like vascular spaces with extravasated red cells — not a true sarcoma in the conventional sense despite the name.

Hemangioendothelioma

  • Borderline behavior — intermediate between benign hemangioma and angiosarcoma
  • Epithelioid hemangioendothelioma: occurs in medium/large veins; plump, epithelioid endothelial cells; ~40% recur, 20–30% metastasize, 15% die of disease

Angiosarcoma

  • Aggressive malignant endothelial tumor, older adults, no sex predilection, long latency
  • Associated with prior mastectomy/lymphedema, radiation, or exposure to arsenic, vinyl chloride, Thorotrast (classically liver angiosarcoma)
  • Deceptively small red nodules progress to large fleshy hemorrhagic masses
  • Immunohistochemistry: CD31, von Willebrand factor confirm endothelial origin (varying differentiation makes diagnosis difficult on morphology alone)

Hemangiopericytoma (Solitary Fibrous Tumor Family)

  • Rare; retroperitoneum, lower thigh; very slow-growing (diagnosis may take ~20 years)
  • Classic "staghorn" branching vascular pattern — but tumor cells lie outside the endothelium-lined vessels (unlike angiosarcoma)
  • CD31 negative (distinguishes from angiosarcoma, which is CD31 positive); silver stain highlights reticulin sheath around each tumor cell
  • Angiography: spider-like branching vessels
  • Prognosis depends on grade; ~75% 5-year survival, but long-term outcome variable
Board Tip CD31-positive spindle/epithelioid cells forming vascular spaces → think angiosarcoma/Kaposi (true endothelial tumors). CD31-negative cells surrounding — but not forming — a staghorn vascular pattern → think hemangiopericytoma.
