1. Balanced Ligamentous Tension / Ligamentous Articular Strain (BLT/LAS)

Case Connection JV is a 52-year-old male with 6 weeks of right hip pain that started after beginning a new job requiring lots of walking and stairs. Pain occasionally radiates down his leg but there's no numbness, tingling, or weakness. He wants an alternative to the NSAIDs he's been taking.

What Is BLT/LAS?

Balanced ligamentous tension (BLT) and ligamentous articular strain (LAS) are techniques designed to address somatic dysfunction of the ligamentous structures surrounding a joint. They relieve strain and balance tension abnormalities associated with, or causing, certain somatic dysfunctions.

Origins: The concepts originated with Dr. Still, were further developed by Dr. Sutherland, and were documented in a 1949 paper by Drs. Howard and Rebecca Lippincott. Two study groups — one in New England, one in Texas — interpreted the treatments in that paper and taught them as BLT and LAS respectively; the geographic naming distinction persists today, though they're treated as one technique.

Focus on ligamentous balance: this technique emphasizes ligaments, rather than muscular leverage, as the primary means of reducing somatic dysfunction. The goal is to bring the joint back to proper alignment, with ligaments exerting just enough tension to balance the weakened elements of the ligamentous structure against the healthy ones — this balance point is called balanced tension.

Section 1.1

Q1. What anatomic structure does BLT/LAS primarily focus on to reduce somatic dysfunction?

BLT/LAS emphasizes ligaments — rather than muscular leverage — as the primary means of reducing somatic dysfunction, bringing the joint back into alignment at a point of balanced tension.
Section 1.1 — Vignette

Q2. A 45-year-old woman with chronic low back pain is treated by finding a point where the ligaments surrounding a lumbar facet joint are under equal tension in all planes, then holding that position passively until a release is palpated. The patient does not actively contract any muscles during treatment. Which of the following techniques is being described?

This describes BLT/LAS: a passive technique that finds a point of balanced ligamentous tension and holds it until release, without requiring the patient to actively contract muscles (which would instead describe muscle energy technique).

How Does BLT/LAS Work?

BLT/LAS functions by balancing the tension among the ligaments supporting a joint, resetting the joint's normal proprioceptive feedback. When motion is carried beyond the normal range, tension becomes unbalanced, which can lead to locking of the articular mechanism and TART changes.

Healthy Joint Function

In a healthy joint, all ligaments maintain balanced tension to properly align the joint and allow physiologic motion. During motion, the associated ligaments remain constantly taut, with no change in tensile forces.

Injury and Dysfunction

In injury or dysfunction, something disrupts the joint, causing one or more ligaments to strain and lose their natural tensile forces — some become more lax than usual, others more taut than usual. This imbalance alters the joint's physiologic motion.

Tensegrity and Joint Stability

Tensegrity (tensional integrity) describes individual units arranged and suspended by a continuous tension network — analogous to vertebral segments (the units) connected by muscles and ligaments (the tension network). Under this principle, each component experiences equal tension, allowing perfect alignment and motion; imbalances disrupt the entire structure and force compensatory changes elsewhere.

Treatment Mechanism

Because ligaments can't stretch or contract like muscles, their tension varies only in limited ways:

  • Tension among a joint's ligaments is normally evenly distributed.
  • During physiologic motion, ligament relationships change as the joint changes position, but total system tension stays constant.
  • Joint injury or inflammation causes somatic dysfunction and a tension imbalance.

BLT/LAS aims to restore equal tension to the ligamentous structures surrounding the joint, realign it, and facilitate the restoration of tensegrity and ligament healing.

This is a classic tensegrity model — individual rigid units (analogous to vertebral segments) suspended by a continuous tension network (analogous to muscles/ligaments), where disturbing one unit forces compensatory changes throughout the system.

When Is BLT/LAS Indicated? Contraindications & Safety

BLT/LAS has a wide range of uses, including joint laxity/instability, joint dysfunction secondary to chronic surrounding muscle hypertonicity, and joint dysfunction following common strains or sprains.

Indications

  • Joint dysfunction: restricted motion, misalignment, asymmetry — commonly the spine, pelvis, and extremities.
  • Muscle tension and hypertonicity: restoring ligamentous tension indirectly influences muscle tone.
  • Pain management: resolving underlying somatic dysfunction can decrease inflammation and pain.
  • Limited range of motion: beneficial for joint stiffness, immobility, and overuse syndromes.
  • Postural abnormalities: correcting ligamentous imbalances that contribute to poor posture.

Contraindications & Safety

Relative contraindications: acute fractures, open wounds, soft tissue or bony infections, abscesses, deep venous thrombosis (embolism risk), anticoagulation, disseminated or focal neoplasm, recent post-operative conditions over the treatment site (wound dehiscence risk), and aortic aneurysm.

Pearl: BLT/LAS is a generally safe technique with no absolute contraindications other than lack of patient consent.

BLT/LAS is indicated for joint dysfunction, muscle tension and hypertonicity, pain management, limited range of motion, and correction of postural abnormalities — addressing somatic dysfunctions of the spine, pelvis, extremities, and elsewhere.

Since the lumbar dysfunction is distant from the abdominal incision here, treatment may still be reasonable, but the surgical site itself should be avoided as a treatment area.

How Is BLT/LAS Performed?

For COMLEX purposes, this technique is primarily classified as indirect, though it can be performed using both direct and indirect methods depending on training. Not all schools teach it as strictly one or the other — some simply focus on finding a point of balanced tension.

General Protocol

The affected ligaments are placed in a position of equal tension in all appropriate planes. For a vertebral dysfunction, this could mean positioning the superior segment over the inferior segment to reach a balance point, then waiting for a release in tissue tension.

Alternative Three-Step Approach

  1. Step 1 — Disengage: compress or decompress the patient's joint to maximize motion of the joint and associated tissues (like pushing in a car's clutch to shift gears).
  2. Step 2 — Exaggerate: mobilize the joint and tissues into the position of ease until a balance point is obtained in all planes of motion.
  3. Step 3 — Balance: maintain the joint and tissues in this position of ease until the tissues release.
Mnemonic: "DEB likes her BLT sandwich" — Disengage, Exaggerate, Balance. The entire technique can be as quick as 30 seconds.

Specific Example: Treating the Pelvis

  1. Disengage: with the patient supine, place your palms over each ASIS. Apply posterior and medial compression to gap the SI joints posteriorly, just until tissues engage.
  2. Exaggerate: maintaining pressure through the ASIS to the SI joints, move the innominates in all planes of motion — into the barrier (direct) or into the ease (indirect) — via anterior/posterior rotation, medial/lateral movement, or superior/inferior motion, to reach a point of balanced tension.
  3. Balance: hold this position until you feel the tissues release.

It's also passive — the patient doesn't need to actively participate.

2. Approach to Lymphatic Techniques

Case Connection SL is a 34-year-old, 26 weeks pregnant, with progressively worsening bilateral hand numbness/pain and finger cramping, consistent with pregnancy-related carpal tunnel syndrome. Exam shows swelling in her hands and feet.

What Are Lymphatic Techniques?

Lymphatic techniques are a cornerstone of osteopathic medicine aimed at optimizing lymphatic system function. The lymphatic system (lymphatic capillaries, lymphangions, and a one-way valve mechanism) transports excess fluid from the interstitium into lymphatic vessels and back to the venous system — vital for preventing cellular waste buildup.

One key application is managing conditions like lymphedema. These methods operate within the respiratory-circulatory model of osteopathic treatment, targeting somatic dysfunctions that restrict venous and lymphatic return.

Lymphatic techniques fall into two categories:

  • Those that alleviate restrictions to lymphatic circulation.
  • Those that promote and augment lymph flow.

Effective treatment typically combines both approaches to optimize the lymphatic system's functional capacity.

Section 2.1

Q14. What is the main purpose of lymphatic techniques in osteopathic medicine?

Lymphatic techniques aim to enhance lymphatic system function by promoting movement and drainage of lymphatic fluid, preventing the buildup of cellular waste and promoting overall health — operating within the respiratory-circulatory model.
Section 2.1 — Vignette

Q15. A physician chooses to treat a patient's peripheral edema using techniques designed to alleviate restrictions to lymphatic circulation and directly augment lymph flow, rather than using techniques based on cranial rhythmic impulse or the neurologic model. Which osteopathic model best describes this framework?

Lymphatic techniques operate within the respiratory-circulatory model of osteopathic treatment, targeting somatic dysfunctions that restrict venous and lymphatic return.

How Do Lymphatic Techniques Work?

Removing Impediments to Lymph Flow

Enhancing Respiration and Diaphragm Mechanics: the diaphragm generates pressure variations within the abdominal and thoracic cavities during breathing. As the diaphragm descends on inhalation, it pressurizes the abdomen, pulling lymphatic fluid into lymphatic capillaries from the interstitial spaces. As it ascends on exhalation, abdominal pressure drops, propelling lymphatic fluid superiorly toward the venous system.

Manual Techniques: thoracic inlet release, doming the respiratory diaphragm, and pelvic diaphragm release target horizontal restrictions to lymphatic drainage. Other techniques include popliteal drainage, pectoral lift, and cervical drainage.

Pearl: lymphatic techniques are distinct from, but complementary to, myofascial release and other soft tissue techniques — MFR reduces overall tissue tension (TART) and creates a more favorable environment for lymphatic circulation, while lymphatic techniques specifically promote lymph flow.

Promoting Lymph Flow

Some techniques promote and augment lymph flow/drainage and provide immune support when focused on specific organs (e.g., liver, spleen). Rather than removing impediments, these treatments stimulate the immune system and help patients recover from specific conditions.

Thoracic Pump Technique: compresses the ribcage/thoracic region to create a negative intrathoracic pressure gradient. Gentle rhythmic compression during inhalation/exhalation creates pressure changes that influence drainage of lymphatic structures above and below the thoracic diaphragm.

Pedal Pump Technique: passive rhythmic dorsiflexion/plantarflexion of the foot and ankle. Alternately contracting/relaxing the calf muscles induces pressure changes within lymphatic vessels (especially the lower extremity), propelling lymphatic fluid upward for reabsorption into the circulatory system.

Section 2.2

Q16. During inhalation, how does diaphragmatic descent affect lymphatic fluid?

As the diaphragm descends on inhalation, it exerts pressure on the abdominal contents, creating a favorable pressure gradient that pulls lymphatic fluid into the lymphatic capillaries from the interstitial spaces.
Section 2.2

Q17. Which technique involves passive rhythmic dorsiflexion/plantarflexion of the foot and ankle to propel lymphatic fluid upward?

The pedal pump technique uses passive rhythmic dorsiflexion/plantarflexion, alternately contracting and relaxing the calf muscles to induce pressure changes in the lower extremity's lymphatic vessels and propel fluid upward.
Section 2.2 — Vignette

Q18. A physician performs gentle rhythmic compressions on a supine patient's ribcage, timed with the patient's breathing, to generate a negative intrathoracic pressure gradient. Which mechanism is primarily responsible for the therapeutic effect of this technique?

This describes the thoracic pump technique: rhythmic compression of the ribcage during inhalation/exhalation creates pressure fluctuations within the thoracic cavity that influence lymphatic drainage of structures both above and below the thoracic diaphragm.

When Are Lymphatic Techniques Indicated? Contraindications & Safety

Indications

  • Reduced lymph flow: edema, tissue congestion, lymphatic stasis.
  • Infections: draining lymph nodes involved in fighting pneumonia, bronchitis, sinusitis, otitis media, pharyngitis — supporting immune response.
  • Inflammation: facilitating removal of inflammatory substances.
  • Fluid imbalance: heart failure or pregnancy, used cautiously within the respiratory-circulatory model.
  • Somatic dysfunctions: where muscle tightness or other issues hinder lymphatic function.

Contraindications

  • Acute indurated lymph nodes (do not treat directly)
  • Untreated cancer (risk of spread)
  • Liver or spleen friability (e.g., mononucleosis, hepatitis)
  • Anuria (fluid volume increase risks end-organ damage unless the urinary system/diuretics can remove it)
  • Acute or chronic infections (abscess, necrotizing fasciitis, high fever) — risk of dissemination
  • Acute bone fractures, especially open (infection risk)
  • Circulatory disorders (venous obstruction, hemorrhage, embolism)
  • Coagulopathies or unstable cardiac conditions — approach with caution
Pearl: lymphatic techniques generally are considered safe with no known complications, but require patient consent and cooperation, and adequate evaluation to rule out contraindications first.
Section 2.3

Gray discoloration, crepitus (crackling under palpation), severe pain, and systemic toxicity (tachycardia, lightheadedness) are classic red flags for necrotizing fasciitis — an acute, aggressive infection that is an absolute contraindication to lymphatic treatment due to the high risk of disseminating the infection through the lymphatic system.

How Are Lymphatic Techniques Performed?

There is a specific, four-step sequence for proper implementation:

1. Open Thoracic Inlet

The thoracic inlet (superior thoracic aperture) is the opening above the first rib, anterior to the trapezius muscle. "Opening" it means relaxing the fascia, tissue, and musculature surrounding the thoracic and right lymphatic ducts (common techniques: myofascial release, BLT). Because the inlet commonly obstructs flow from elsewhere in the body, opening it first is critical so later treatments can properly drain. Note: if there's a first rib dysfunction (especially inhaled), it should also be addressed.

2. Treat the Diaphragms

The body has five diaphragms; the thoracic inlet is one of them (already addressed in step 1). This step targets the other four, maximizing normal diaphragmatic motion to enhance interstitial fluid reabsorption — improving circulation and increasing fluid reabsorption into the lymphatic system. Myofascial release and soft tissue techniques are commonly used, focusing on the diaphragms' anatomical attachments/origins where congestion occurs.

3. Mobilize Local Congestion

Targets specific areas of fluid accumulation/stagnation to improve tissue fluid dynamics. Drainage techniques (popliteal fossa, cubital fossa) and mesenteric lifts (relaxing myofascia around mesenteric lymphatic vessels) are used. Important: work proximal to distal so lymph drains toward the thoracic and right lymphatic ducts.

4. Enhance Lymph Flow with Pump Techniques

Gentle, rhythmic pump/lift techniques move fluid beyond its normal flow rate, mobilizing fluid from tissues into the lymphaticovenous system — creating pressure gradients that direct drainage toward the thoracic duct and right lymphatic duct.

Section 2.4

3. Still Technique

Case Connection MK is a 22-year-old male with 3 months of dull, achy shoulder pain and no recalled injury. He denies radiation, numbness, tingling, or weakness. Exam shows AC joint tenderness and restricted active range of motion, likely from overuse playing baseball.

What Is Still Technique?

Still technique addresses somatic dysfunction through a combination of indirect (starting in a position of ease) and direct (ending with movement through the restrictive barrier) positioning. It's a passive technique requiring no active patient participation.

Treatment begins by positioning the restricted tissue into its direction of ease, then applying a facilitating force (usually compression). That force is maintained while transitioning from indirect to direct positioning, ultimately moving through the restrictive barrier. It's similar to facilitated positional release but adds a direct-treatment component of moving into/through the restrictive barrier.

Origins: developed by Dr. Andrew Taylor Still, founder of osteopathy, and outlined (incompletely) in his book Osteopathy Research and Practice. In 1996, Richard L. Van Buskirk, DO, discovered manuscripts with more detailed descriptions, allowing him to recreate the method for treating the entire body.

Section 3.1

Q27. Which statement best describes what makes Still technique unique compared to a purely indirect technique like facilitated positional release?

Still technique is both indirect and direct: it starts in a position of ease (indirect) and finishes by moving through the restrictive barrier (direct) while maintaining a vector force — distinguishing it from purely indirect techniques.
Section 3.1 — Vignette

Q28. A physician performs a technique on a restricted thoracic vertebral segment: the segment is first moved into its position of ease, a gentle compressive force is applied, and the segment is then carried through neutral and past the restrictive barrier while that force is maintained — all without requiring the patient to actively participate. Which technique is this, and how does it differ from HVLA?

This is Still technique: passive, combining an indirect starting position with a direct finish, using a sustained low-force vector (≤5 lb) rather than the rapid, high-velocity/low-amplitude thrust characteristic of HVLA.

How Does Still Technique Work?

Still technique involves three main components:

  • Vector force: applied as compression or traction, acting as a lever to guide targeted tissues through their range of motion. The key is directing the force through the dysfunctional tissue and maintaining it while transitioning from indirect to direct positioning. Uses no more than 5 pounds of pressure.
  • Palpating hand/finger: plays a monitoring role, assessing the treated tissues to gather information about the vector force and positioning.
  • Operating hand: positions tissues, introduces the vector force, and orchestrates controlled movement throughout the entire range of motion from indirect to direct and past the restrictive barrier.

Still technique starts at a position of ease, where neurologic protective mechanisms are minimally activated and myofascial tissue is fully relaxed. The vector force resets neuro-fascial imbalances that triggered the somatic dysfunction. Applied in a normalizing direction, it restores balance and frees the tissue from its restrictive barrier — this indirect-to-direct transition relieves tension, allowing the tissue to move through the restriction and enhancing mobility/function.

Section 3.2

Q29. Which statement correctly describes the role of the palpating hand/finger in Still technique?

The palpating hand/finger is the monitoring component — it assesses the treated tissue to gather information about the vector force and positioning, while the separate operating hand actually introduces the force and moves the tissue.
Section 3.2

Q30. What is the maximum amount of pressure typically used for the vector force in Still technique?

The vector force in Still technique — whether compression or traction — should use no more than about 5 pounds of pressure, directed toward the dysfunctional joint or tissue.
Section 3.2 — Vignette

Q31. During Still technique, a physician uses one hand solely to feel for tissue relaxation and gather feedback about the treatment, while the other hand actually repositions the joint and applies the compressive force. Which two components of Still technique does this describe, respectively?

The monitoring hand is the palpating hand/finger, which assesses tissue response and gathers information about the vector force and positioning. The hand that repositions the joint and introduces the force is the operating hand.
Section 3.2 — Vignette

Q32. Still technique begins by placing the dysfunctional tissue into a position of ease before any force is applied. What is the primary physiologic rationale for starting in this position?

Starting at a position of ease minimizes activation of neurologic protective mechanisms and allows myofascial tissue to fully relax — this relaxed starting point is what allows the subsequent vector force to reset the neuro-fascial imbalance driving the dysfunction.

When Is Still Technique Indicated? Contraindications & Safety

Still technique is particularly effective for decreased range of motion in a joint or tissue, and can be used for nearly any somatic dysfunction because it's quick, gentle, and efficient. It can be performed seated or supine, expanding the patient populations it's suitable for.

Common Indications

  • Somatic dysfunction involving joints, muscles, or fascia
  • Patients with mild to moderate pain who cannot tolerate more aggressive techniques
  • Older adults or those with fragile conditions
  • Scenarios where rapid resolution of dysfunction is beneficial (e.g., time-constrained clinical settings)

Contraindications & Safety

Generally safe and well tolerated, but use caution in patients with acute pain or symptom exacerbation in the positions required for treatment. Contraindications for the region to be treated include:

  • Acute fracture
  • Inflamed or infected joints
  • Severe osteoporosis
  • Vascular compromise and vertebrobasilar insufficiency
Section 3.3

Septic arthritis is an infected joint — a direct contraindication to Still technique at that site. The other options describe chronic, non-infectious, non-fracture musculoskeletal complaints where Still technique would be appropriate.

Severe osteoporosis is explicitly listed as a contraindication to Still technique, given the fragility of the bone and joint structures involved — even though Still technique is otherwise noted as useful in older or fragile patients in general, severe osteoporosis at the treatment site is a specific exception.

How Is Still Technique Performed?

The dysfunctional joint is moved into a position of ease, and an appropriate amount of compression (rarely traction), usually about 5 pounds, is added. The physician maintains the compression vector force while moving the segment back toward neutral and then through the restrictive barrier, continually adjusting the direction of the vector to keep it directed toward the dysfunctional joint. After moving through the restrictive barrier, the vector force is slowly released and the patient returned to neutral.

Posterior/exhaled 4th rib (ribs 2-12) describes rib 4 stuck in a backward, depressed (exhaled) position, unable to fully move into inhalation. It can cause pain, restricted ROM, and breathing difficulties; treatment aims to restore normal rib movement.

The Complete Seven Steps

  1. Diagnose: assess the joint or tissue for motion preference and restriction.
  2. Disengage the barrier: place the joint/tissue into a position of maximal ease, away from the restriction (or toward the preferred motion).
  3. Exaggerate: exaggerate the indirect position in all three planes until tissue relaxation is felt on the monitoring hand/finger.
  4. Apply vector force: introduce a vector force with the operating hand (compression or traction), about 5 pounds, directed toward the affected joint/tissue.
  5. Mobilize: use the operating hand to move the joint/tissue slowly toward neutral and then into the restrictive barrier (direct positioning), maintaining the vector force toward the joint/tissue.
  6. Release and reposition: release the vector force and passively return the joint/tissue to neutral.
  7. Reassess: check whether improvement is adequate.
Pearl: Still technique can be repeated as necessary until improved mobility is achieved.
Section 3.4

Q36. Which sequence correctly lists the steps for performing Still technique, in order?

The correct order is: Diagnose → Disengage the barrier → Exaggerate → Apply vector force → Mobilize (toward neutral and through the restrictive barrier) → Release and reposition → Reassess.
Section 3.4

Q37. After correctly diagnosing the dysfunction, what is the next step in Still technique?

After diagnosing the joint or tissue's motion preference and restriction, the next step is to disengage the barrier — placing the tissue into a position of maximal ease, away from the restriction.

After applying the vector force, the next step is to mobilize: using the operating hand to move the joint/tissue slowly toward neutral and then through the restrictive barrier (the direct component), all while maintaining the vector force directed toward the dysfunctional segment.
