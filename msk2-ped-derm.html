00 · Overview & Approach

Lecture Objectives

Dr. Stephens' “Top 21 Pediatric Dermatology Findings”

Two learning objectives drive this whole lecture: (1) review the most common pediatric rashes, and (2) learn the keyword descriptions used in charting and on exams, so you recognize the diagnosis fast when you see or hear those buzzwords.

A resident's-eye view of the scopeDr. Stephens' own dermatology atlas from residency had ~800 photos; the newest edition has over 1,000. He's explicit that no medical student is expected to know even half that book — this lecture picks out only what you're likely to see in practice or on a test.
Explicitly excludedFindings associated with non-accidental trauma (child abuse) are not covered here — that's a separate lecture entirely and outside the scope of this one.
Practice Question
What is explicitly excluded from this lecture's scope?

Study Strategy

Group by category, not as one flat list of 21

Group findings by category (vaccine-preventable vs. non-vaccine infectious vs. congenital vs. “other”) rather than memorizing all 21 as a flat list — the categories themselves are testable and help you narrow a differential fast.

Closing advice, applied earlyAs you go through rotations, look at as many real rashes as possible, and specifically try to see how rashes present across different skin tones — most textbook photos underrepresent darker skin, and presentations vary.

01 · Measles

Measles

Vaccine-preventable — and re-emerging
  • Paramyxoviridae virus
  • Macular-papular rash starting behind the ears and spreading to the entire body over 3–4 days
  • Becomes blotchy and peeling by week 2
  • Koplik spots — described as “grains of salt” — found bilaterally on the buccal mucosa
  • Highly contagious, spreads very quickly
Current relevance (2025)Measles is entirely vaccine-preventable, yet the U.S. was experiencing a major outbreak in 2025, with at least one death from this preventable disease that year.
From the lecturer's own experienceDr. Stephens saw measles clinically only once, during residency, in an unvaccinated immigrant population — reinforcing how rare it should be with adequate vaccination coverage.
Practice Question
A child has a macular-papular rash that started behind the ears and small white “grains of salt” spots on the bilateral buccal mucosa. What is the diagnosis and what are the buccal spots called?
Practice Question
By what timeframe does the measles rash typically become blotchy and start peeling?

02 · Mumps

Mumps

Painful parotid swelling is the buzzword
  • Painful parotid gland swelling — classically unilateral or bilateral facial swelling
  • Fever
  • Headache
  • Loss of appetite
  • Can also cause swelling of other glands, including orchitis and the axillary lymph nodes
Key buzzword“Painful parotid gland swelling with fever” — this phrase alone should trigger mumps on boards.
Practice Question
A child presents with fever, headache, poor appetite, and painful unilateral facial swelling over the parotid gland. What is the diagnosis?
Practice Question
Besides the parotid gland, what other structures can mumps cause swelling in?

03 · Rubella & Congenital Rubella

Rubella (Postnatal)

The vaccine-preventable disease Dr. Stephens has never actually seen
  • RNA Togavirus
  • Classic pink macular-papular rash
  • Starts on the face, then spreads to the entire body
  • Sub-occipital and post-auricular lymphadenopathy — classic associated finding
From the lecturer's own experienceDr. Stephens specifically notes he has never seen a case of postnatal rubella in clinical practice — unlike measles and mumps — reflecting how well vaccination has controlled it.
Practice Question
What lymphadenopathy pattern is classically associated with (postnatal) rubella?

Congenital Rubella

Blueberry muffin baby
  • Causes “blueberry muffin baby” — a skin condition with widespread bluish-purple macules from extramedullary hematopoiesis
  • Affected infants also appear notably yellow (jaundiced) from the associated systemic illness
High-yield pairing“Blueberry muffin baby” + jaundice = congenital rubella syndrome — a classic boards image-recognition pairing.
Practice Question
A newborn presents with widespread bluish-purple macules described as a “blueberry muffin” appearance, along with jaundice. What is the diagnosis?

04 · Meningococcal Rash

Meningococcal Rash

A true pediatric emergency
  • Non-blanching petechiae or purpura
  • Widespread hemorrhagic red-brown rash
  • Diagnosis via blood culture or PCR
Blanching bedside trickIf you're unsure whether a rash is blanching, press a clear glass firmly against the skin — you can see through it whether the rash disappears (blanches) and returns, or not.
Why this is an emergencyMeningococcal rash is a very bad finding — affected children can crash within hours. High clinical suspicion and rapid action are essential.
From the lecturer's own experienceDr. Stephens notes that thanks to vaccination, he has never personally seen meningococcal meningitis or the meningococcal rash — and hopes never to.
Practice Question
A febrile child has a widespread, hemorrhagic, red-brown rash that does not blanch with pressure. What is the most urgent concern?
Practice Question
What bedside technique can help determine whether a rash is blanching?

05 · Varicella Infection (Chicken Pox)

Varicella (Chicken Pox)

“New crops” every 1–2 days
  • Small, pruritic, fluid-filled blisters
  • Rash lasts 5–10 days, with new blisters (new crops) appearing every 1–2 days
  • Usually self-limiting with minimal side effects, but can become systemic — especially dangerous in younger children, with risk of severe disability and death
  • Breaking blisters can scab and lead to permanent scarring
Historical differentiator“New crops” of blisters (lesions in different stages simultaneously — macules, papules, vesicles, crusts all at once) is the classic distinguishing feature historically used to tell chicken pox apart from smallpox, which produced lesions all in the same stage.
A personal anecdote from the lecturerWhile working in a school's sheet metal department during a lab remodel, Dr. Stephens' professor opened an old freezer and immediately recognized preserved smallpox specimens inside — smallpox is eradicated in nature but still exists in select labs and has been considered a potential bioweapon. Thankfully, in practice today you'll only ever see chicken pox.
Vaccine bonusVaricella vaccination also protects against developing shingles (herpes zoster) later in life, since the virus can reactivate from dorsal root ganglia after primary infection.
Practice Question
A child has small, itchy, fluid-filled blisters in several different stages of development at once — some fresh, some crusted. What historical diagnostic feature does this “new crops” pattern help distinguish chicken pox from?
Practice Question
What long-term benefit does the varicella vaccine provide beyond preventing chicken pox itself?

06 · Erythema Marginatum

Erythema Marginatum

A Jones major criterion, rarely actually seen
  • One of the Jones criteria for rheumatic fever — specifically a major criterion
  • Follows Strep pyogenes infection, classically 2–4 weeks prior, usually untreated
  • Patients often report a preceding sore throat they “didn't think much of”
From the lecturer's own experienceDr. Stephens notes that in his entire career he has seen only two cases of rheumatic fever, and neither had erythema marginatum — reinforcing that it's fairly rare even when rheumatic fever itself occurs, but it remains one of the major diagnostic criteria tested on boards.
Clinical patternAsk about a recent sore throat in any patient with a new rash plus joint symptoms — a missed or undertreated strep throat 2–4 weeks earlier is the setup for rheumatic fever.
Practice Question
Erythema marginatum is a major criterion for which diagnosis?
Practice Question
How long after a Strep pyogenes infection does erythema marginatum classically appear, and what is the typical antecedent history?

07 · Impetigo

Impetigo

Honey-colored crusting, and a rash Dr. Stephens sees constantly
  • Classically caused by Staph aureus or Strep pyogenes
  • Red sores with honey-colored, fluid-filled vesicles — the classic description
  • Progresses to skin breakdown with oozing and sometimes bleeding, especially with scratching
  • Topical antibiotics are first-line treatment — must be effective against staph
Real-world prevalenceThis is one of the rashes Dr. Stephens says he sees all the time in clinical practice — high real-world prevalence in addition to being board-relevant.
Practice Question
A child has red sores on the face with honey-colored, fluid-filled vesicles. What is the diagnosis and first-line treatment?

08 · Scarlet Fever

Scarlet Fever

Strawberry tongue and sandpaper skin
  • Caused by Strep pyogenes infection
  • Strawberry tongue
  • Fine erythematous rash with circumoral pallor (clearing right around the mouth)
  • Sandpaper-textured skin on palpation
Confirmed by touchDr. Stephens confirms clinically that the skin genuinely does feel like rough sandpaper when you run your hand over it — a tactile buzzword worth remembering for exam and boards.
Practice Question
A child with strep pharyngitis develops a strawberry tongue, a fine rash that feels like sandpaper, and pallor around the mouth. What is the diagnosis?
Practice Question
What does “circumoral pallor” refer to in scarlet fever?

09 · Erythema Infectiosum (Fifth Disease / Slap Cheek Disease)

Erythema Infectiosum

Parvovirus B19 and the “slapped cheek”
  • Classic bilateral red cheeks — looks like the child was slapped on both sides
  • Rash may then spread to the arms and extensor surfaces
  • Caused by Parvovirus B19 infection
  • Typical prodrome: runny nose, some cough, maybe low-grade fevers, then the bilateral cheek rash develops
Testable triad“Slapped cheek” appearance + a preceding mild URI prodrome + parvovirus B19 is the classic testable triad.
Practice Question
A child had a mild runny nose and cough last week, and now presents with bright red cheeks bilaterally that look like they were slapped, with rash spreading to the extensor arms. What is the causative organism?

10 · Hand, Foot, and Mouth Disease

Hand, Foot, and Mouth Disease

Dehydration, not the rash, is the real admission risk
  • Affects the hands, feet, and mouth as the name suggests
  • Erythematous macules and papules
  • One of the few viral exanthems that presents on the palms of the hands and soles of the feet
  • Self-limiting, usually lasting only 5–7 days
Where kids actually get into troubleThe mouth sores can become so severe that children refuse to drink and become dehydrated. Dr. Stephens notes that as a pediatric hospitalist, this — not the hand/foot findings — is the actual reason these kids get admitted.
Practice Question
A toddler with hand, foot, and mouth disease is refusing all oral intake due to painful mouth sores. What is the primary clinical concern that would prompt admission?
Practice Question
What distinguishes the distribution of hand, foot, and mouth disease from most other viral exanthems?

11 · Roseola Infantum

Roseola Infantum

The rash appears only after the fever breaks
  • High fever for 3–5 days — “high” meaning 103–104°F
  • Notably lacking other symptoms — no significant runny nose, vomiting, diarrhea, or ear pain
  • After the fever breaks, a body-wide macular-papular rash develops, starting on the chest and spreading outward
Why these kids get worked upBecause these children have persistent high fevers with no localizing source, they often undergo a sepsis workup (urine/blood cultures, sometimes a full sepsis workup depending on age) before the diagnosis becomes clear once the rash appears.
From the lecturer's own experienceDr. Stephens sees this “at least a couple times every year.” Interestingly, despite looking dramatic, the rash itself doesn't seem to bother the children at all and causes no scarring.
Practice Question
An infant has had a fever of 104°F for 4 days with no other symptoms, and a full sepsis workup was unremarkable. The fever then breaks and a macular-papular rash appears on the trunk, spreading outward. What is the diagnosis?
Practice Question
Why do children with roseola often undergo a sepsis workup before the diagnosis is apparent?

12 · Molluscum Contagiosum

Molluscum Contagiosum

Dome-shaped, umbilicated, and spread by scratching
  • Classically described as dome-shaped lesions with central indentation (umbilication)
  • Often flesh-colored; can become erythematous or break down with scratching
  • Common on the face, neck, axilla, and thighs, usually as multiple lesions
  • Self-limiting — resolves in a few months — but contagious, and scratching promotes further spread

Treatment

  • Blistering agents can be used to remove lesions
  • Generally, leaving mild/less severe cases alone is reasonable, especially in sensitive areas like around the eye
  • If widely spread or causing issues, referral to dermatology for further treatment
Practice Question
A child has multiple flesh-colored, dome-shaped papules on the trunk, each with a central indentation. What is the diagnosis?
Practice Question
Why does scratching molluscum lesions tend to worsen the overall clinical picture?

13 · Candida Dermatitis (Candida Diaper Rash)

Candida Dermatitis

Satellite lesions are the differentiator
  • Erythematous, non-blanching rash
  • Found in skin folds and flexures — the diaper area is classic because it's a wet, moist environment
  • Satellite lesions — smaller separate lesions surrounding a central area of spread — is the key distinguishing feature
Differentiating from plain diaper rashOrdinary diaper rash is contact irritation without satellite lesions. The presence of satellite lesions as candida spreads outward from a central area is what confirms a candidal etiology.
Practice Question
An infant has an erythematous rash in the diaper area with a central patch and smaller separate “satellite” lesions surrounding it. What does this finding indicate?

14 · Scabies

Scabies

A burrowing mite, and a household eradication challenge
  • Can affect all ages, not just children, but common enough in kids to include here
  • Caused by a tiny skin mite that burrows under the skin and lays eggs
  • Contagious, but usually requires prolonged contact — brief casual contact typically won't transmit it, but sharing clothes or close/sexual contact can
  • Found commonly in skin folds — classically the web spaces between fingers and toes and the inguinal area
Household eradication is difficultTo fully eradicate scabies you must starve the mites of all contact — in a large household this is hard to coordinate, and it commonly results in one child after another coming down with it as it passes around the family.
Practice Question
Why is scabies difficult to eradicate in a large household?
Practice Question
Where are scabies infestations most commonly found on physical exam?

15 · Tinea Corporis (Ringworm)

Tinea Corporis

A fungal infection, despite the name
  • Classically a circular, slightly raised, erythematous rash with central clearing
  • The central clearing pattern is where the historical (mistaken) idea of a “worm” causing it came from
  • Can affect any part of the skin, literally meaning “anywhere on the body”
  • Contagious with contact — hand washing is important, and it can spread human-to-surface-to-human, not just directly person-to-person
Practice Question
A circular, slightly raised, erythematous plaque with central clearing is found on a child's arm. What historical misconception led to this condition's common name?

16 · IgA Vasculitis (Henoch-Schönlein Purpura / HSP)

IgA Vasculitis (HSP)

Abdominal pain and arthritis can precede the rash
  • Palpable purpura with abdominal pain and arthritis
  • The purpura does not blanch
  • Normally self-limiting, but can affect the kidneys — kidney function should be monitored
From the lecturer's own experienceDr. Stephens has seen this multiple times, including twice in children who had undergone appendectomies — this was before routine CT scanning of every child with suspected appendicitis. Those children underwent surgery, then broke out in the rash post-operatively — illustrating that the abdominal pain and arthritis of HSP can actually precede the rash, leading to diagnostic confusion with a surgical abdomen.
Order of symptom onset can varyAbdominal pain and arthritis may show up before the purpuric rash appears, which is a key pitfall — don't rule out HSP just because there's no rash yet in a child with abdominal pain and joint symptoms.
Practice Question
A child develops non-blanching palpable purpura on the lower extremities along with abdominal pain and joint pain. What organ system should be monitored given the risk of involvement?
Practice Question
Why might a child with HSP be mistaken for having a surgical abdominal emergency like appendicitis?

17 · Atopic Dermatitis (Eczema)

Atopic Dermatitis (Eczema)

Secondary infection changes the treatment
  • A very common skin disorder in children
  • May be seasonal or triggered by specific factors like cold or certain detergents
  • As children get older, eczema can become secondarily infected from scratching
Treatment pitfallWhen eczema becomes secondarily infected, treating with topical steroids alone is not enough — antibiotics are also needed to clear the soft tissue infection.
Severity spectrumMost eczema can be managed without serious intervention, but some cases are severe enough to require dermatology referral.
Practice Question
A child with known eczema develops increased redness, warmth, and oozing at a chronically scratched patch. What is the appropriate treatment adjustment?

18 · Seborrheic Dermatitis (Cradle Cap)

Seborrheic Dermatitis (Cradle Cap)

The worst case of dandruff you've ever seen
  • Often found in young children — hence the common name “cradle cap”
  • Described as an erythematous rash with yellow, scaly flakes — like “the worst case of dandruff you've ever seen”
  • Normally develops within the first few weeks of life
  • Treated topically, and typically resolves by the fourth month
Practice Question
A 3-week-old infant has an erythematous scalp rash with yellow, greasy scaling. When would this condition typically be expected to resolve?

19 · Congenital Birthmarks: Port-Wine Stain, Infantile Hemangioma & Nevus Simplex

Three Vascular Birthmarks, Compared

Frequently confused with one another — the differences are the real testable skill

These three congenital vascular skin findings are grouped together because they're frequently confused with one another — understanding the differences between them is the real testable skill here.

Capillary Malformation (Port-Wine Stain)

  • Dark red or purple, flat birthmark — the most severe of the three
  • Commonly found on the face, chest, or back
  • Increases in size during puberty, pregnancy, or menopause
  • Some respond to laser therapy, but not all
Referral triggerAny child born with a large port-wine stain should be seen by dermatology.

Infantile Hemangioma (Strawberry Mark)

  • Raised birthmark, can occur anywhere on the body — including internally
  • Usually increases in size during the first six months of life, then shrinks and disappears by around age 7
  • A single hemangioma in a non-critical area is usually just watched — it will resolve on its own
  • Internal hemangiomas are the real concern — Dr. Stephens has personally seen them in the airway and the liver; multiple growing hemangiomas in an airway can become life-threatening
  • Initial treatment is with beta blockers, which reduce the size — surprising to many students since beta blockers aren't an obvious first thought for a vascular skin lesion; laser treatment is also an option

Nevus Simplex (Stork Bite / Angel's Kiss)

  • The least critical of the three
  • Flat, red mark commonly on the forehead, eyelids, or back of the neck
  • Most fade by age 1–2; they do not worsen or spread and cause no problems
  • Management is simple reassurance to the parents — and documenting it in the chart, since it can otherwise be mistaken for an injury
Severity ranking, most to least concerningPort-wine stain (needs dermatology referral if large) > infantile hemangioma (mostly benign, but screen for internal/airway involvement) > nevus simplex (reassurance only).
Practice Question

Practice Question

Practice Question

21 · Final Board Pearls

Lecturer's Own Wrap-Up

  • This lecture is intentionally not exhaustive — the goal was recognizing the keyword/buzzword descriptions most likely to appear in charting and on exams, not memorizing an entire dermatology atlas.
  • Vaccine-preventable diseases (measles, mumps, rubella, meningococcal disease, varicella) are increasingly relevant again given real-world outbreaks — know both the classic exam findings and that these are, by definition, preventable.
  • Several conditions hinge on a timing pattern: erythema marginatum (weeks after strep), roseola (rash after fever breaks), HSP (abdominal pain/arthritis can precede the rash) — pay attention to sequence, not just appearance.
  • Distinguishing similar-looking entities is a recurring board theme: candida dermatitis vs. plain diaper rash (satellite lesions), port-wine stain vs. infantile hemangioma vs. nevus simplex (flat vs. raised, severity, natural history).
  • Practice looking at rashes across different skin tones whenever you get the chance clinically — most textbook images underrepresent this variation.
Overarching test-taking frameworkFor any pediatric rash question, first ask (1) is this vaccine-preventable, (2) what organism/mechanism, and (3) what's the one buzzword phrase tied to it — most board questions can be answered with that three-step filter.
