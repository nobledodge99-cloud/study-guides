1. Fast Facts

  • Obstructive defect = FEV1/FVC ≤ 70%
  • Restrictive defect = TLC ≤ 80% predicted; FEV1/FVC is normal or increased
  • Post-bronchodilator FEV1 and age are the strongest mortality predictors in COPD
  • DLCO < 50% predicted predicts exercise oxygen desaturation
  • Asthmatic airways are 1000× more sensitive to bronchoconstricting agents than normal airways

2. Lung Volumes & Capacities (Objective 2)

Basic Volumes (directly measured, not derived)

VolumeDefinition
Tidal Volume (TV)Air inspired or expired with each normal breath
Inspiratory Reserve Volume (IRV)Max additional air inspired past the end of a normal inspiration
Expiratory Reserve Volume (ERV)Max additional air expired past the end of a normal expiration
Residual Volume (RV)Air remaining after maximal expiration — the only volume that cannot be measured by spirometry

Capacities (sums of ≥2 volumes)

CapacityDefinitionFormula
Total Lung Capacity (TLC)Volume in lungs at end of maximal inspirationTLC = RV + IRV + TV + ERV
Vital Capacity (VC)Max volume forcefully expelled after maximal inspirationVC = IRV + TV + ERV = TLC − RV
Functional Residual Capacity (FRC)Volume remaining after a normal expirationFRC = RV + ERV
Inspiratory Capacity (IC)Max volume inspired from end-expiratory position — least clinically significant of the fourIC = TV + IRV
RV measurement — Since RV can't be captured by spirometry, it (and anything containing it — FRC, TLC) requires one of: gas dilution (N2 washout, He dilution), body plethysmography, or radiographic measurement (CXR/CT).

3. Frequently Used Tests

  • A full PFT set = spirometry + helium lung volumes + DLCO; results are compared to predicted values based on height, age, sex, race
  • Sniff test (fluoroscopy) — assesses diaphragmatic movement/strength
  • Max inspiratory/expiratory pressure — patient inspires/expires against a closed valve to assess respiratory muscle strength
  • Exercise cardiopulmonary testing — differentiates cardiac vs. pulmonary causes of dyspnea, assesses functional capacity
  • Indications: evaluate dyspnea, evaluate known lung injury, monitor disease progression, assess response to therapy, preop risk assessment, prognostication
  • PFTs are not for routine screening of asymptomatic healthy patients (may have a role in smokers/at-risk populations for subclinical disease)
  • Daily spirometry is useful in managing acute asthma exacerbations

4. Obstruction — Interpretation

FEV1/FVC ≤ 70% → Obstructive defect
SeverityFEV1 (% predicted)
Mild≥ 80%
Moderate50% – <80%
Severe30% – <50%
Very Severe< 30%
  • TLC may be ↑ (hyperinflation); VC may be ↓ because RV is markedly enlarged (air trapping)
  • Flow-volume loop: concave expiratory limb, relatively normal inspiration
  • Common causes: COPD, asthma, cystic fibrosis, bronchiolitis obliterans

Bronchodilator Reversibility Testing

Positive = FEV1 or FVC ↑ ≥12% AND ≥200 mL absolute

Distinguishes reversible obstruction (asthma) from fixed obstruction (emphysema). Most bronchodilator-responders also improve clinically, but many non-responders in the lab still improve clinically — treatment decisions remain clinical, not purely PFT-driven.

Methacholine Challenge Testing

Positive = FEV1 ↓ ≥20% from baseline
  • Used when intermittent bronchospasm doesn't show up as a resting FEV1/FVC drop
  • Up to 50% of people with allergic rhinitis (no asthma symptoms) have a positive test — positive ≠ asthma
  • Negative predictive value ≈ 95% if no bronchodilators were used before testing

Flow-Volume Loops for Localization

  • Extrathoracic obstruction → truncated/plateaued inspiratory limb
  • Intrathoracic obstruction → truncated/plateaued expiratory limb
  • Comparing limb patterns differentiates fixed (both limbs plateaued) from variable obstruction
Age trap — FEV1/FVC normally declines with age; normal elderly patients without obstruction may sit below 70–80%. In this setting, compare against the predicted ratio for age, not the flat 70% cutoff, to avoid over-calling obstruction.

5. Restriction — Interpretation

TLC < 80% predicted → Restrictive defect
SeverityTLC (% predicted)
Mild65% – 80%
Moderate50% – 65%
Severe< 50%

Using RV/FRC to suggest a cause

↓ FRCNoncompliant lung OR chest wall pathology (morbid obesity, kyphoscoliosis)
RV normalChest wall disease
RV ↓Parenchymal lung disease, large pleural effusion
RV ↑Neuromuscular disease (e.g., ALS)
TLC, RV, FRC all ↓ proportionallyParenchymal lung disease (e.g., interstitial lung disease)
Board trap — A restrictive pattern with a normal FRC should raise suspicion for neuromuscular disease (RV mainly reflects airway closure, so it can rise even as VC falls from weak respiratory muscles, not shrinking lung tissue).
Spirometry alone is not enough — reduced FEV1/FVC can occur with severe obstruction too (FRC rises so much it limits IC and reduces VC). A reduced FVC only suggests restriction; you need measured lung volumes (TLC) to confirm true restrictive physiology.

Flow-volume loop shape is largely preserved but scaled down (smaller overall curve); volume-time curve shows a rapid upslope to a smaller vital capacity.

Common causes: fibrosis, sarcoidosis, muscular disease, chest wall deformities.

6. Mixed Defects (Objective 1)

  • Mixed obstructive + restrictive pattern = both criteria present simultaneously: FEV1/FVC ≤ 70% and TLC < 80% predicted
  • Cannot be diagnosed by spirometry (FEV1/FVC) alone — lung volumes (TLC) must be measured to confirm the restrictive component is real and not just hyperinflation-driven pseudo-restriction from severe obstruction

7. Flow-Volume Loops by Severity

Obstructive progression

  • Mild: curve descends faster than normal, mildly concave
  • Moderate → Severe: peak becomes sharper, expiratory flow drops precipitously (dynamic airway collapse of diseased, more compressible conducting airways)
  • Volume-time curve: slower, gradual upslope to max volume (vs. rapid rise in normal) → corresponds to prolonged forced expiratory time on exam

Restrictive

  • Loop shape largely normal but uniformly smaller
  • Volume-time curve: rapid upslope, but plateaus at a smaller vital capacity

8. Gas Transfer (DLCO)

CO has an essentially unlimited reservoir on hemoglobin, so the diffusion gradient depends only on alveolar CO pressure — DLCO therefore isolates resistance across the alveolar-capillary membrane as the rate-limiting factor.

↓ DLCO↑ DLCO
Interstitial lung disease (scarring → loss of alveolar architecture)Polycythemia (↑ volume for CO diffusion)
Emphysema (↓ surface area)Pulmonary hemorrhage (↑ extravascular blood volume)
CHF with pulmonary edemaEarly CHF (↑ pulmonary blood volume)
Pulmonary hypertensionLeft-to-right cardiac shunts
Pulmonary embolism

9. Pearls & Pitfalls

  • Abnormal PFTs in obese patients generally reflect intrinsic respiratory dysfunction, not just obesity — except in the morbidly obese
  • Patient effort heavily affects PFT accuracy/reproducibility — patient must be alert and cooperative; examiner coaching improves test quality
  • Reference values depend on age, gender, race, and especially height — correct reference selection is essential for valid interpretation

One-line differentiator — Obstructive disease shrinks VC by ballooning TLC/RV/FRC (air trapping); restrictive disease shrinks VC by shrinking TLC/RV/FRC (less lung to fill). Mixed = both patterns confirmed on lung volumes.

12. Approach to Cough (Objective 3)

Cough is the single most common respiratory complaint. Classify by duration first — it drives the differential.

DurationDefinitionCommon causes
Acute< 8 weeksCommon cold, acute bacterial sinusitis, pertussis, COPD exacerbation, allergic/irritant rhinitis (less common but important: PE, pneumonia, heart failure, aspiration)
SubacuteFollows acute onset, ongoingPostinfectious cough (~30% bacterial: H. influenzae, S. pneumoniae, B. pertussis)
Chronic> 8 weeksSee below — 98% from 8 common conditions
The "big 3" of chronic cough — Upper airway cough syndrome (postnasal drip), asthma, and GERD account for the large majority of chronic cough in immunocompetent adults. Other common causes: chronic bronchitis/COPD, eosinophilic bronchitis, bronchiectasis, ACE-inhibitor use, postinfectious cough.

Postinfectious cough: usually nonproductive, persists 3–8 weeks after a URI, normal CXR.

Diagnosis

  • CXR in all patients with chronic cough
  • In current smokers or ACE-inhibitor users: hold further workup and reassess after ≥4 weeks of smoking cessation or drug discontinuation first
  • Further testing guided by history/exam: sinus imaging, methacholine challenge (asthma), barium esophagography/esophageal pH (GERD), bronchoscopy
  • If empiric treatment (e.g., antihistamine/decongestant for UACS, ICS for asthma, PPI for GERD) partially works, keep it — causes are often multiple
Red flags requiring urgent workup — hemoptysis, smoker >45 y/o with new/changed cough or voice change, heavy smoking history (≥30 pack-years, age 55–80), trouble swallowing, vomiting, recurrent pneumonia, abnormal exam/CXR.

Treatment

  • Treating the specific identified cause succeeds in 84–98% of cases — nonspecific cough suppression is rarely the right first move
  • Antitussives/mucolytics/decongestants have no strong evidence for acute cough from a URI
  • For refractory/unexplained chronic cough: gabapentin or pregabalin can help cough-specific quality of life; low-dose opioids (e.g., morphine) help cough in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

13. Approach to Wheezing (Objective 3)

Wheeze = continuous musical sound >80–100 msec from flow through critically narrowed, collapsible bronchi. Most classically associated with asthma, but has a broad differential.

Upper airway causesVocal cord dysfunction, epiglottitis, retropharyngeal abscess, laryngotracheal injury (post-intubation), neoplasm, anaphylaxis
Lower airway causesCOPD, pulmonary edema, aspiration, PE, bronchiolitis, cystic fibrosis, carcinoid syndrome, bronchiectasis, endobronchial sarcoidosis, lymphangitic carcinomatosis, parasitic infection
Asthma mimic — Vocal cord dysfunction (paradoxical vocal fold movement) can look just like asthma: no response to bronchodilators, stridor + wheeze together, extrathoracic variable obstruction on flow-volume loop, and confirmed by paradoxical vocal cord adduction on laryngoscopy. Can coexist with true asthma.

Diagnosis — Flow-Volume Loop Localization

Same logic as the obstruction section above — the shape tells you where the lesion sits:

  • Variable extrathoracic obstruction (e.g., vocal cord lesion) → flattened inspiratory limb
  • Variable intrathoracic obstruction (e.g., tracheomalacia) → flattened expiratory limb
  • Fixed obstruction (e.g., tracheal tumor) → both limbs flattened
  • Small airway obstruction → concave expiratory limb (the classic obstructive lung disease pattern)

Treatment

Treat the underlying cause — wheeze typically resolves completely or partially. Treating incidental, minimally symptomatic GERD does not improve wheezing.

14. Approach to Dyspnea (Objective 3)

Dyspnea = the unpleasant sensation of difficult/labored breathing — exertion-appropriate breathlessness in a healthy person during exercise does not count.

Acute dyspnea — rule out life-threats first

  • Pulmonary embolism, pulmonary edema, acute airway obstruction (anaphylaxis, foreign body), pneumothorax, pneumonia

Chronic dyspnea — broader differential

  • COPD, asthma, interstitial lung disease, heart failure, cardiomyopathy, GERD, hyperventilation syndrome

Mechanisms (useful framework)

↑ Respiratory drive — chemoreceptorHypoxemia, hypercapnia, metabolic acidosis, anemia, ↓ cardiac output
↑ Respiratory drive — pulmonary receptorILD, pleural effusion, PE/pulm. HTN, heart failure, mild asthma
↑ Respiratory drive — behavioralHyperventilation syndrome, anxiety/panic disorder
↑ Ventilatory pump effortMuscle weakness (myasthenia gravis, Guillain-Barré), ↓ chest wall compliance (kyphoscoliosis, obesity), airflow obstruction (asthma, COPD)

Diagnosis — tiered workup

Level 1 (initial)Level 2 (if unclear)Level 3 (specialist)
CBC, metabolic panel, CXR, ECG, spirometry, pulse oximetryEcho, BNP, full PFTs, ABG, HRCT, Holter, V/Q scan, exercise treadmillBronchoscopy, esophageal pH testing, lung biopsy, cardiac catheterization, CPET
BNP is the go-to test for distinguishing a cardiac vs. pulmonary cause of acute dyspnea when the picture is unclear.

Treatment

Confirm the cause by seeing which specific therapy resolves the dyspnea — multiple contributing conditions may need simultaneous treatment. For refractory dyspnea despite maximal treatment of underlying heart/lung disease, opioids can reduce air hunger.

15. Approach to Hemoptysis (Objective 3)

Massive hemoptysis = ≥600 mL blood expectorated in 24–48 hours (~3–10% of cases)
Pseudohemoptysis — blood from the oral cavity, nares, pharynx, or from aspirated hematemesis can mimic true hemoptysis; distinguishing the source matters before working up the lungs.

Frequency by category

Massive hemoptysis — most common causesInfection (TB, bronchiectasis, lung abscess), cancer, aspergilloma, cystic fibrosis; iatrogenic: PA rupture (balloon catheterization), tracheoarterial fistula (tracheostomy)
Nonmassive hemoptysis — most common causesBronchitis (>1/3), bronchogenic carcinoma (~1/5), TB (7%), pneumonia (5%), bronchiectasis (1%)
Idiopathic/central2–32% of cases, most common in men 30–50 y/o; rarely reveals a source even with long follow-up

Broad categories to keep in mind: cardiovascular (AVM, congenital heart disease, PE, mitral stenosis), pulmonary vascular, infection, rheumatologic (Goodpasture, GPA, SLE), drugs/toxins (anticoagulants, cocaine), trauma, iatrogenic (biopsy, catheter injury), tracheobronchial/airway disease, hematologic (coagulopathy, thrombocytopenia).

Diagnosis

  • History: chronicity pattern is a clue — months-to-years of recurrent hemoptysis suggests bronchial adenoma/bronchiectasis; daily small-volume hemoptysis for weeks suggests bronchogenic carcinoma
  • Exam: telangiectasias (hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia), unilateral wheeze/crackles (localized mass), tachypnea/phlebitis/friction rub (PE)
  • Labs: CBC, urinalysis (hematuria → systemic disease like GPA/Goodpasture/SLE), coagulation studies
  • CXR first — but note it's normal in up to 30% of hemoptysis cases
  • Bronchoscopy localizes bleeding in up to 93% (fiberoptic); best yield when done during or within 24 hours of active bleeding (drops to ~50% by 48 hours)

Treatment

  • Treat the underlying cause
  • Bronchial artery embolization — emergency treatment for massive hemoptysis and for bronchiectasis-related bleeding
  • Inhaled tranexamic acid — safe/effective for controlling nonmassive hemoptysis
