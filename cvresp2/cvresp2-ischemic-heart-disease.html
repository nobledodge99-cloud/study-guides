1. Ischemic Heart Disease: Definition & Risk Factors

Ischemic heart disease (IHD) is a broad term for clinical syndromes caused by atherosclerosis and narrowing of the coronary arteries. Narrowing limits blood flow; combined with myocardial O2 demand, this causes tissue ischemia. Mild/transient ischemia is recoverable; severe or prolonged ischemia causes irreversible tissue death (infarction).
Terminology Atherosclerosis in the coronary arteries = coronary artery disease (CAD). When CAD becomes severe enough to cause myocardial ischemia, it's called IHD. Coronary artery atherosclerosis causes most IHD, though a rarer cause is vasospastic angina (coronary occlusion from vasospasm, not atherosclerosis).

Risk Factors — Three Categories

CategoryFactors
NonmodifiableIncreasing age; male sex; race/ethnicity; family history of premature ASCVD
ModifiableType 2 diabetes mellitus; hypertension; dyslipidemia (↑LDL/↑triglycerides); cigarette smoking/tobacco use (the single biggest one); obesity and metabolic syndrome; physical inactivity; chronic kidney disease
Risk-enhancing factorsChronic systemic inflammatory disorders (rheumatoid arthritis, SLE, HIV, psoriasis); adverse pregnancy-related conditions (preeclampsia, gestational diabetes, premature menopause); persistently elevated triglycerides and lipoprotein(a); psychosocial stressors and adverse social determinants of health
Current Framing Rather than focusing on single cutoff values, current thinking emphasizes a person's lifetime exposure and overall risk. Inflammation, insulin resistance, endothelial dysfunction, and abnormal lipid metabolism all accelerate atherosclerosis — especially with ongoing cardiometabolic risk factors.

Epidemiology

IHD causes >9 million deaths/year worldwide (low- and middle-income countries hit hardest); >18 million US adults have CAD. More common in men and older adults. Marginalized groups tend to develop CAD earlier, have more complications, and worse outcomes — driven by more risk factors, less healthcare access, and other social determinants.
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Which of the following is considered the single most significant modifiable risk factor for ischemic heart disease?

Explanation: While all listed factors are modifiable risk factors for IHD, cigarette smoking/tobacco use is specifically emphasized as one of the biggest modifiable risk factors, alongside hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia.

2. A patient with a history of preeclampsia during pregnancy is now being risk-stratified for future cardiovascular disease. How should this history be classified?

Explanation: Adverse pregnancy-related conditions such as preeclampsia, gestational diabetes, and premature menopause are classified as risk-enhancing factors for ASCVD, a category distinct from the traditional nonmodifiable and modifiable risk factors.

3. What is the current emphasis in ASCVD risk assessment, as opposed to relying only on single risk factor cutoff values?

Explanation: Current thinking in ASCVD risk assessment emphasizes a person's lifetime exposure and overall risk, rather than focusing only on whether individual risk factors cross single cutoff values -- reflecting a more personalized, cumulative approach to risk.

2. Pathophysiology: Atherosclerosis & Plaque Rupture

How Atherosclerosis Develops

Endothelial damage (from chronic exposure to high cholesterol, hypertension, smoking, hyperglycemia, systemic inflammation) → increased LDL entry into the arterial wall, where it's oxidized/modified → attracts monocytes → monocytes differentiate into macrophages, take up oxidized LDL, become foam cells (the first visible sign of atherosclerosis — a "fatty streak") → ongoing inflammation causes smooth muscle cell migration/proliferation → a fibrous cap forms over a core of dead, fatty material (the atheroma).
Board Tip — Which Layer? Atherosclerosis develops in the intimal layer of the arterial wall — where cholesterol and fat deposit and form the plaque.

Stable vs. High-Risk (Vulnerable) Plaque

Initially, the artery may compensate and preserve its lumen size. As plaque grows/hardens and the fibrous cap thins, blood flow is reduced. Plaque destabilization — not gradual narrowing — is the main driver of acute coronary syndromes.
High-Risk Plaque Features
Large lipid/necrotic core
Thin fibrous cap
High density of active macrophages within the plaque
Intraplaque hemorrhage
Damage/erosion to the endothelial surface layer
The Final Common Pathway to MI When a plaque ruptures or its surface is damaged, it exposes thrombogenic material to the bloodstream → triggers platelet aggregation and thrombus formation → can rapidly and completely occlude the artery → reduced blood flow to the heart, or a full heart attack.
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Which layer of the coronary artery wall is the primary site of cholesterol and lipid deposition in atherosclerosis?

Explanation: Atherosclerosis develops in the intimal layer of the arterial wall, where cholesterol and fat deposit, become oxidized, and are taken up by macrophages to form foam cells and eventually the atherosclerotic plaque.

2. According to current understanding, what is the primary trigger for acute coronary syndromes, rather than simply the gradual narrowing of the coronary artery lumen?

Explanation: Plaque destabilization, rather than gradual arterial narrowing, is the main cause of acute coronary syndromes -- when a high-risk plaque ruptures or its surface erodes, it exposes thrombogenic material that triggers platelet aggregation and thrombus formation, which can acutely and completely occlude the artery.

3. Which of the following is a feature of a high-risk ('vulnerable') atherosclerotic plaque, as opposed to a stable plaque?

Explanation: High-risk plaques are characterized by a thin fibrous cap covering a large lipid/necrotic core, a high density of active macrophages, intraplaque hemorrhage, and endothelial surface damage -- features that predispose to rupture and acute coronary events, in contrast to more stable, thick-capped plaques.

3. Stable, Unstable & Vasospastic Angina

Angina pectoris = chest pain caused by myocardial ischemia without necrosis — myocytes get reduced (not absent) oxygen, so they don't die and don't release cardiac enzymes (troponin, CK-MB). Angina is a symptom of CAD, not a disease itself.

Stable Angina

FeatureDetail
Prevalence~3% of the US population
PatternPredictable — the same level of exertion reliably brings on the same pain each time; does not worsen over time
Pain qualitySubsternal, non-sharp — squeezing, tight, or burning; gradual onset; lasts 2–5 minutes (not seconds, not hours); not positional
ReliefRest or sublingual nitroglycerin
PathophysiologyFixed atherosclerotic narrowing (no thrombus); at rest, O2 demand is low and flow is adequate; with exertion, the diseased vessel can't meet increased demand → ischemia stimulates visceral afferent (sympathetic) fibers → pain
Alternate name"Chronic coronary syndrome"

Unstable Angina

FeatureDetail
Incidence~3 per 1,000 people annually — less common than stable angina, but far more dangerous
PatternNew onset, occurs at rest or unexpectedly, or more severe/frequent than the patient's prior chest pain; may not resolve with rest or nitroglycerin; typically lasts >10 minutes
PathophysiologyFibrous cap rupture → exposed collagen triggers platelet aggregation → thrombus (platelets + fibrin) forms → inadequate flow even at rest
Relationship to MIA likely continuum with MI — may progress to MI; classified as part of ACS since initial management resembles MI treatment
A Vanishing Diagnosis? With more sensitive troponin assays, many/most cases historically called "unstable angina" now show elevated troponin and are reclassified as small MIs (NSTEMI) — the term "unstable angina" is used less over time, though it's still used clinically.

Histologic Comparison

Board Tip Stable and unstable angina both show atherosclerosis in the coronary arteries — but only unstable angina shows rupture of the fibrous cap with superimposed thrombus formation.
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A patient reports chest pain that reliably occurs after walking exactly two blocks, resolves with rest, and has been unchanged in character for the past 6 months. Which type of angina does this represent, and what is the underlying pathophysiology?

Explanation: This presentation -- predictable, exertional chest pain that is unchanged over time and relieved by rest -- is classic for stable angina, caused by fixed atherosclerotic narrowing of the coronary arteries without superimposed thrombus.

2. A young female patient has recurrent anginal chest pain, but coronary angiography shows no obstructive disease. What is this condition called, and what is a key feature of its evaluation?

Explanation: Cardiac syndrome X refers to angina in females without obstructive coronary artery disease, attributed to abnormal coronary microvascular function or altered pain perception; more than 50% of these patients have a normal coronary angiogram.

3. What key histologic feature distinguishes unstable angina from stable angina in the coronary arteries?

Explanation: While both stable and unstable angina show underlying atherosclerosis in the coronary arteries, only unstable angina shows rupture of the fibrous cap with superimposed thrombus formation -- the key pathophysiologic distinction between the two.

4. Acute Coronary Syndrome: NSTEMI vs. STEMI

Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) is an umbrella term for any acute chest pain presentation where MI is suspected — a clinical classification based on symptoms and ECG findings, not pathology. ACS includes three syndromes: unstable angina, NSTEMI, and STEMI.
ACS vs. Chronic Coronary Syndrome The contrasting term "chronic coronary syndrome" describes the longstanding, stable symptoms of stable angina.

Myocardial Infarction — Basic Mechanism

MI = myocyte death from lack of O2. Occurs when an atherosclerotic plaque ruptures → thrombus forms → obstructs (partially or completely) blood flow. Thrombus formation causes most MIs, though other mechanisms exist (e.g., severe hypotension reducing cardiac O2 delivery).

NSTEMI vs. STEMI — The Key Distinction

FeatureSTEMINSTEMI
Coronary occlusionTotal (100%) occlusionPartial (<100%) occlusion — still enough to limit O2 delivery and kill myocytes
Thickness of injuryFull-thickness loss of myocardiumLess-than-full-thickness (less severe) injury
ECGST-segment elevationNo ST elevation; may show ST depression, T-wave inversion, or be nonspecific
Cardiac enzymesElevatedElevated
Urgency of reperfusionMore aggressive/urgent — rapid angioplasty or thrombolytics; "time is myocardium," initiate within 90–120 minutes of ED arrivalAlso frequently receives angioplasty, but not as urgently given the lesser occlusion
Board Tip — Only 50% of MIs Show ST Elevation ST-segment elevation is present in only about 50% of MIs confirmed by cardiac enzymes — an ECG without ST elevation does NOT rule out MI.

Unstable Angina vs. NSTEMI — The Overlap

Board Tip Classic teaching: unstable angina has a normal/stable ECG and normal cardiac enzymes (ruling out MI), while NSTEMI has elevated enzymes without ST elevation. In practice, with high-sensitivity troponin assays, many cases once labeled "unstable angina" now show troponin elevation and are reclassified as NSTEMI.

Ischemic Heart Failure

Chronic ischemic damage to the myocardium can cause permanent loss of contractility and heart failure. Coronary artery disease/ischemia causes most ventricular dilation and systolic HF (decreased LV systolic pumping function). Presents with typical HF symptoms: dyspnea, edema, severe fatigue.
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A patient presents with chest pain and elevated troponin, but the EKG shows no ST-segment elevation. What is the underlying mechanism, and how does it differ from a STEMI?

Explanation: NSTEMI results from partial (less than 100%) coronary artery occlusion, causing a less-than-full-thickness myocardial injury -- still enough to elevate cardiac enzymes, but pathologically distinct from the total occlusion and full-thickness injury seen in STEMI.

2. Approximately what percentage of myocardial infarctions confirmed by cardiac enzyme elevation actually show ST-segment elevation on EKG?

Explanation: ST-segment elevation is present in only about 50% of myocardial infarctions confirmed by cardiac enzymes, which is why a normal or non-diagnostic EKG cannot be used alone to rule out MI.

3. Why is the time-to-reperfusion goal for STEMI (90-120 minutes) more urgent than for NSTEMI?

Explanation: Because STEMI results from total coronary occlusion causing full-thickness myocardial damage, rapid reperfusion (within 90-120 minutes of ED arrival) is critical to salvage as much myocardium as possible -- captured by the phrase 'time is myocardium.' NSTEMI, from partial occlusion, is also often treated with angioplasty but with less urgency.

Anginal Equivalents

Some patients — often older patients, females, and those with diabetes mellitus — may not have classic chest pain. Instead, they present with less specific symptoms: dyspnea, dizziness, or nausea — termed "anginal equivalents."
Board Tip — Historical Bias Most early IHD literature was gathered exclusively in male patients, so many preconceptions about typical angina/MI presentations are limited by this sex and age bias. Always keep anginal equivalents in mind, especially in women, older adults, and diabetics.

📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. An 82-year-old diabetic woman presents with new-onset dyspnea and nausea but no chest pain. Why should ischemic heart disease still be on the differential?

Explanation: Anginal equivalents -- dyspnea, dizziness, or nausea without classic chest pain -- are recognized presentations of myocardial ischemia, particularly in older patients, females, and those with diabetes mellitus, and IHD must remain on the differential despite the absence of typical chest pain.

2. A patient's chest pain worsens with deep inspiration and improves when leaning forward. Which diagnosis does this classically suggest, as opposed to myocardial ischemia?

Explanation: Chest pain that worsens with deep inspiration and improves with leaning forward is a classic feature of acute pericarditis, distinguishing it from ischemic chest pain, which is typically aggravated by exertion rather than respiration or position.

3. Why is caution advised when applying classic teaching about anginal presentation to all patient populations equally?

Explanation: Most early literature on ischemic heart disease was gathered exclusively in male patients, meaning many common preconceptions about angina and MI presentation reflect this sex and age bias -- an important reason to actively consider anginal equivalents in women, older patients, and other underrepresented groups.

Key History Elements

Onset (what were they doing?), location (localized vs. diffuse), duration, character (patient's own words), aggravating/alleviating factors (exertional? eating? breathing? position? new workout?), radiation, timing/frequency, and associated symptoms (SOB, nausea/vomiting, fever, diaphoresis, cough, dyspepsia, edema, calf pain/swelling, recent illness).

Risk Factor Screening by Differential

ConcernRisk Factors to Screen For
ACSPrior MI, family history of cardiac disease, smoking, HTN, hyperlipidemia, diabetes
PEPrior DVT/PE, hormone use (incl. OCPs), recent surgery, cancer, immobility
OtherRecent GI procedures (scopes); drug abuse (cocaine, methamphetamines)

Physical Exam

Full vitals (BP in both arms — a discrepancy raises concern for aortic dissection); general appearance (diaphoresis, distress); skin exam (shingles); neck exam (JVD, especially with inspiration = Kussmaul sign); chest palpation (reproducible pain, crepitus); heart, lung, abdominal exams; extremities (unilateral swelling, calf pain, edema, pulse symmetry).

Initial Workup

EKG (ideally within first 10 minutes of arrival; consider serial EKGs); chest X-ray; CBC, BMP, troponin (consider serial troponin 4 hours apart), lipase; CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA) if PE suspected (V/Q scan if CTPA contraindicated); bedside ultrasound if tamponade suspected.
Board Tip Younger patients and those without risk factors can still have an MI — don't anchor on age alone. Aortic dissection can cause a stroke — always consider it in the workup.
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A patient describes chest pain by pointing precisely to one spot with a single finger, describing it as sharp and stabbing. Is this pain more consistent with a visceral or somatic origin?

Explanation: The ability to precisely localize pain with one finger, combined with a sharp, stabbing quality, is characteristic of somatic pain, such as musculoskeletal chest wall pain -- in contrast to visceral pain (like ischemic heart pain), which is vague, diffuse, and described as dull, deep, or squeezing.

2. What percentage of ED chest pain presentations is attributable to acute coronary syndrome, based on the epidemiologic data reviewed?

Explanation: Acute coronary syndrome accounts for approximately 31% of ED chest pain presentations, making it the single most common identifiable cause, followed closely by GERD (30%) and musculoskeletal causes (28%).

3. Why is it important to measure blood pressure in both arms during the physical exam of a patient with chest pain?

Explanation: Measuring blood pressure in both arms is important because a significant discrepancy between the two readings raises concern for aortic dissection, a life-threatening cause of chest pain that must not be missed.

7. Troponin I: Pathophysiology & Role in Chest Pain Evaluation

Why Cardiac Enzymes Matter

In angina, myocytes get reduced but not absent oxygen — they don't die, so they don't release troponin or CK-MB. This is why lab tests don't help diagnose angina the way they do MI. In MI, myocytes actually die (necrosis), releasing these intracellular proteins into the bloodstream — troponin and CK-MB become elevated only when myocardial cell death has occurred.

Troponin's Diagnostic Role Across the ACS Spectrum

ConditionTroponin Level
Stable anginaNormal (no myocyte death)
Vasospastic anginaNormal
Unstable angina (classic definition)Normal by traditional assay
NSTEMIElevated, without ST elevation
STEMIElevated, with ST elevation
The High-Sensitivity Troponin Shift Traditional troponin assays are normal in unstable angina. But high-sensitivity troponin assays are increasingly able to detect elevated troponin even in what was classically called "unstable angina" — meaning many of these cases are now recognized as small NSTEMIs rather than true angina without necrosis. This is why the term "unstable angina" is used less over time, even though it's retained clinically.

Practical Use in the Chest Pain Workup

Order troponin as part of the initial ACS workup; consider serial troponin levels ~4 hours apart to capture a rising/falling pattern and detect delayed elevation. Elevated troponin, combined with the clinical picture and EKG findings, helps classify the patient along the ACS spectrum (unstable angina/NSTEMI/STEMI) and guides urgency of intervention.
Board Tip A single normal troponin at presentation does not rule out MI — this is precisely why serial troponin testing is standard practice; myocardial injury markers take time to rise into the detectable/diagnostic range after the ischemic insult begins.
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Why do cardiac enzyme levels (troponin, CK-MB) remain normal in stable angina but become elevated in myocardial infarction?

Explanation: In angina (including stable angina), myocytes receive reduced but still adequate oxygen and do not die, so intracellular proteins like troponin and CK-MB are not released. In MI, actual myocyte death (necrosis) occurs, releasing these enzymes into the bloodstream -- this is the fundamental reason troponin distinguishes ischemia without infarction from true infarction.

2. Why has the use of the term 'unstable angina' decreased over time in clinical practice?

Explanation: With the advent of high-sensitivity troponin assays, many cases that would have historically been classified as unstable angina (with normal traditional troponin) now show detectable troponin elevation, leading experts to reclassify these as small NSTEMIs -- hence the declining clinical use of the term 'unstable angina.'

3. Why is serial troponin testing (e.g., 4 hours apart) recommended rather than relying on a single troponin level at presentation?

Explanation: A single normal troponin at presentation does not rule out MI, because troponin release and detection takes time following the onset of myocardial injury. Serial troponin testing (such as 4 hours apart) captures the rising (or falling) pattern needed to accurately diagnose or exclude MI.

8. Risk Stratification for Chest Pain & Stress Testing

Pretest Probability Drives Testing Strategy

Stress testing is most useful in patients with an intermediate pretest probability of CAD (based on history and physical). Low-risk patients have a high false-positive rate with stress testing; high-risk patients may have more false-negatives and are often better served by direct angiography.
Sensitivity Ceiling Regardless of stress test type, sensitivity for detecting obstructive CAD is only 70–90% — stress testing will not detect all patients with IHD.

Exercise Electrocardiography (Treadmill EST)

FeatureDetail
Positive test criteriaIschemic ECG changes — classically large ST-segment depressions (>2–3 mm), most significant with concurrent angina-like chest pain; exercise-induced hypotension (drop >10 mmHg) also qualifies
Result categoriesLow-risk, intermediate-risk, or high-risk
ContraindicationsAcute MI within the prior 2–3 days, unstable angina, active arrhythmias, decompensated heart failure

📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A patient with a low pretest probability of CAD undergoes exercise stress testing and the result is positive. What is the most important consideration in interpreting this result?

Explanation: Stress testing is most useful in intermediate-risk patients; in low-risk patients, a positive result carries a high false-positive rate, so the result must be interpreted cautiously in the context of the patient's overall pretest probability.

2. What EKG finding, along with the degree of blood pressure change, defines a positive exercise stress test?

Explanation: A positive exercise stress test is classically defined by large ST-segment depressions (>2-3mm), especially with concurrent angina-like chest pain, and/or exercise-induced hypotension (a drop in blood pressure of more than 10 mmHg).

3. Why is exercise stress testing not useful for diagnosing vasospastic (Prinzmetal) angina, even though it may still be performed in these patients?

Explanation: In vasospastic angina, stress testing is performed to rule out underlying atherosclerotic coronary artery occlusion as a competing or coexisting diagnosis -- it is not useful for provoking or diagnosing the vasospasm itself, which requires different provocative testing (e.g., ergonovine or acetylcholine challenge) if needed.

9. Diagnostic Testing: Imaging & Angiography

Coronary CT Angiography (CTA)

High-resolution, sensitive for coronary occlusion. Used in low-risk angina patients where stress testing might not be appropriate — good for ruling out CAD. Positive results require confirmatory testing (stress ECG or coronary angiography).

Coronary Angiography

The definitive, invasive test — uses contrast dye and x-ray to directly visualize coronary occlusion. Advantage: percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) can be performed during the same procedure (diagnosis + treatment). Downsides: invasive, expensive — not every stable angina patient needs it.
Angina TypeAngiography Timing/Use
Stable anginaUsed if the patient fails medical management, or stress ECG shows a large amount of at-risk myocardium (ST depressions in multiple leads)
Unstable anginaMost patients undergo angiography within 24 hours of ED arrival (not as immediate as STEMI)
Vasospastic anginaMay be done to exclude occlusive disease; vasospasm can be induced with ergonovine/acetylcholine under observation (now uncommon)

Additional Modalities Used in Practice

Echocardiogram, cardiac MRI, and cardiac CT can also aid stratification. Wall motion abnormalities on TTE may prompt ischemic workup even in an asymptomatic patient. Cardiac CT/MRI can show coronary artery calcification, prompting further workup even in asymptomatic or stable patients.
ECG Reminder ECG is essential for evaluating both stable and emergent chest pain patients, but remember: ST elevation is present in only ~50% of enzyme-confirmed MIs — a normal or nonspecific ECG never fully excludes ischemia.
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A low-risk patient with atypical chest pain undergoes coronary CTA, which shows a significant stenosis. What is the appropriate next step?

Explanation: While coronary CTA is useful for ruling out CAD in low-risk patients, a positive result requires confirmatory testing such as stress ECG testing or coronary angiography before proceeding with further management decisions.

2. What is a key advantage of coronary angiography over noninvasive testing modalities?

Explanation: A key advantage of coronary angiography is that percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), such as balloon angioplasty, can be performed during the same procedure if a significant lesion is found -- combining diagnostic and therapeutic capability, unlike purely diagnostic noninvasive tests.

3. For a patient with unstable angina who is hemodynamically stable, what is the typical timing for coronary angiography, in contrast to a STEMI?

Explanation: Most patients with unstable angina undergo coronary angiography within 24 hours of arrival at the emergency department -- urgent, but not requiring the same immediate (within 90-120 minutes) timeline as STEMI, which represents complete coronary occlusion.

📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. Which of the following is NOT one of the input fields in the current live ASCVD Risk Estimator Plus tool?

Explanation: Race is not one of the input fields in the current live ASCVD Risk Estimator Plus tool -- a change from the original 2013 Pooled Cohort Equations, which did require race as an input. The current tool instead collects sex, age, HDL/total cholesterol, smoking status, systolic BP, BMI, eGFR, lipid-lowering/antihypertensive medication use, and diabetes status, with UACR, HbA1c, and zip code as optional additional predictors.

2. A patient's calculated 10-year ASCVD risk is 12%. Based on standard risk categories, what statin intensity would typically be recommended?

Explanation: A 10-year ASCVD risk of 12% falls into the intermediate risk category (7.5-20%), for which a moderate-intensity statin targeting at least 30% LDL reduction is typically recommended.

3. A patient with a borderline 10-year ASCVD risk (6%) has an inconclusive risk discussion. Their coronary artery calcium score comes back at 150. What does this suggest?

Explanation: A CAC score above 100 suggests that starting statin therapy is reasonable, providing additional risk information to help guide the treatment decision when the 10-year ASCVD risk score alone falls into an inconclusive borderline or intermediate range.

11. Primary Prevention: Lifestyle & Risk Factor Management

Primary prevention = assessing ASCVD risk in a structured way, making lifestyle changes, and treating key risk factors (cholesterol, BP, diabetes, smoking) before a first cardiovascular event, matching treatment intensity to overall risk.

Diet

The Mediterranean diet (vegetables, fruits, legumes, nuts, whole grains, fish) is highly recommended. Replace saturated fats with mono- and polyunsaturated fats. DASH diet sodium reduction lowers BP and reduces cardiovascular event risk. Sugar-sweetened/artificial sweeteners and trans fats both correlate with increased 10-year ASCVD risk.

Weight loss consistently improves ASCVD risk profile. Recommendations: 200–300 min/week physical activity; low-calorie diet (800–1500 kcal/day) if indicated.

Tobacco Cessation

Assess tobacco use at every primary care visit; a brief (3–10 min) status assessment with quit counseling should occur at each encounter. Nicotine replacement (patches, gum, lozenges, nasal spray, oral inhaler) and medications (bupropion, varenicline) assist cessation.

Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus

Diagnosed at HbA1c >6.5%. ~12% of US adults have diabetes; 90–95% is type 2. Initial management: heart-healthy diet, ≥150 min/week activity, weight loss if overweight/obese; metformin is first-line therapy. If HbA1c remains >7% despite lifestyle + metformin, add an SGLT2 inhibitor or GLP-1 agonist (both reduce ASCVD risk).

Aspirin for Primary Prevention

Increasingly controversial. Per ACC/AHA 2019: may be considered in patients ages 40–70 with significant CV risk factors and low bleeding risk — a relatively weak recommendation requiring individualized risk-benefit discussion. Routine aspirin use is generally NOT advised for primary prevention.
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A patient asks about starting aspirin for primary prevention of cardiovascular disease. What is the current guidance on this practice?

Explanation: Current guidance indicates that routine aspirin use is generally not advised for primary prevention; its use may be considered in patients aged 40-70 with significant ASCVD risk factors and low bleeding risk, following an individualized risk-versus-benefit discussion.

2. What is the first-line pharmacologic therapy for a patient newly diagnosed with type 2 diabetes mellitus, in the context of cardiovascular risk reduction?

Explanation: Metformin is considered first-line therapy for type 2 diabetes mellitus, used alongside lifestyle modifications; SGLT2 inhibitors or GLP-1 receptor agonists are added if HbA1c remains above 7% despite these measures, given their demonstrated ASCVD risk reduction.

3. A patient with stage 1 hypertension (132/84 mmHg) has a calculated 10-year ASCVD risk of 12%. What is the recommended management approach?

Explanation: For stage 1 hypertension with a 10-year ASCVD risk of 10% or greater, pharmacologic therapy should be initiated in addition to nonpharmacologic (lifestyle) measures -- this patient's 12% risk exceeds that threshold, warranting pharmacologic treatment.

12. Secondary Prevention

Secondary prevention targets individuals with established cardiovascular disease (post-MI, post-PCI, post-CABG, or diagnosed CAD) — a lifelong, intensive process. Main goals: lower LDL-C, control BP, stop smoking, participate in cardiac rehab, use proven medications to prevent recurrent events.

Primary vs. Secondary Prevention — Key Contrast

Board Tip Aspirin use is the clearest contrast point: controversial/generally not advised in primary prevention, but strongly recommended (unless contraindicated) in secondary prevention. Similarly, statin intensity is risk-stratified in primary prevention but is high-intensity/maximally tolerated regardless of baseline LDL once secondary prevention is indicated.
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. How does the recommendation for aspirin use differ between primary and secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease?

Explanation: This is a key distinction: in primary prevention, routine aspirin use is generally not advised and is considered only in select higher-risk, lower-bleeding-risk patients. In secondary prevention (established CVD), low-dose aspirin is strongly recommended unless contraindicated.

2. What is the LDL-C goal for a patient undergoing secondary prevention after a myocardial infarction?

Explanation: In secondary prevention, the goal is to achieve an LDL-C of less than 70 mg/dL, using a high-intensity or maximally tolerated statin regardless of the patient's baseline lipid levels, as long as the medication is tolerated.

3. Which medication is recommended for a patient in secondary prevention who is intolerant or allergic to aspirin?

Explanation: Clopidogrel at a daily dose of 75 mg is recommended as an alternative antithrombotic agent for secondary prevention in patients who are intolerant or allergic to aspirin.

13. Acute Treatment of Cardiac Ischemia

Initial Steps for Suspected ACS

Cardiac monitor; IV access; chewable aspirin 162–325 mg; add a P2Y12 inhibitor (clopidogrel or ticagrelor) unless bypass surgery is imminent; control pain; consider O2 therapy; quickly obtain ECG and troponin; start anti-ischemic and antithrombotic treatment; consider an early invasive approach if appropriate.

Nitroglycerin

Shown to have a mortality benefit. Target: 10% MAP reduction in normotensive patients, 30% MAP reduction in hypertensive patients. Avoid in hypotensive patients and those with inferior ST elevation (right ventricular involvement makes these patients preload-dependent).
Mechanism Recap NTG generates cGMP → vasodilation, preferentially of veins → ↓venous return → ↓LV preload → ↓contraction force and myocardial O2 demand. Also causes direct coronary vasodilation, increasing O2 supply. Most common adverse effect: headache (>60% of patients); can also cause postural hypotension.

NSTEMI & Unstable Angina

Admit for cardiology consult and workup. DAPT + parenteral anticoagulation in the acute phase, adjusted for each patient's ischemic vs. bleeding risk. High-risk NSTEMI patients generally do better with an early invasive evaluation strategy.

High-intensity lipid-lowering, BP management, smoking cessation support, cardiac rehab referral, and cardiometabolic optimization should all begin early, in the hospital — not deferred to outpatient follow-up.

Stable Angina — Acute Symptom Relief

Sublingual nitroglycerin is used PRN for acute anginal chest pain relief (as opposed to the daily preventive medications discussed in Section 14).
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A patient with an inferior wall STEMI (ST elevation in II, III, aVF) is being considered for nitroglycerin administration. What is the concern with this approach?

Explanation: Inferior STEMI frequently involves the right ventricle, making cardiac output highly dependent on adequate preload. Nitroglycerin's venodilating effect reduces preload, which can precipitate severe hypotension in these preload-dependent patients -- hence the recommendation to avoid nitrates with inferior ST elevation.

2. For a STEMI patient who cannot undergo primary PCI within the target window and no transfer for PCI is feasible in time, what is the appropriate next step?

Explanation: If primary PCI is not achievable within the target timeframe, thrombolytic therapy should be administered within 30 minutes, with subsequent transfer for angiography if needed -- reflecting the urgency of restoring reperfusion ('time is myocardium').

3. When should secondary prevention measures (high-intensity statin, blood pressure management, smoking cessation counseling) be initiated for a patient admitted with acute coronary syndrome?

Explanation: Secondary prevention measures, including high-intensity lipid-lowering therapy, blood pressure management, smoking cessation support, and cardiac rehab referral, should begin early during the acute hospital admission itself, rather than being deferred to outpatient follow-up.

14. Post-Acute/Chronic Treatment of Cardiac Ischemia

For patients with chronic coronary disease (stable angina) managed in the outpatient setting, the main goals are: control symptoms, prevent MI/stroke/death, and reduce atherosclerotic risk.

Stepwise Antianginal Therapy

Adjust based on heart rate, blood pressure, and comorbidities:
StepTherapy
1st-lineBeta-blockers or calcium channel blockers
If symptoms persistAdd long-acting nitrates or ranolazine

Beta-Blockers — Detailed Mechanism (Stable Angina)

First-line for stable angina; reduce angina frequency/severity but do NOT decrease overall mortality in stable angina specifically. Block myocyte β1 receptors → ↓contractility and ↓heart rate → ↓myocardial O2 demand. Selective agents (metoprolol, atenolol) block only β1 (avoid β2-mediated bronchospasm); both selective and nonselective agents work for angina.
If Beta-Blocker Intolerant Alternatives: a nondihydropyridine CCB (diltiazem, verapamil), or a long-acting nitrate.

Statins in Chronic Management

Reduce LDL and stabilize plaque — should be prescribed to anyone with angina. High-intensity statin is first choice; add ezetimibe if LDL remains above target; consider PCSK9 inhibitors or bempedoic acid next depending on risk/tolerance/cost.

Aspirin in Chronic Angina

Doesn't directly treat stable angina (not platelet/thrombus-mediated) — prescribed for secondary prevention in patients with a history of ASCVD, MI, stroke, stent, or CABG. Mechanism: COX pathway inhibition, preventing platelet-mediated clotting.

Antiplatelet Therapy Duration

For most patients with established CAD, single antiplatelet therapy is standard. After ACS or PCI, DAPT duration/intensity is tailored to each patient's ischemic vs. bleeding risk.

Renin-Angiotensin System Inhibitors

ACE inhibitors or ARBs recommended for CAD patients who also have hypertension, diabetes, CKD, or LV dysfunction.

Invasive/Surgical Options for Refractory Stable Angina

ProcedureIndication
PCI (angioplasty ± stent)Patients who fail medical management or have high-risk features (multiple risk factors, severe pain)
CABGParticularly for left main CAD or multivessel disease; commonly harvested vessels: internal mammary artery, leg veins
Why Left Main Gets Surgery, Not Just a Stent The left main artery gives rise to both the circumflex and LAD arteries, supplying a huge myocardial territory. Stenting here is risky — if the stent occludes, it deprives the entire left coronary circulation at once. CABG is generally favored for this anatomy.
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. A patient with stable angina intolerant to beta-blockers needs an alternative first-line agent. Which medication class would be an appropriate substitute?

Explanation: For patients intolerant of beta-blockers, a nondihydropyridine calcium channel blocker such as diltiazem or verapamil is an appropriate alternative first-line agent for stable angina, with a long-acting nitrate as another option.

2. Why is coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) generally preferred over PCI/stenting for left main coronary artery disease?

Explanation: The left main coronary artery gives rise to both the circumflex and left anterior descending arteries, supplying a large area of myocardium. Because stent occlusion in this critical location would acutely deprive the entire left coronary circulation, CABG is generally favored over PCI/stenting for significant left main disease.

3. In stable angina, what is a key limitation of beta-blocker therapy despite being first-line treatment?

Explanation: While beta-blockers are first-line therapy for stable angina and effectively reduce the frequency and severity of anginal episodes, they do not decrease overall mortality in stable angina specifically -- an important nuance distinguishing symptom control from mortality benefit.

15. Complications of Myocardial Infarction

Categories of Complications

CAD/MI can cause ongoing perfusion problems, sudden plaque changes, and changes in the heart's structure or electrical system.

Structural Complications

ComplicationDetail
Damage to heart muscle or valvesCan occur post-MI
Ventricular septal defect ("hole in the heart wall")Post-MI complication
Left ventricular aneurysm ("bulge")Post-MI complication
Cardiogenic shockCan result in severe cases of the above structural complications

Electrical Complications (Arrhythmias)

Atrial fibrillation and ventricular tachyarrhythmias can occur from poor blood flow or heart scarring — these raise the risk of sudden cardiac death.

Heart Failure

A long-term consequence of CAD — repeated myocardial insults cause the heart to remodel (change shape) and progressively lose pumping ability. CAD is the leading cause of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), driving many hospitalizations and deaths.

Systemic/Vascular Complications

CAD is linked to atherosclerosis elsewhere in the body — increasing risk of stroke, peripheral artery disease, and recurrent blood clots.

Populations at Higher Complication Risk

Patients with diabetes, chronic kidney disease, or poorly managed risk factors more often have repeated episodes of reduced blood flow and worse overall outcomes.
Board Tip — The Complication Cascade Recurrent ischemia → myocardial scarring/remodeling → HFrEF; separately, scarred/damaged tissue also creates an arrhythmogenic substrate → AFib or ventricular tachyarrhythmias → risk of sudden cardiac death. Both pathways stem from the same underlying repeated ischemic insult.
📝 Check Your Understanding (3 questions)

1. What is the leading cause of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF)?

Explanation: Coronary artery disease is the leading cause of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction; repeated ischemic insults over time cause the heart to remodel and progressively lose contractile function.

2. Why do patients with a history of myocardial infarction have an increased risk of ventricular tachyarrhythmias and sudden cardiac death?

Explanation: Myocardial scarring from a prior infarction creates an arrhythmogenic substrate -- areas of scar tissue can disrupt normal electrical conduction, predisposing to ventricular tachyarrhythmias and increasing the risk of sudden cardiac death.

3. Which patient populations are noted to have a higher risk of complications and repeated episodes of reduced coronary blood flow after an MI?

Explanation: Patients with diabetes, chronic kidney disease, or poorly managed risk factors are noted to have a higher risk of repeated episodes of reduced blood flow and worse overall complications and outcomes following myocardial infarction.
