1. Cardiac Electrophysiology for Pharm

Conduction pathway: SA node → atrial myocardium → AV node → bundle of His → right/left bundle branches → Purkinje fibers → ventricular myocardium.

ECG Correlates

  • P wave — atrial depolarization
  • PR interval — AV nodal conduction delay
  • QRS complex — ventricular depolarization
  • T wave — ventricular repolarization

Drug-induced ECG changes (QRS widening, QT prolongation) are key pharmacologic markers of both efficacy and toxicity.

Fast-Response Tissues

Atrial/ventricular myocytes, His-Purkinje fibers — depolarization driven by fast Na⁺ influx.

  • Phase 0: rapid depolarization (fast Na⁺ current)
  • Phase 1: transient early repolarization (K⁺ efflux)
  • Phase 2: plateau (Ca²⁺ influx balanced by K⁺ efflux)
  • Phase 3: repolarization (K⁺ efflux predominates as Ca²⁺ channels close)
  • Phase 4: resting membrane potential (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger)

Slow-Response Tissues

SA and AV nodes — lack functional fast Na⁺ channels; depolarization relies on Ca²⁺ influx through L-type channels.

  • Phase 4: spontaneous depolarization (inward "funny" current, mostly Na⁺, augmented by T-type Ca²⁺) — underlies automaticity
  • Phase 0: upstroke via L-type Ca²⁺ influx
  • Phase 3: repolarization via outward K⁺ currents
Pearl: This ionic difference explains why CCBs and beta-blockers are the nodal agents (calcium-dependent tissue) while Na⁺ channel blockers act on fast-response tissue and have minimal nodal effect.

Basic Electrophysiologic Properties

  • Pacemaker hierarchy: SA node (60–100 bpm) → AV node (40–60 bpm) → His-Purkinje system (20–40 bpm)
  • Automaticity: spontaneous AP generation, primarily SA/AV nodes
  • Effective refractory period (ERP): time during which a cardiac cell cannot be re-excited — a protective mechanism against premature impulses, and a major pharmacologic target

2. Mechanisms of Arrhythmias

Arrhythmias arise from abnormal impulse formation, abnormal impulse conduction, or a combination of both.

Abnormal Impulse Formation — Enhanced Automaticity

  • Pacemaker cells depolarize faster than normal, or non-pacemaker cells acquire spontaneous depolarizing activity → ectopic beats/tachyarrhythmias
  • Common triggers: ↑ sympathetic tone, myocardial ischemia/hypoxia, electrolyte disturbances (hypokalemia)
  • Suppressed by: beta-blockers (Class II) and non-DHP CCBs (Class IV) — reduce phase 4 slope, slow HR

Abnormal Impulse Formation — Triggered Activity

  • Early afterdepolarizations (EADs): occur during phase 3; associated with prolonged APs & QT prolongation; precursor to TdP; triggered by Class III agents, hypokalemia, congenital long QT
  • Delayed afterdepolarizations (DADs): occur after full repolarization (phase 4); related to intracellular Ca²⁺ overload; seen with digoxin toxicity, excess catecholamines, myocardial ischemia
  • Treatment: CCBs, magnesium, or drugs that decrease sympathetic tone

Abnormal Impulse Conduction — Conduction Block

  • Impulse fails to propagate through part of the conduction system → bradyarrhythmias or substrate for reentrant circuits
  • Used therapeutically: intentional AV nodal blockade to control ventricular rate in SVT

Abnormal Impulse Conduction — Reentry

Requires three conditions:

  1. Two pathways with different conduction velocities or refractory periods
  2. Unidirectional block in one pathway
  3. Slowed conduction in the alternate pathway (allows time for tissue to recover excitability)

This enables a self-perpetuating loop. Reentrant tachyarrhythmias: AVNRT, atrial flutter, monomorphic VT.

  • Class I agents slow conduction velocity (block fast Na⁺ channels) → destabilize the reentry loop
  • Class III agents prolong the ERP → prevent premature re-excitation
  • Adenosine, beta-blockers, non-DHP CCBs acutely interrupt the circuit and restore sinus rhythm in SVT
Quick Check — Mechanisms

Delayed afterdepolarizations (DADs) are most classically associated with which clinical scenario?

DADs occur after full repolarization (phase 4) and relate to intracellular calcium overload — classically from digoxin toxicity, excess catecholamines, or ischemia. EADs (not DADs) relate to QT prolongation and Class III drugs.

4. Class I — Sodium Channel Blockers

Reduce the fast inward Na⁺ current during phase 0 → slow depolarization rate & conduction velocity in fast-response tissue → widened QRS complex.

Use-Dependence (Phase-Dependence)

  • Class I drugs preferentially bind Na⁺ channels in the open or inactivated states — more common at fast heart rates or in depolarized/ischemic tissue
  • Greater effect in diseased myocardium and during tachyarrhythmias
  • Recovery kinetics differ by drug: fast dissociators (lidocaine) act mainly at high heart rates with minimal effect at normal sinus rates; slow dissociators (flecainide, >10 sec recovery) block significantly even at resting rates, prolonging PR/QRS at baseline
  • Raises the threshold for excitability → ↓ automaticity; suppresses triggered activity (EADs/DADs); can terminate reentrant arrhythmias by slowing conduction enough to extinguish the circuit (but this same slowing can also be proarrhythmic)

Class IA — Procainamide CORE, Quinidine & Disopyramide ADDITIONAL

  • MOA: moderate Na⁺ block + K⁺ channel block → prolongs QRS and QT/APD; intermediate dissociation kinetics
  • Use: SVT and VT — use has declined given safer alternatives
  • Shared risk: QT prolongation and TdP for all three agents

Procainamide (CORE):

  • Blocks open-state Na⁺ channels + nonspecific K⁺ channels; slows conduction
  • Active metabolite NAPA retains Class III (K⁺-blocking) activity and contributes to QT prolongation; accumulates in renal dysfunction
  • Use: acute atrial and ventricular arrhythmias (IV, e.g., post-MI monomorphic VT if amiodarone/lidocaine unavailable)
  • ADRs: drug-induced lupus erythematosus (especially slow acetylators), hypotension, GI upset, agranulocytosis, rare hepatotoxicity, QT prolongation/TdP
  • Monitor procainamide + NAPA levels, especially in renal impairment

Quinidine (Additional): blocks Na⁺ + multiple K⁺ channels; alpha-blocking & vagolytic activity can paradoxically enhance AV conduction (worsening ventricular response in atrial flutter) — often needs a co-administered AV nodal blocker. ADRs: GI toxicity, cinchonism, immune reactions, TdP. Strong CYP2D6/P-gp inhibitor.

Disopyramide (Additional): strong anticholinergic effects (dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention, blurred vision); negative inotrope — avoid in heart failure; used for ventricular arrhythmias with preserved systolic function; QT prolongation/TdP risk; hepatic + renal clearance.

Class IB — Lidocaine CORE, Mexiletine ADDITIONAL

  • MOA: preferentially bind open/inactivated Na⁺ channels, especially in depolarized/ischemic tissue; shorten APD by accelerating phase 3; rapid kinetics; minimal atrial effect
  • Use: ventricular arrhythmias, particularly acute ischemic settings

Lidocaine (CORE):

  • Used acutely for monomorphic VT or to prevent VF after cardioversion
  • Main action: ventricular tissue & Purkinje fibers; little atrial effect
  • Extensive first-pass metabolism → IV only; short half-life (1–2 hr) — bolus + infusion; adjust for HF/liver disease
  • ADRs: neurologic (tremor, confusion, slurred speech, paresthesia, seizures; nystagmus is an early toxicity sign)
  • CI: significant conduction disease without pacemaker support

Mexiletine (Additional): oral analog of lidocaine, resists first-pass metabolism; used for chronic suppression of ventricular arrhythmias; hepatic metabolism; ADRs: GI symptoms, CNS effects; avoid in structural heart disease; CI: cardiogenic shock, severe SA/AV disease without pacing; CYP450 inducers lower levels.

Class IC — Flecainide CORE, Propafenone ADDITIONAL

  • MOA: strongest Na⁺ channel blockade of Class I, very slow dissociation kinetics → marked conduction slowing even at normal rates; minimal effect on QT/APD

Flecainide (CORE):

  • Blocks fast Na⁺ current, delayed rectifier K⁺ and Ca²⁺ currents to a lesser extent, and RyR2 (relevant to CPVT)
  • Widens PR, QRS, and QT intervals
  • Use: SVT in patients without structural heart disease; refractory VT; CPVT; some inherited channelopathies
  • Eliminated by kidney & liver (CYP2D6)
  • ADRs: blurred vision, dizziness, worsening HF; most serious concern is proarrhythmia; overdose → AV block, wide QRS, hemodynamic collapse (may respond to IV sodium bicarbonate)
  • CI: history of MI or structural heart disease — classic exam point (CAST trial legacy)

Propafenone (Additional): potent Na⁺ blocker + weak beta-blocking activity; used for AF/flutter/SVT without structural heart disease; hepatic (CYP2D6) metabolism, dose-reduce in liver disease; ADRs: metallic taste, constipation, dizziness, can worsen arrhythmias, avoid in reduced EF.

Case Snapshot

A patient with a prior MI is started on flecainide for symptomatic PVCs and subsequently has an increase in sudden cardiac death risk. What historical trial does this reflect, and what's the teaching point?

Answer: This reflects the CAST trial findings — Class IC agents are proarrhythmic in structural/ischemic heart disease and are contraindicated post-MI.

Quick Check — Class I

Which Class I subclass shortens the action potential duration and is most useful in ischemic/depolarized ventricular tissue?

Class IB drugs (lidocaine, mexiletine) shorten APD and preferentially act on ischemic/depolarized tissue due to their affinity for inactivated sodium channels.

Flecainide is contraindicated in which patient population?

Class IC agents markedly slow conduction and are proarrhythmic in structural/ischemic heart disease — flecainide is contraindicated post-MI.

5. Class II — Beta-Adrenergic Blockers

Metoprolol CORE · Propranolol, Esmolol ADDITIONAL

Mechanism & Effects

  • β₁-antagonism at the AV node (and to a lesser extent SA node) → ↓ phase 4 depolarization slope, ↓ AV nodal conduction, ↑ AV nodal refractoriness → ↑ PR interval
  • Limits transmission of rapid atrial impulses to the ventricles
  • Effective for: ventricular rate control in AF and paroxysmal SVT; suppress AV-nodal-dependent tachycardias (AVNRT, AVRT); suppress PVCs; beneficial post-MI and in congenital long QT syndrome
  • Do NOT terminate arrhythmias directly — valuable for rate control, not rhythm conversion

Class-Wide ADRs & Contraindications

  • ADRs: bradycardia, AV block, hypotension, fatigue
  • CI: severe bradycardia, 2nd/3rd-degree AV block (without pacemaker), acute decompensated HF, cardiogenic shock, severe bronchospastic disease
  • Caution: asthma, diabetes, peripheral vascular disease — beta-1 selective agents preferred here

Metoprolol CORE

  • β₁-selective; used for chronic rate control in AF and other SVTs
  • Moderately lipophilic → CNS penetration (fatigue, vivid dreams, depression); sexual dysfunction reported
  • Generally safer than nonselective agents in mild asthma/COPD
  • Metabolized by CYP2D6 — poor metabolizers/CYP2D6 inhibitors (e.g., SSRIs like fluoxetine, paroxetine) ↑ levels
  • DI: combining with non-DHP CCBs (verapamil, diltiazem) ↑ risk of bradycardia/heart block

Propranolol ADDITIONAL

Nonselective (β₁ + β₂); highly lipophilic → CNS effects (fatigue, depression, nightmares); β₂ blockade → bronchospasm, cold extremities, blunted hypoglycemia awareness; extensive first-pass hepatic metabolism (CYP1A2, CYP2D6 substrate); additive bradycardia/AV block with other AV nodal blockers.

Esmolol ADDITIONAL

β₁-selective, ultra-short acting; metabolized by RBC esterases → half-life ~9 min; IV only; ideal for perioperative, critical care, or emergency rapid titration/reversal (e.g., intraoperative tachyarrhythmias, hypertensive crisis with tachycardia). Hydrophilic → minimal CNS penetration; effects resolve quickly on discontinuation.

Quick Check — Class II

Which beta-blocker is preferred for rapid intraoperative rate control given its ultra-short half-life?

Esmolol's ~9-minute half-life (metabolized by RBC esterases) allows rapid titration and quick reversal, making it ideal for perioperative/critical care settings.

6. Class IV — Calcium Channel Blockers

Verapamil & Diltiazem CORE (no additional agents listed for this class)

Mechanism

  • Non-DHP CCBs directly inhibit L-type Ca²⁺ channels (open & inactivated states), particularly in the SA and AV nodes where conduction is calcium-dependent
  • ↓ Ca²⁺ influx → ↓ nodal automaticity & conduction → slows atrial signal transmission to ventricles, disrupts AV nodal reentry
  • ↓ slope of phase 0 depolarization & slows phase 4 spontaneous depolarization in nodal tissue
  • Suppress EADs and DADs; rarely terminate arrhythmias directly — used for rate control, not rhythm conversion
  • Verapamil may also mildly suppress SA node activity (dose-dependent bradycardia)

Therapeutic Use

  • Rate control in SVT (AVNRT) and AF/flutter — IV for acute, oral for chronic management
  • Ineffective for ventricular arrhythmias and potentially harmful in wide-complex tachycardia of uncertain origin (especially with an accessory pathway)

PK / Adverse Effects / Interactions

  • Extensive first-pass hepatic metabolism → low oral bioavailability; both are CYP3A4 substrates and inhibitors
  • ADRs: bradycardia, hypotension, AV block, peripheral edema, fatigue, dizziness; constipation particularly with verapamil (dose-dependent)
  • DI: additive AV nodal suppression with beta-blockers or digoxin (risk of severe bradycardia/AV block); as CYP3A4 inhibitors, can raise levels of statins, cyclosporine, amiodarone, and digoxin
  • CI: severe hypotension, 2nd/3rd-degree AV block (no pacemaker), sick sinus syndrome, acute decompensated HF
  • High-yield CI: AFib in the setting of WPW syndrome — AV nodal blockade can enhance conduction through the accessory pathway, precipitating VF and hemodynamic collapse. Never use verapamil/diltiazem in AF + WPW.
Pearl: Class II and Class IV agents share the same clinical niche — AV nodal rate control — but act through different mechanisms (beta blockade vs. direct Ca²⁺ channel blockade). Combining them additively risks bradycardia/AV block.
Quick Check — Class IV

A patient with atrial fibrillation and known WPW syndrome is given IV verapamil. What is the major concern?

AV nodal blockade in AF+WPW can preferentially enhance conduction down the accessory pathway, leading to a dangerously fast ventricular response and potential VF. Non-DHP CCBs (and other AV nodal blockers) are contraindicated in this setting.

7. Class III — Potassium Channel Blockers

Amiodarone, Sotalol, Dofetilide CORE · Dronedarone, Ibutilide ADDITIONAL

Shared Mechanism

  • Block cardiac K⁺ channels (mainly phase 3 repolarization) → prolonged repolarization, ↑ APD, ↑ ERP → suppresses reentrant arrhythmias
  • ECG correlate: QT interval prolongation
  • Used for both atrial and ventricular arrhythmias
  • Class-wide risk: proarrhythmia, especially Torsades de Pointes (TdP) — risk rises with hypokalemia, bradycardia, or co-administration of other QT-prolonging drugs
  • Exception: amiodarone rarely induces TdP despite significant QT prolongation, likely due to its multichannel effects (Na⁺, Ca²⁺, β-blockade) that offset the risk

Amiodarone CORE

  • Multi-class activity: Class III (K⁺ block, dominant), Class I (Na⁺ block), Class II (noncompetitive beta-blockade), Class IV (mild Ca²⁺ block)
  • Use: broad spectrum — AF/SVT (rhythm & rate control) and life-threatening VT/VF; favored in structural heart disease/HF (not shown to increase mortality here, unlike many other antiarrhythmics)
  • Available IV (emergency, e.g., pulseless VT/VF) and oral (long-term rhythm maintenance)
  • PK: highly lipophilic, huge volume of distribution, very long/complex half-life (weeks to months); requires a loading phase; hepatic metabolism via CYP3A4 (active metabolite)
  • ADRs (extensive): pulmonary toxicity (interstitial pneumonitis, fibrosis — can be fatal), thyroid dysfunction (hyper or hypo, from iodine content), hepatotoxicity, corneal microdeposits, photosensitivity with blue-gray skin discoloration, bradyarrhythmias; TdP is rare despite QT prolongation
  • Routine monitoring: thyroid, liver, pulmonary function
  • DI: inhibits CYP3A4, CYP2C9, P-gp → ↑ digoxin and warfarin levels (reduce warfarin dose ~30–50%, monitor INR closely); additive QT/bradycardia risk with sotalol, azithromycin, fluoroquinolones, haloperidol, beta-blockers

Sotalol CORE

  • Racemic mixture: Class II (L-enantiomer nonselective beta-blockade) + Class III (both enantiomers block delayed rectifier K⁺) → prolonged APD & QT
  • Use: life-threatening VT, maintenance of sinus rhythm in AF/flutter, pediatric arrhythmias
  • Nearly complete bioavailability, not metabolized — excreted unchanged renally; renal function is a key determinant of safety
  • ADRs: TdP (dose-limiting, worsened by renal impairment, hypokalemia, bradycardia, other QT drugs); fatigue, bronchospasm, bradycardia (nonselective beta-blocker effects)
  • CI: baseline QTc >450 ms, CrCl <40 mL/min, overt HF, asthma
  • Initiation/dose escalation requires a monitored setting with continuous ECG and renal function assessment

Dofetilide CORE

  • Pure Class III — selectively blocks the rapid component of the delayed rectifier K⁺ current (IKr); no significant Na⁺, beta, or Ca²⁺ activity
  • Use: conversion to and maintenance of sinus rhythm in AF/flutter — valuable in structural heart disease/HF (does not increase mortality here)
  • ~100% oral bioavailability; eliminated unchanged, renally — dose based on CrCl
  • Must be initiated in a monitored inpatient setting for ≥3 days with continuous ECG and renal monitoring; baseline QTc must be <440–450 ms; correct K⁺/Mg²⁺ before starting
  • ADRs: TdP (dose-dependent, worse with renal impairment, bradycardia, electrolyte disturbance)
  • DI: agents inhibiting renal cation secretion (cimetidine, trimethoprim, verapamil) and other QT-prolonging drugs
  • CI: marked renal impairment, baseline QT prolongation, hypokalemia, bradycardia

Dronedarone ADDITIONAL

Non-iodinated amiodarone analog developed to reduce thyroid/pulmonary toxicity; multi-class activity (blocks Na⁺, Ca²⁺, K⁺, has anti-adrenergic effect) but less effective than amiodarone at maintaining sinus rhythm. Approved to reduce CV hospitalization risk in paroxysmal/persistent AF/flutter. Metabolized by/inhibits CYP3A4, CYP2D6, P-gp; take with food. ADRs: GI upset, bradycardia, QT prolongation, rare serious hepatotoxicity. Black box warning: contraindicated in symptomatic HF or permanent AF (increased mortality). DI: ↑ digoxin, metoprolol, statin levels; avoid strong CYP3A4 inhibitors.

Ibutilide ADDITIONAL

IV-only, used exclusively for acute conversion of AF/flutter to sinus rhythm (not maintenance). Blocks IKr and uniquely activates a slow inward Na⁺ current, prolonging phases 2 & 3 (especially in atrial tissue) — more effective in atrial flutter than AF. ADRs: TdP (~4–8%) — requires continuous ECG monitoring for ≥4 hours post-infusion or until QT returns to baseline. CI: baseline QT prolongation, history of drug-induced TdP, marked electrolyte abnormalities. Correct K⁺/Mg²⁺ before administration.

Quick Check — Class III

Why does amiodarone rarely cause Torsades de Pointes despite prolonging the QT interval?

Amiodarone's broad multichannel blockade is thought to counterbalance the QT-prolonging risk that would otherwise predispose to TdP with pure K⁺ channel blockade.

Which Class III agent must be initiated in a monitored inpatient setting for at least 3 days due to TdP risk?

Dofetilide requires inpatient initiation with continuous ECG/renal monitoring for at least 3 days because of its dose-dependent, renally-cleared TdP risk.

8. "Class V" — Miscellaneous Agents

Adenosine, Digoxin, Magnesium sulfate CORE (no additional agents listed for this class)

Adenosine CORE

  • First-line for acute termination of PSVT, particularly AVNRT
  • MOA: activates A1 receptors (Gi-coupled) → opens inward-rectifying K⁺ channels & inhibits L-type Ca²⁺ channels in the AV node → hyperpolarization + transient AV nodal block
  • Also used diagnostically to differentiate regular narrow-complex tachycardias (prompt conversion supports AVNRT/AVRT; transient slowing without termination suggests atrial tachycardia)
  • PK: half-life <10 seconds — rapid IV bolus through a large/proximal vein + saline flush; initial dose 6 mg, may repeat 12 mg if ineffective within 1–2 min
  • Blunted by methylxanthines (caffeine, theophylline); potentiated by dipyridamole (inhibits adenosine reuptake)
  • ADRs: flushing, chest pressure, dyspnea, sense of impending doom, transient bradycardia/high-grade AV block (self-limited); rarely triggers AF or bronchospasm (caution in reactive airway disease)

Digoxin CORE

  • Use: rate control in AF/flutter, particularly with concurrent HFrEF
  • MOA (indirect): ↑ vagal tone → ↑ AV nodal refractoriness & ↓ conduction → slows ventricular rate
  • MOA (direct, inotropic): inhibits Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase → ↑ intracellular Na⁺ → ↓ Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger activity → ↑ intracellular Ca²⁺ → ↑ contractility (positive inotrope)
  • Narrow therapeutic index — toxicity signs: arrhythmias (PVCs, VT, various AV blocks), GI symptoms (N/V, abdominal pain), visual disturbances (xanthopsia/yellow-green vision, blurred vision), neurologic symptoms (confusion, dizziness, weakness)
  • Risk factors for toxicity: hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, renal impairment, P-gp inhibitor interactions (amiodarone, verapamil, quinidine raise digoxin levels)
  • Requires monitoring of digoxin levels, renal function, and electrolytes

Magnesium Sulfate CORE

  • First-line treatment for TdP — give promptly, even before correcting underlying electrolytes or stopping the offending QT-prolonging drug
  • Also used for digoxin-induced arrhythmias
  • Proposed mechanism: modulates Ca²⁺/K⁺ channels, suppresses EADs, stabilizes cardiac membranes — effective even when serum Mg²⁺ is normal
  • Administered IV over 15–20 minutes; may repeat
  • ADRs: hypotension, flushing, rarely respiratory depression or conduction abnormalities (especially with rapid infusion/high doses)
  • Caution in significant renal impairment (Mg²⁺ is renally excreted — can accumulate)
Case Snapshot

A patient develops Torsades de Pointes on the monitor. What is the first-line treatment, and should you wait for a lab-confirmed low magnesium level before treating?

Answer: IV magnesium sulfate is first-line and should be given promptly — it works even with a normal serum magnesium level. Don't wait for lab confirmation in an unstable rhythm.

Quick Check — "Class V"

Which drug is both diagnostic and therapeutic in the acute setting of a regular narrow-complex tachycardia?

Adenosine's transient AV block both terminates AVNRT/AVRT (therapeutic) and reveals underlying atrial activity if the tachycardia doesn't terminate (diagnostic).

9. Clinical Application: Putting It Together

When selecting an antiarrhythmic, consider three questions:

  1. Where is the arrhythmia originating? Supraventricular (atria/AV node) vs. ventricular arrhythmias require different agents.
  2. Is the goal rate control, rhythm control, or acute termination?
    • AF/flutter rate control → beta-blockers or non-DHP CCBs
    • AF/flutter rhythm control → Class I or Class III agents
    • AV-node-dependent SVT acute termination → adenosine (transient AV block interrupts the reentrant circuit)
    • Unstable/life-threatening VT/VF → may require immediate electrical therapy (cardioversion/defibrillation) rather than drugs alone
  3. What cardiac disease or toxicity risk limits drug selection? Structural heart disease, LV function, baseline QT interval/QT-prolongation risk, renal/hepatic function, and the drug's own proarrhythmic potential.
Framework: match the location and mechanism of the arrhythmia + the therapeutic goal + the patient's individual risk factors.

11. High-Yield Clinical Takeaways

  • Class I: Na⁺ channel blockade → slowed conduction/QRS widening
    • IA: ↑ APD/QT → TdP risk
    • IB: best in ischemic/depolarized ventricular tissue
    • IC: marked conduction slowing; avoid with prior MI/structural heart disease
  • Class II & IV: slow AV nodal conduction → rate control (not rhythm conversion)
  • Class III: prolong repolarization/ERP → QT prolongation and TdP risk
    • Amiodarone: broad efficacy + substantial extracardiac toxicity; TdP uncommon despite QT effect
    • Sotalol/dofetilide: renal function and QT monitoring are essential
  • Adenosine: first-line for acute AV-nodal-dependent SVT (AVNRT)
  • Magnesium: first-line for TdP, even with normal serum Mg²⁺
  • Never use AV nodal blockers (CCBs, beta-blockers, digoxin, adenosine) in AF with WPW
  • Drug selection always depends on the arrhythmia + underlying cardiac disease + proarrhythmic risk of the drug itself
