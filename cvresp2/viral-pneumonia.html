Part 1 · The Core Distinction

1. Viral vs. Bacterial Pneumonia — Contrasting Pathophysiology

Same alveolar battlefield, two different immune scripts — and the script determines the presentation, the imaging, and the timeline.

Bacterial vs. viral pneumonia — the immune sequence
Bacterial (e.g., pneumococcal)Viral (e.g., SARS-CoV-2)
Key immune cellNeutrophils, macrophagesLymphocytes
SequenceBacteria grow in alveoli → capillaries open, neutrophils flood in → edema → macrophages clean up debris → resolutionVirus enters cells, replicates → infected cells destroyed as virus bursts out → lymphocytes sense/destroy infected cells; type I pneumocytes replaced by type II → hyaline membranes + fluid-filled alveoli
SputumProductive, green/yellow (neutrophils + debris + dead bacteria)Classically nonproductive
Imaging patternLobar consolidation (dense, well-demarcated opacity)Patchy, interstitial/peri-hilar infiltrates (sporadic, branches out from the bronchioles, not a solid consolidated block)
Classic symptoms more likelyChest pain, rigors, heavy productive coughMyalgias, headache, fatigue, nonproductive cough — lower probability of chest pain/rigors than bacterial

The pathology-level explanation

In bacterial pneumonia, opened capillaries and neutrophil influx cause edema that fills the alveolar space — a fairly compact, localized process that resolves once macrophages clear the debris. In viral pneumonia, infected pneumocytes die as virus bursts through the cell membrane; the resulting hyaline membranes (thick pink protein/debris bands lining the alveoli, seen together with type II pneumocyte proliferation and fluid-filled alveolar walls) are what thicken the alveolar-capillary interface and impair gas exchange — this is why viral pneumonia patients get short of breath even when the process looks patchier on imaging than a lobar bacterial consolidation.

The chest x-ray timing trap
Radiographic changes lag clinical resolution by 6-8 weeks in both bacterial and viral pneumonia. Never order a chest x-ray just to confirm a patient is improving or clearing their pneumonia — those changes will persist long after the patient feels better. The only reason to repeat a chest x-ray is if the patient is getting worse.
Check yourself — 3 questions

A chest x-ray shows a dense, well-demarcated area of consolidation in one lobe with air bronchograms, in a patient with a productive green cough and rigors. Which pathophysiologic process and pathogen category best fits this presentation?

Lobar consolidation with productive, purulent (green/yellow) sputum and rigors is the classic bacterial pattern — driven by neutrophil influx and alveolar edema, distinct from the lymphocyte/hyaline-membrane pattern of viral pneumonia.

A patient recovering well clinically from pneumonia (afebrile, eating, ambulating normally) still has a hazy, incompletely resolved chest x-ray at the 2-week follow-up visit. What is the most appropriate next step?

Chest x-ray changes lag clinical improvement by roughly 6-8 weeks in both bacterial and viral pneumonia; imaging is not used to track response to treatment, and repeat imaging is reserved for patients who are clinically worsening.

Which alveolar finding is characteristic of viral pneumonia's pathophysiology and directly contributes to impaired gas exchange by thickening the alveolar-capillary interface?

Viral pneumonia is characterized by destruction of infected pneumocytes, hyaline membrane formation, and replacement of type I with type II pneumocytes — thickening the alveolar wall and impairing oxygen exchange.
Part 1 · Host Factors

2. Why Viral Pneumonia Hits the Very Young and Very Old Hardest

Viral pneumonia classically presents more severely at the extremes of age — but the underlying immune explanation differs at each end.

Older patients — dysfunctional resident memory T cells

A mouse influenza study found that aged lungs accumulate intrinsically dysfunctional CD8+ tissue-resident memory (TRM) cells, driven by elevated TGF-β. These dysfunctional TRM cells respond poorly to TCR stimulation and are weak antiviral effectors — yet they still drive persistent pulmonary inflammation and fibrosis, worsening morbidity/mortality even though they aren't clearing virus effectively. In short: more inflammation, less actual antiviral benefit.

Younger patients — immunologic immaturity

In children, the mechanism is simpler: the immune system either hasn't been challenged before (no prior exposure/memory response) or is not yet mature enough to mount a fully effective, well-regulated response.

Same outcome, different mechanism
Don't conflate the two explanations on an exam. Elderly = a specific dysfunctional CD8+ TRM/TGF-beta-driven inflammatory phenotype. Young children = immunologic immaturity/naivety. Both land in the same 'more severe disease at the extremes of age' bucket, but for different reasons.
Check yourself — 2 questions

A study of aged mice recovering from influenza pneumonia found elevated TGF-beta and accumulation of dysfunctional CD8+ tissue-resident memory cells. What is the net clinical effect of this finding?

The dysfunctional CD8+ TRM cells in aged lungs are poor antiviral effectors but still drive ongoing inflammation and fibrosis — worse outcomes despite (or because of) an active but ineffective immune response.

Why do young children also tend to have more severe presentations of viral pneumonia, despite not having the same dysfunctional CD8+ T-cell phenotype described in aged mice?

In children, severity at the young end of the age spectrum is generally attributed to an immature or previously unchallenged immune system, a distinct mechanism from the dysfunctional T-cell-driven inflammation seen in aging.

3. The Causative Agents — DNA vs. RNA Viruses

DNA viruses are the minority cause of lower respiratory tract infection (other than adenovirus); RNA viruses are the classic, high-impact group and the focus of the rest of this guide.

Ranking by impact

Dr. Riesberg's own framing for the rest of this lecture: rank these agents by virulence, social impact, and long-term health consequences — virulence being a pathogen's ability to cause damage to a host. The tour runs from the “bad” (rhinovirus) to the “ugly” (coronaviruses), which is exactly the order the remaining sections follow.

Check yourself — 2 questions

Which family of DNA viruses is a more common cause of lower respiratory tract infection than the herpesvirus group (HSV, VZV, CMV)?

Among DNA viruses, adenovirus is a more common cause of lower respiratory tract infection than the herpesvirus family, which is a less frequent cause overall.

RSV, parainfluenza, measles, mumps, and human metapneumovirus all belong to which viral family?

RSV, parainfluenza types 1-4, measles, mumps, and human metapneumovirus are all paramyxoviruses.
Part 2 · Ranking the Pathogens

4. Rhinovirus — “The Bad”

Exceptionally common, low virulence, but massively contagious.

  • Picornavirus — tiny, naked (non-enveloped), icosahedral, positive-sense RNA.
  • >100 serotypes — too many to bother serotyping most of them clinically.
  • Small infectious dose needed → highly contagious, airborne due to small particle size.
  • Accounts for 1/3 to 1/2 of all upper respiratory infections every fall/winter (persists into summer too).

Clinical presentation

Matches allergic rhinitis — sore throat, runny nose, congestion — minus the itchy eyes. If itchy eyes are present, think allergy instead.

Treatment: supportive care only.

The one differentiator to remember
Rhinovirus symptoms mimic allergic rhinitis almost exactly — the presence or absence of itchy eyes is the practical bedside clue: itchy eyes point to allergy, their absence points to rhinovirus.
Check yourself — 2 questions

A patient presents with sore throat, runny nose, and congestion, but specifically denies any itchy eyes. Which diagnosis is favored by the absence of this particular symptom?

Rhinovirus presents very similarly to allergic rhinitis, but classically without itchy eyes — a practical clue that favors viral rhinitis over an allergic cause.

What structural and epidemiologic features of rhinovirus make it responsible for up to half of all upper respiratory infections each fall and winter?

Rhinovirus's small, non-enveloped icosahedral structure and low infectious dose make it highly contagious and airborne, contributing to its dominance among URIs each season.

5. The Paramyxoviruses — RSV, Parainfluenza & Human Metapneumovirus

Worse than rhinovirus mainly for its social impact — the pediatric presentations are dramatic and worrying for parents, even when the child is often clinically stable.

Presentation

  • Young children: upper respiratory symptoms, but also lower tract disease (bronchiolitis, pneumonia). Classic finding: croup — a barking, seal-like cough with stridor (noisy inhalation/exhalation).
  • Even when the child is doing "clinically okay," croup causes major sleep disturbance, irritability, and poor feeding — the disease burden is as much about disruption as danger.
  • Adults: generally just upper respiratory infection symptoms.

Treatment

Ribavirin interferes with viral RNA synthesis and can slow symptoms — but is NOT indicated for an otherwise healthy, immunocompetent child with croup or another paramyxovirus infection. Primary treatment is supportive care (antipyretics, hydration).

The parent-reported 'cure' that isn't
Parents frequently report that a hot shower or a blast of cold air (e.g., the car ride to the ED) resolves their child's croup symptoms. There is no clinical evidence supporting this as treatment — it's a real, commonly reported phenomenon, but not something to recommend or rely on.
Check yourself — 2 questions

A toddler presents with a barking, seal-like cough and audible stridor on both inspiration and expiration, worse at night, with the parent otherwise describing the child as playful during the day. Which viral family is classically responsible for this presentation?

Croup — barking cough with stridor — is a classic presentation of paramyxovirus infections (RSV, parainfluenza, human metapneumovirus) in young children.

A parent of an otherwise healthy, immunocompetent child with croup asks about prescribing ribavirin after reading about it online. What is the appropriate response?

Although ribavirin does interfere with viral RNA synthesis and can slow symptoms, it is not indicated for otherwise healthy, immunocompetent children with croup or other paramyxovirus infections — supportive care remains primary treatment.

6. Influenza — “A Real Bad Boy”

History — the 1918 pandemic

Started in Haskell County, Kansas in 1917, spread through a US Army training camp and then worldwide via WWI troop movements. Killed an estimated 50 million people, with case-fatality rates >2.5% (vs. <0.1% for other flu pandemics). Unusually, it hit young, healthy adults hard (atypical for flu, which classically hits the very young/old) — largely because of bacterial co-infection/super-infection driving the high lethality.

Virology

Orthomyxovirus. Two key surface spikes: hemagglutinin (H) and neuraminidase (N) — these antigens shift and mutate annually (e.g., "H5N1" names the specific H and N subtypes), which is why the WHO has to make an annual "best guess" at which strains will predominate for that season's vaccine.

Clinical presentation

Sudden-onset cough, fever, URI symptoms, fatigue, joint/muscle aches, headache, sore throat — a true flu-like illness that "hits you like a truck," distinct from a common cold. Highly contagious — isolate symptomatic patients, quarantine exposed contacts.

Why influenza is uniquely dangerous

Higher incidence of bacterial co-infection/super-infection with Staph aureus, Strep pneumoniae, or H. influenzae — this is what makes flu disease much more serious than most other respiratory viruses, and was the major driver of 1918 mortality.

Treatment — targeting neuraminidase

Oseltamivir (Tamiflu), zanamivir (Relenza), peramivir (Rapivab) — all attack the neuraminidase spike. Can shorten symptom duration/severity, and can be used as prophylaxis for exposed household contacts or higher-risk individuals. Most effective within the first 24 hours of symptom onset; largely ineffective after 48 hours for treating active symptoms (prophylactic use in contacts is a separate indication).

Flu shot myths

  • “The flu shot gave me the flu” — false; it contains no live virus. The cytokine/inflammatory response to the antigen can feel like a mild, transient "mini-flu" (sore arm, feeling run-down for a day) — be upfront with patients about this rather than denying it happens.
  • “I'm not putting foreign stuff in my body” — the body already handles thousands of foreign substances daily (bacteria, fungi, pollens); the vaccine antigen is a drop in that bucket.
Check yourself — 4 questions

The 1918 influenza pandemic was unusual in that it caused severe disease and high mortality in young, otherwise healthy adults rather than the classic extremes of age. What was the primary driver of this atypically high lethality?

The 1918 pandemic's high case-fatality rate (>2.5%) in young, healthy adults was largely attributed to bacterial co-infection and super-infection, a hallmark complication of severe influenza.

A patient asks why a new flu vaccine formulation is needed every year, unlike some other vaccines. What is the underlying virologic reason?

Influenza's hemagglutinin (H) and neuraminidase (N) surface antigens undergo frequent antigenic shift and mutation, requiring the WHO to predict likely circulating strains and reformulate the vaccine annually.

A patient presents 30 hours after the onset of classic flu symptoms. Which antiviral class is most appropriate, and how does timing affect its benefit?

Neuraminidase inhibitors (oseltamivir, zanamivir, peramivir) are most beneficial when started within the first 24-48 hours of symptom onset; after 48 hours, they offer little benefit for shortening the course of active illness.

A patient declines the flu vaccine, stating it once gave them the flu. What is the most accurate explanation for what they likely experienced?

Inactivated flu vaccines contain no live virus and cannot cause influenza; the immune/cytokine response to the antigen can produce transient, mild symptoms resembling a 'mini-flu,' which is worth acknowledging honestly with patients.

7. Coronaviruses — From Nothing-Virus to Global Pandemic

Historically a low-interest family — responsible for only about 20% of common colds — until three successive emergences over less than two decades changed that completely.

The three major coronavirus emergences
Year / NameOriginPresentationMortalityWhy it did/didn't spread globally
2002 — SARS (severe acute respiratory syndrome)ChinaSevere clinical disease~11%High mortality/severity made cases easy to identify, isolate, and trace — contained relatively effectively
2012 — MERS (Middle East respiratory syndrome)Saudi Arabia; zoonotic, camel-to-humanFever, cough, pronounced shortness of breath~35%Extremely high mortality paradoxically limited global spread — most infected patients became severely ill and died quickly, curbing onward transmission
2019 — SARS-CoV-2 / COVID-19Wuhan, ChinaHighly variable, from asymptomatic to ARDS/deathInitial estimates 4–12%; stabilized around ~2.5% by end of first yearLower mortality + significant asymptomatic/mild transmission → much harder to contain, leading to a true global pandemic
A number worth memorizing
SARS-CoV-2's mortality rate stabilized around 2.5% during 2020 — the exact same case-fatality rate as the 1918 influenza pandemic. High mortality that kills patients quickly (like MERS's 35%) can actually limit a virus's global spread, while a lower, more survivable mortality rate paired with pre-symptomatic transmission (like COVID-19) is what allows a true pandemic.
Check yourself — 2 questions

MERS had a case-fatality rate around 35%, dramatically higher than SARS-CoV-2's roughly 2.5%. Yet MERS never became a global pandemic on the scale of COVID-19. What is the paradoxical explanation?

Counterintuitively, MERS's very high mortality rate limited its global spread, since severely ill and rapidly dying patients had less opportunity to transmit the virus widely compared to a virus with milder or delayed symptoms.

Which coronavirus emergence was successfully contained largely because its high mortality and severe clinical presentation made cases easy to identify and isolate?

2002 SARS caused severe, easily recognizable disease with high mortality, which allowed for relatively effective identification, isolation, and contact tracing that helped contain its spread.
Part 2 · Case Study of a Virus Gone Bad

8. SARS-CoV-2 — Pathogenesis, Clinical Course & Treatment

Terminology

COVID-19 is the clinical syndrome; SARS-CoV-2 is the causative agent — keep these distinct.

Pathogenesis

SARS-CoV-2 binds the ACE2 receptor (angiotensin converting enzyme 2), most heavily expressed on respiratory epithelium but present throughout the body in varying amounts (kidney, heart, GI tract). The virus internalizes and replicates, triggering an inflammatory reaction — early in the pandemic this sometimes escalated to fatal cytokine storms. Because ACE2 is expressed outside the lungs, SARS-CoV-2 caused unusually broad systemic effects: cardiac involvement and clotting abnormalities (including pulmonary emboli, both microvascular and macrovascular).

A cautionary case
An extremely fit Ironman triathlete developed COVID-19, was short of breath with a limited oxygen requirement for 5-7 days, and convalesced over about 8 weeks. On returning to training, he suffered a fatal massive MI with no family history of cardiac disease — postmortem autopsy revealed persisting inflammation, likely driving coronary thrombosis as a delayed consequence of the inflammatory cascade triggered by the infection.

Clinical course

  • Incubation ~5–7 days.
  • Early/unique symptom: loss of taste and smell — distinctly associated with COVID-19 more than other respiratory viruses.
  • ~1/3 of hypoxic patients can be entirely asymptomatic — "happy hypoxia" (oxygen saturations in the mid-80s with no dyspnea).
  • Can progress to ARDS and death, or remain mild.
  • Other organ involvement: GI, kidney, heart, coagulation system, CNS — reflecting widespread ACE2 distribution.
  • Long COVID: persisting symptoms (fatigue prominent) in an estimated 10–15% of patients; pathophysiology unclear but thought to involve persisting inflammatory mediators.

Treatment

Remdesivir, molnupiravir, and nirmatrelvir + ritonavir (Paxlovid) were the major antivirals to reach approval, reducing severity, hospitalization risk, and in some populations mortality, especially in high-risk patients.

Vaccines — mRNA technology

A genuinely new technology: the mRNA vaccine uses the body's own cellular machinery to produce a viral antigen, which the immune system then responds to and clears. Myth to correct: the mRNA does not persist in the body — once the antigen is produced, the mRNA strand degrades, the immune response runs its course, and nothing but immune memory remains. Ongoing viral antigenic shift means vaccine efficacy will continue to be highly variable over time.

Check yourself — 4 questions

A patient with COVID-19 has an oxygen saturation of 84% but reports no dyspnea and appears comfortable. What is this phenomenon called, and what does it illustrate about SARS-CoV-2?

'Happy hypoxia' describes the unusual finding of significant hypoxemia (oxygen saturations in the 80s) without a corresponding sense of dyspnea, seen in a notable subset of COVID-19 patients.

A patient asks whether the mRNA from their COVID-19 vaccine remains in their body indefinitely. What is the accurate response?

mRNA vaccines use transient genetic material: once it directs production of the viral antigen, it is degraded by normal cellular processes, leaving behind only the immune system's memory response — the claim that it persists in the body is a myth.

Why did SARS-CoV-2 produce clinically significant effects in organs outside the lungs, such as the heart, kidneys, and coagulation system?

Because ACE2 is expressed in the heart, kidneys, GI tract, and vasculature in addition to the lungs, SARS-CoV-2 infection and its downstream inflammatory and coagulation effects were not confined to the respiratory tract.

Nirmatrelvir/ritonavir (Paxlovid), remdesivir, and molnupiravir were developed as treatments for COVID-19. In general, what clinical benefit did these antivirals demonstrate, particularly in high-risk patients?

These antivirals were found to reduce disease severity, hospitalization risk, and — particularly in high-risk patients — mortality related to COVID-19.

Check yourself — 2 questions

A patient reports new loss of sense of smell along with cough and fatigue. Based on relative symptom frequency across common respiratory illnesses, which diagnosis does this symptom most specifically point toward?

Loss of taste or smell is essentially never seen with allergies, the common cold, or influenza, but occurs 'sometimes' with COVID-19 — making it a relatively specific (though not universal) clue for that diagnosis.

Which symptom is a poor discriminator among the common cold, influenza, and COVID-19 because it occurs 'often' in all three?

Cough is frequently present in the common cold, influenza, and COVID-19 alike, making it a poor standalone discriminator between these diagnoses — unlike loss of taste/smell or shortness of breath, which show more differential patterns.
Part 3 · Testing & Stewardship

10. Diagnostic Testing — Viral PCR Panels

Multiplex viral PCR panels (e.g., BioFire) replaced the old, slow, and often futile approach of trying to culture respiratory viruses (which don't grow nearly as readily as bacteria).

Advantages

  • Turnaround time as fast as 45 minutes.
  • Speciates among many common respiratory viruses (and some bacteria) in a single run.
  • A genuinely useful antibiotic stewardship tool — confirming a viral etiology (e.g., rhinovirus, a "nothing" pathogen you can't treat) supports withholding unnecessary antibiotics, while identifying flu lets you actually prescribe a targeted antiviral.
  • Many patients are simply reassured by finally knowing a specific cause for their symptoms.

Limitations

  • Somewhat expensive.
  • Standard respiratory viral panels do not include SARS, MERS, or SARS-CoV-2 — those require separate/dedicated testing.
  • Up to 15% of asymptomatic people can have a "colonizing" virus detected — a positive result doesn't automatically mean that virus is the cause of the patient's current symptoms.
  • Significant rates of bacterial co-infection exist even when a virus is identified — a positive viral PCR doesn't rule out a concurrent bacterial process requiring treatment.
Don't over-trust a positive result
A positive viral PCR in an asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic patient may just reflect colonization (up to 15% of cases), not the actual cause of illness — and even a genuine, symptomatic viral infection doesn't exclude a bacterial co-infection that still needs antibiotics.
Check yourself — 3 questions

A multiplex respiratory viral PCR panel returns positive for coronavirus in an asymptomatic patient being tested for an unrelated reason. What is the most important limitation to keep in mind when interpreting this result?

Because up to 15% of asymptomatic individuals can have a detectable colonizing virus, a positive PCR result must be interpreted in the context of the patient's actual clinical presentation, not treated as automatic proof of causative infection.

A clinician orders a standard multiplex respiratory viral PCR panel to evaluate a patient with severe pneumonia and suspects SARS-CoV-2. Why might this standard panel fail to identify the cause?

Standard multiplex respiratory viral PCR panels commonly exclude SARS, MERS, and SARS-CoV-2, which require separate, dedicated testing to identify.

How does rapid viral PCR testing support antibiotic stewardship in patients with respiratory symptoms?

Rapid, specific viral identification helps clinicians confidently withhold antibiotics when a virus (not amenable to antibacterial treatment) is confirmed, while also flagging treatable viral infections like influenza — both of which support responsible antibiotic use.
