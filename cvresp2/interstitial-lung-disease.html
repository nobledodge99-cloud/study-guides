Interstitial lung disease — framework & common findings

Interstitial lung diseases (ILDs) are a large, heterogeneous collection of disease processes, not caused by one specific condition. What they share is involvement of the pulmonary parenchyma, typically with a combination of inflammation and fibrosis. There is no single universal classification system — this guide organizes them into 4 categories to make studying easier.

Common findings across ILDs

  • Shortness of breath is universal
  • Often diffuse disease on imaging
  • Restrictive pattern on PFTs (with a couple of exceptions) — FVC <80% predicted
  • Alterations in alveolar/airway architecture, often with an elevated A-a gradient
  • Fever is uncommon in most
  • Clubbing — generally only in IPF; more likely to reflect malignancy elsewhere
  • Extrapulmonary symptoms/signs are rare — if present, think connective tissue disease, vasculitis, or sarcoidosis
  • Hemoptysis, chest pain, and wheezing are relatively uncommon — notable exceptions: hemoptysis with granulomatosis with polyangiitis (Wegener's) and Goodpasture syndrome
  • Diagnosis often requires biopsy — transbronchial biopsy via bronchoscopy is easier; thoracoscopic or open lung biopsy may be needed depending on disease distribution
  • Treatment: removing the causative exposure is one of the best options; many ILDs simply don't have good treatment options
Clinical pearlBuilding a comparison table across these 12 axes as you study is explicitly Dr. Kinder's own suggested strategy for this topic — it is a very efficient way to keep dozens of similar-sounding diseases straight.

Ways to compare and organize the ILDs as you study

Which processes have eosinophilia · which have fever · smoking as a cause/risk factor · treatment · age · time course of onset · kidney involvement · imaging characteristics (ground glass, nodules, honeycombing, reticular, distribution) · in which is bronchoalveolar lavage helpful · which have an associated pneumothorax · DLCO · exposure history.

Category 1 — Exposure-Related Interstitial Lung Disease

Hypersensitivity pneumonitis

An immune-mediated reaction to inhaled organic compounds — an antigen-antibody complex forms and that complex causes the inflammation. Consider it in patients with recurrent or persistent pneumonia. Presents in acute, subacute, and chronic forms.

Treatment: for acute forms, corticosteroids help symptoms but do not change the course of the disease.

Classic organic-antigen examples

SyndromeExposure
Farmer's LungThermophilic actinomycetes in moldy hay
Bird Fancier's / Bird Breeder's LungPet birds — dust from feces/feathers
Air Conditioner LungAir conditioning systems (contaminated with organic antigen)
Isocyanate-induced HPIsocyanates (industrial chemical exposure)
Clinical pearlHypersensitivity pneumonitis is immune-mediated — an antigen + antibody complex drives the inflammation. Contrast this with byssinosis below, which needs no sensitization at all.

Byssinosis (organic dust, non-immune)

Caused by inhalation of cotton, flax, or hemp dust. Unlike hypersensitivity pneumonitis, byssinosis is not immune-mediated — no sensitization is needed. Early: occasional chest tightness. Late: regular chest tightness by the end of Monday (the classic "Monday fever"/textile-mill pattern, reflecting re-exposure after a weekend away from the mill).

Pneumoconioses — inorganic dust diseases

Caused by inhalation of inorganic dusts: coal worker's pneumoconiosis, asbestosis, silicosis, and berylliosis. Full detail for each is below in its own dedicated section (from the Pneumoconiosis handout) — this entry is the placeholder within the exposure-related ILD category.

Clinical pearlOrganize the pneumoconioses by dust type: silica (silicosis), coal dust (CWP), asbestos fibers (asbestosis), and beryllium (berylliosis/chronic beryllium disease, a sarcoidosis mimicker).

Radiation pneumonitis & medication-induced lung disease

Radiation-induced lung injury (RILI)

Radiation pneumonitis follows thoracic radiation exposure — listed in Category 1 as an exposure-related ILD.

Medication-induced

DrugKey points
AmiodaroneRisk relates to high cumulative dose — doses >400 mg/day, and duration over months
NitrofurantoinHas acute and subacute forms, thought to be more of a hypersensitivity reaction; also a chronic form related to total lifetime dose
MethotrexateCan cause pneumonitis; also relevant as a treatment for rheumatoid arthritis that can itself be a cause of lung disease (see Category 2)
Clinical pearlAmiodarone lung toxicity tracks with cumulative dose (>400mg/day, months of exposure); nitrofurantoin has both a hypersensitivity-type acute/subacute presentation and a separate chronic, lifetime-cumulative-dose-related form — don't lump them into one mechanism.

Category 2 — Secondary to Connective Tissue (Collagen Vascular) Disease

Collagen vascular disease of the lung

DiseaseKey lung-related facts
Systemic sclerosis (scleroderma)Associated with SCL-70 antibody; CREST syndrome; fibrosis of lung and arterial intimal thickening → severe pulmonary hypertension; increased risk of bronchogenic carcinoma
Rheumatoid arthritis~1/3 of RA patients get ILD; pleural effusion with very low glucose (normally only seen in empyema); necrobiotic nodules in the upper lung zones; treatment for RA (methotrexate, or historically gold) can itself be the cause of lung disease; increased risk of bronchogenic carcinoma
Mixed connective tissue diseaseLung involvement in 75%; associated with anti-RNP (ribonucleoprotein) antibody
Polymyositis-dermatomyositisCan cause ILD (part of the collagen vascular category)
Lupus (SLE)Can cause ILD (part of the collagen vascular category)
Clinical pearlAn RA pleural effusion with a very low glucose mimics empyema biochemically — but the clinical context (known RA, chronicity) should point you to a rheumatoid effusion instead of infection.

Category 3 — Idiopathic Interstitial Pneumonias

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF)

DomainFindings
EtiologyMajority of all interstitial lung disease; a diagnosis of exclusion
Symptoms/signsNo extrapulmonary signs; may have clubbing of the fingers
DiagnosticsDLCO is low. CXR: diffuse reticulonodular disease, bibasilar (base and up the sides), honeycomb appearance. Open lung biopsy may be needed. Pulmonary hypertension is prognostic — higher = worse.
TreatmentNo good options. Pirfenidone and nintedanib slow FVC reduction (do not reverse disease)
Prognosis50% mortality at 3 years
Clinical pearlIPF is the disease in this whole topic most classically associated with clubbing — remember the common-findings rule: clubbing in an ILD points you toward IPF (or, more broadly, malignancy).

Nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP)

DomainFindings
Etiology/pathophysiologyWide range of possible causes — hence "non-specific"
DiagnosisLung biopsy often needed; often with bibasilar ground glass or reticular changes on imaging

Cryptogenic organizing pneumonia (COP)

DomainFindings
Signs/symptomsAssociated with fever; progresses faster than most other ILDs
DiagnosticsCXR: diffuse, patchy infiltrates; inflammation of small airways and alveoli. Open lung biopsy; migratory areas on imaging; Masson bodies on pathology
TreatmentMostly responds well to steroids, slowly tapered over 6 months; some recover spontaneously
OtherUsually initially diagnosed as pneumonia — the tell is that it fails several courses of antibiotics
Clinical pearlCOP is the ILD that most looks like pneumonia clinically (fever, patchy infiltrates) — the clue that it isn't infectious pneumonia is failure of multiple antibiotic courses plus migratory infiltrates.

Respiratory bronchiolitis — interstitial lung disease (RB-ILD)

DomainFindings
Etiology/pathologyStrong association with smoking; similar to DIP — some suggest these are the same underlying process
DiagnosisUpper lobes; centrilobular ground glass nodules; bronchial wall thickening without fibrosis

Desquamative interstitial pneumonia (DIP)

DomainFindings
Etiology/pathophysiologyFilled with alveolar macrophages; the name comes from a historical misunderstanding — these cells were originally thought to be desquamated pneumocytes. Strong association with smoking (>80%)
TreatmentVariable success with corticosteroids and immunosuppressants
Clinical pearlDIP and RB-ILD are both overwhelmingly smoking-related and are thought by some to represent the same disease spectrum — a favorite paired fact.

Acute interstitial pneumonia (AIP) — Hamman-Rich syndrome

DomainFindings
Etiology/pathophysiologyRare; middle age or older; smoking is NOT associated with increased risk
Signs/symptomsThe most acute of all the ILDs, with rapid progression; can have fever
DiagnosisARDS picture; diffuse alveolar damage
TreatmentSupportive; corticosteroids often used, but usefulness not established; lung transplant. 60% die in the first 6 months (median survival ~1½ months) — survivors usually regain lung function completely
Clinical pearlAIP is the ILD to think of when a patient develops an ARDS-like picture with diffuse alveolar damage and no identifiable cause — its all-or-nothing course (rapid death vs. complete recovery) is unusual among ILDs.

Lymphocytic interstitial pneumonia (LIP)

DomainFindings
Etiology/pathophysiologyLymphoproliferative (both monoclonal and polyclonal); associated with Epstein-Barr virus and HIV; can also be autoimmune
Signs/symptomsFever
DiagnosisLung biopsy; lymphocytosis
TreatmentNo treatment needed if asymptomatic; corticosteroids sometimes help symptoms; antiretroviral therapy if HIV-associated

Category 4 — Miscellaneous

Sarcoidosis

A systemic granulomatous disease of unknown etiology that can affect almost any organ. Most common between 40-60 years. In the U.S., incidence is 3.5x higher in Black Americans than White Americans; women have a higher incidence than men. Up to 50% of patients are asymptomatic at diagnosis, often found incidentally on a screening chest x-ray showing enlarged hilar/mediastinal nodes.

Lofgren's syndrome

An acute presentation: erythema nodosum + arthritis + bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy. Erythema nodosum is more common in women, arthritis more common in men. Lofgren's syndrome has a favorable prognosis and, alone, is pathognomonic for sarcoidosis — it may not require biopsy confirmation.

Organ involvement

SystemKey facts
Pulmonary>90% of patients — mediastinal/hilar lymphadenopathy and/or parenchymal disease; non-caseating granuloma pathologically (nonspecific); 50% of patients have only respiratory symptoms
Skin25-35% — lesions favor scars/tattoos; erythema nodosum in 10% (good prognostic sign; biopsy of erythema nodosum is not useful — not sarcoid granulomas); lupus pernio affects nose/cheeks/ears
Ocular>25% — lacrimal gland enlargement, conjunctival nodules (biopsy-able), uveitis; ocular symptoms can precede pulmonary disease by up to 20 years
CardiacGranulomas in 25% at autopsy, but only clinically suspected in 5%; AV block, ventricular arrhythmias, LV dysfunction, sudden death — most common cause of death from sarcoidosis (not the most common complication, just the most lethal one)
NeurologicGranulomas in 25% at autopsy; 10% symptomatic; can precede diagnosis in up to 75% of neurosarcoidosis cases; CN VII, II, VIII most commonly affected; myelopathy/peripheral neuropathy possible
Liver/spleenFever/weight loss/jaundice in <5%; hepatomegaly in 20%, splenomegaly in 5-15%
Bone/jointMost have arthralgias; 35% develop arthritis — ankles and knees most common
Calcium metabolismVitamin D dysregulation in 50% — granulomas activate vitamin D, pulling calcium from bone, causing hypercalcemia, hypercalciuria, nephrolithiasis, nephrocalcinosis; 10-14% of chronic patients have a symptomatic renal stone
Clinical pearlErythema nodosum biopsy is a trap — it is NOT useful for diagnosing sarcoidosis, because the erythema nodosum lesion itself does not contain sarcoid granulomas, even though its presence (as part of Lofgren's syndrome) is a strong diagnostic and prognostic clue.

Diagnosis & testing

Made on clinical + radiographic + histologic evidence of non-caseating granulomas in one or more organs (except Lofgren's, which may stand alone). Biopsy the safest accessible site — skin, peripheral lymph nodes, lacrimal glands, or conjunctiva. Bronchoscopy with transbronchial lung biopsy has a diagnostic yield of at least 85%. BAL may show lymphocytosis and an elevated CD4/CD8 ratio, but this is not diagnostic. ACE is produced by sarcoid granulomas and is elevated in 60% of patients — but this is nonspecific (not sensitive or specific).

Chest x-ray staging

StageFindings
0Normal
1Bilateral hilar adenopathy
2Bilateral hilar adenopathy + parenchymal infiltrates
3Parenchymal infiltrates without lymphadenopathy
4Advanced parenchymal disease with fibrosis, bullae, cavities

PFT: restrictive ventilatory defect (can have a concurrent obstructive component if bronchi are compressed by nodes). DLCO: decreased. Also obtain EKG/cardiac monitoring, echocardiography, CBC, CMP (liver/renal/calcium), 24-hour urine (hypercalciuria), and an ophthalmology exam. Serum ACE is not sensitive or specific.

Differential diagnosis

Infection (mycobacteria, fungi, spirochetes, bacteria, parasites), neoplasia (lymphoma, carcinoma), extrinsic allergic alveolitis (bird fancier's lung, farmer's lung), chemical (silica, beryllium), immunologic (Crohn's disease, primary biliary cirrhosis, vasculitis/Wegener's).

Treatment & prognosis

Treat only symptomatic patients — ~75% recover without corticosteroids. Prednisone 20-40 mg daily for 3 months, monitoring FVC for improvement; if improved, wean to 10-15 mg daily for 6-9 months then taper off. Cytotoxic/immunosuppressive agents for nonresponders. Spontaneous remission in 50% at 3 years; Lofgren's has the best prognosis; 2/3 remission by 10 years; 1/3 have unrelenting disease. Less than 5% die from sarcoidosis, usually from cardiac arrhythmias.

Pulmonary vasculitis disorders

Granulomatosis with polyangiitis (Wegener's granulomatosis)

DomainFindings
EtiologyVasculitis
Signs/symptomsUpper respiratory tract involvement (sinus disease); renal involvement; blood in urine or hemoptysis — think Wegener's or Goodpasture whenever hemoptysis is a presenting complaint in an ILD-type illness
DiagnosisC-ANCA positive; biopsy lung or nasal mucosa — not kidney biopsy
TreatmentHigh-dose steroids + cyclophosphamide

Eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (Churg-Strauss syndrome)

DomainFindings
EtiologyNecrotizing small-vessel vasculitis with eosinophilic infiltration; classic association: asthma + a leukotriene receptor antagonist (e.g., montelukast/Singulair) and no concurrent corticosteroid
DiagnosisCXR infiltrates; labs show eosinophilia
TreatmentStop the leukotriene receptor antagonist; mepolizumab
Clinical pearlChurg-Strauss (EGPA) classically 'unmasks' in an asthmatic being weaned off corticosteroids onto a leukotriene receptor antagonist — the LTRA doesn't cause the vasculitis, but withdrawing steroid control that was suppressing it does. First step in management is stopping the LTRA.

Goodpasture syndrome

DomainFindings
Etiology/pathophysiologyMore common in males
Signs/symptomsRapid presentation; lung AND kidney involvement; no nasal symptoms (unlike Wegener's)
DiagnosisAnti-GBM antibodies (anti-glomerular basement membrane); linear pattern on electron microscopy; C3 and IgG deposits
TreatmentPlasmapheresis; nephrectomy if pulmonary hemorrhage is severe
Clinical pearlGoodpasture vs. Wegener's: both cause pulmonary-renal syndromes with hemoptysis, but Goodpasture has NO nasal/sinus involvement and is anti-GBM positive, while Wegener's has upper airway disease and is C-ANCA positive.

Eosinophilic lung diseases

Eosinophilic pneumonia comes in 3 types. Eosinophilic lung disease can also be caused by parasitic infections — always rule out parasites.

Acute eosinophilic pneumonia

Rapid onset with fever. CXR looks like ARDS. Treatment: corticosteroids.

Chronic eosinophilic pneumonia

Middle-aged women; 50% have asthma. CXR: infiltrates described as the "photonegative of pulmonary edema" (peripheral rather than central infiltrates). Labs: high ESR. Bronchoalveolar lavage shows eosinophils. Treatment: corticosteroids — relapses are common.

Acute bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA)

An allergic reaction to Aspergillus — bronchiectasis comes first, then Aspergillus moves in and the body becomes sensitized (Type I and Type III/immune-complex response). Signs: brownish sputum. CXR: recurrent infiltrates, "tram-track" bronchial markings. Labs: very high IgE. Treatment: long-term corticosteroids — counterintuitive, since Aspergillus itself is non-invasive here and this is fundamentally an allergic reaction; adding itraconazole can help.

Clinical pearlChronic eosinophilic pneumonia's 'photonegative of pulmonary edema' pattern — peripheral infiltrates sparing the central lung, the reverse of cardiogenic pulmonary edema's central/perihilar pattern — is one of the single most test-favorite radiographic descriptions in this whole topic.

Pulmonary Langerhans cell histiocytosis (eosinophilic granuloma)

DomainFindings
Etiology/pathophysiologyRisk factors: smoking, male sex; increased lung volume — one of the only ILDs that is non-restrictive
Signs/symptoms10% present with pneumothorax; posterior pituitary involvement → lytic bone lesions, diabetes insipidus
DiagnosisSmall cystic spaces in the upper lung zones; Langerhans cells on BAL; Birbeck granules on electron microscopy ("little tennis rackets")
TreatmentStop smoking; corticosteroids can be considered but have limited success
Clinical pearlPLCH breaks the 'ILD = restrictive' rule — it has increased lung volume and is one of the only non-restrictive ILDs. Combine that with cystic upper-lobe disease, smoking history, and pneumothorax risk, and it should stand out from the rest of the group.

Idiopathic pulmonary hemosiderosis & pulmonary alveolar proteinosis

Idiopathic pulmonary hemosiderosis

DomainFindings
Etiology/pathophysiologyMacrophages filled with hemosiderin (a blood breakdown byproduct); does not affect the kidneys (distinguishing it from Goodpasture)
DiagnosisIncreased DLCO — hemoglobin deposited in the interstitium takes up inhaled CO during testing, but this does not mean diffusion is actually better; iron-deficiency anemia

Pulmonary alveolar proteinosis

DomainFindings
Etiology/pathophysiologyLungs fill up with defective alveolar macrophages, causing a buildup of surfactant (phosphatidylcholine) that interferes with gas exchange
DiagnosisCXR: "crazy-paving" pattern — not specific for this disease; open lung biopsy
TreatmentWhole lung lavage
Clinical pearlIdiopathic pulmonary hemosiderosis is one of the rare causes of an INCREASED DLCO (from CO uptake by interstitial hemoglobin) rather than the decreased DLCO typical of most ILDs — a useful outlier to remember.

Lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM)

DomainFindings
Etiology/pathophysiologyProliferation of smooth muscle throughout the lung
Signs/symptomsPre-menopausal women; spontaneous pneumothorax
DiagnosisHoneycombing throughout the lung ("lungs look like Swiss cheese"); many nodules and cysts; chylous pleural effusions
TreatmentLung transplantation is the only hope — disease can recur in the transplanted lung

Pneumoconioses — Detailed (Inorganic Dust Diseases)

Silicosis

A fibrotic lung disease from inhaling crystalline silica, usually as quartz. Industries at risk: mining, tunneling, excavating, quarrying, stonework, foundries, sandblasting, ceramics, and (more recently) denim jean manufacturing (sandblasting for a "stressed" look).

Clinical forms

FormTiming & features
Chronic silicosisMost common form — occurs ≥20 years after exposure. Hallmark pathology: the silicotic nodule — whorled hyalinized collagen fibers with a peripheral zone of dust-laden macrophages. May be asymptomatic or have dyspnea; productive cough is common
Accelerated silicosisNodules develop after 3-10 years of exposure; progressive course
Acute silicosis (silicoproteinosis)Develops 6 months-2 years after massive exposure; dyspnea, cough, weight loss, rapid progression to respiratory failure and death; pathology is an alveolar filling process
Progressive massive fibrosisLesions ≥1cm (often larger), usually upper lobes; leads to respiratory failure, cor pulmonale, weight loss, and death

Associated diseases

Tuberculosis (increased risk), COPD/chronic bronchitis, collagen vascular disease (RA and scleroderma increased), and increased lung cancer risk.

Chest x-ray: symmetric nodular pattern in the upper lobes; hilar adenopathy with eggshell calcification is strongly suggestive; PMF shows coalescence of nodules into larger masses; acute silicosis shows an air-space/interstitial pattern.

PFT: normal early in chronic silicosis, later a mixed obstructive/restrictive pattern. Diagnosis: exposure history + characteristic x-ray changes. Management: irreversible — avoid further exposure, TB testing, smoking cessation; consider lung transplant in acute/accelerated silicosis.

Clinical pearlEggshell calcification of hilar lymph nodes is essentially pathognomonic for silicosis (and can also occur in sarcoidosis, but is classically taught as the silicosis finding) — a favorite chest x-ray descriptor.

Coal worker's pneumoconiosis (CWP)

Results from deposition of coal dust in the lung. Risk increases with intensity of exposure and carbon content — anthracite is most toxic. Underground miners are at higher risk than surface miners.

Pathology: the "coal macule" — macrophages laden with coal dust within the walls of respiratory bronchioles and adjacent alveoli; coal nodules; progressive massive fibrosis may occur. Clinical presentation: usually no symptoms/signs; can have bronchitis symptoms; may progress to PMF.

Associated diseases

Silicosis is also common in coal workers; scleroderma and rheumatoid nodules are increased. Caplan syndrome: rheumatoid arthritis with large cavitary pulmonary nodules, associated with both silicosis and coal worker's pneumoconiosis. TB is not increased unless silicosis is also present; lung cancer risk is not increased (unlike silicosis).

Chest x-ray: resembles silicosis — small rounded opacities; can progress to PMF with nodules 0.5-5cm. PFT: normal early, often obstructive later (coal dust causes emphysema), restriction if fibrosis present. Diagnosis: coal dust exposure + CXR. Management: avoid exposure, stop smoking.

Clinical pearlSilicosis and CWP look alike on imaging and both can progress to PMF, but they diverge on associated risks: silicosis increases TB and lung cancer risk; CWP does not increase either unless silicosis co-exists. Caplan syndrome (RA + cavitary nodules) is shared by both.

Asbestosis

Chronic fibrotic ILD from prolonged inhalation of asbestos fibers. A 20-year latency between exposure and disease is common. Industries at risk: mining, milling, and transportation of asbestos; building demolition; brake lining; shipbuilding; insulation; fireproofing. (Asbestos use in construction was banned in the 1980s.)

Pathology: ferruginous bodies / asbestos bodies, found in sputum or BAL fluid. Symptoms: dyspnea, dry cough, chest tightness/pain. Signs: inspiratory basal crackles, clubbing.

Associated diseases

DiseaseKey facts
Mesothelioma30-40 year latency; incurable; 9-18 month survival
Lung cancerRisk 4-6x higher
Pleural effusionUsually small, exudative, blood-stained, contains leukocytes; most clear spontaneously

Chest x-ray: pleural plaques; pleural effusion with a 10-15 year latency; pleural thickening; rounded atelectasis with a "comet tail" sign; lower lobe and subpleural disease predominate. PFT: restrictive pattern, may see obstruction.

Diagnosis (exposure + lag time are essential, the rest confirmatory): (1) reliable history of asbestos exposure, (2) appropriate lag time between exposure and disease, (3) lung fibrosis on CXR/CT, (4) restrictive PFT, (5) bilateral inspiratory crackles, (6) clubbing of fingers/toes. Management: no effective treatment — avoid exposure, stop smoking, lung transplant.

Clinical pearlAsbestosis is the pneumoconiosis with the most 'named findings' to keep straight: ferruginous/asbestos bodies (pathology), comet-tail sign (rounded atelectasis on imaging), and its own uniquely long-latency malignant complication, mesothelioma.

Beryllium disease (berylliosis)

Industries at risk: aerospace, electronics, ceramics, metal, nuclear, telecommunications, tool and die, welding.

Clinical presentations

FormFeatures
Acute toxic pneumonitisHigh exposure → hypersensitivity response, now rare due to better recognition of beryllium-associated disease; symptoms: cough, chest pain; signs: blood-tinged sputum and crackles
Chronic beryllium diseaseClinical features similar to sarcoidosis, ranging from asymptomatic to severe granulomatous restrictive lung disease; dyspnea, cough, chest pain, weight loss, fatigue, arthralgias, crackles; onset ≥20 years after exposure

Chest x-ray: enlarged hilar/mediastinal lymph nodes, and/or multiple lung nodules; later stages show patchy fibrosis, hyperinflation, and honeycombing. PFT: restriction.

Diagnosis: documented beryllium exposure + evidence of lung disease + a positive BeLPT (beryllium lymphocyte proliferation test) performed on blood or BAL fluid. Management: avoid further exposure, stop smoking, steroids.

Clinical pearlChronic beryllium disease is a sarcoidosis mimicker at every level — granulomatous, restrictive, hilar adenopathy — which is exactly why an occupational/beryllium exposure history must be asked of every patient being worked up for sarcoidosis. The BeLPT is what separates the two.

Mesothelioma

A malignant tumor of the pleura most classically linked to asbestos exposure, with a very long 30-40 year latency between exposure and disease. Once diagnosed, it is incurable, with a survival of only 9-18 months.

Clinical pearlMesothelioma's defining features are its extraordinarily long latency after asbestos exposure and its uniformly poor prognosis — contrast this with asbestos-related lung cancer, whose risk is 'only' 4-6x higher rather than near-universally fatal on diagnosis.

Practice questions

Exam-style question
A 62-year-old presents with progressive dyspnea and digital clubbing. DLCO is markedly reduced, and CXR shows bibasilar reticulonodular opacities with a honeycomb pattern. No extrapulmonary findings are present. What is the most likely diagnosis?
ACryptogenic organizing pneumonia
BAcute interstitial pneumonia
CIdiopathic pulmonary fibrosis
DSarcoidosis
EHypersensitivity pneumonitis
Explanation: Bibasilar honeycomb reticulonodular disease, low DLCO, digital clubbing, and the absence of extrapulmonary signs (a diagnosis of exclusion) are classic for IPF — the ILD most associated with clubbing.
Exam-style question
A patient initially treated for community-acquired pneumonia fails three courses of antibiotics. Repeat imaging shows patchy, migratory infiltrates, and the patient has a low-grade fever. Lung biopsy shows Masson bodies. What is the diagnosis and expected response to treatment?
AIdiopathic pulmonary fibrosis; poor response to any therapy
BAcute interstitial pneumonia; 60% mortality regardless of treatment
CDesquamative interstitial pneumonia; requires smoking cessation only
DLymphocytic interstitial pneumonia; treat only if symptomatic
ECryptogenic organizing pneumonia; responds well to a slow steroid taper over 6 months
Explanation: Masson bodies, fever, migratory patchy infiltrates, and failure of antibiotics for presumed pneumonia are classic for cryptogenic organizing pneumonia, which mostly responds well to corticosteroids tapered slowly over about 6 months.
Exam-style question
A 45-year-old heavy smoker develops an ARDS-like picture with rapid respiratory decline. Biopsy shows diffuse alveolar damage. She has no prior lung disease. What is the expected outcome if she survives the acute illness?
AProgressive decline to death within 3 years, similar to IPF
BComplete recovery of lung function
CConversion to chronic beryllium disease
DPermanent restrictive defect requiring long-term oxygen
EDevelopment of Caplan syndrome
Explanation: Acute interstitial pneumonia (Hamman-Rich syndrome) has an extreme, almost bimodal course: about 60% die within the first 6 months, but survivors usually regain lung function completely — smoking is notably NOT a risk factor for AIP itself.
Exam-style question
A dairy farmer develops recurrent episodes of dyspnea and cough after working with moldy hay, with symptoms improving on weekends away from the farm. Which mechanism underlies this presentation?
ANon-immune mediated reaction requiring no prior sensitization
BDirect fibrotic reaction to inhaled crystalline silica
CNeoplastic transformation of alveolar macrophages
DAn antigen-antibody immune complex reaction to thermophilic actinomycetes
EAnti-GBM antibody-mediated injury
Explanation: Farmer's lung is a classic hypersensitivity pneumonitis, an immune-mediated (antigen + antibody complex) reaction to thermophilic actinomycetes in moldy hay — distinct from byssinosis, which requires no immune sensitization.
Exam-style question
A textile mill worker notices chest tightness that is mild early in the work week but becomes pronounced by the end of every Monday, improving over the weekend. No immune sensitization is required for this process. What is the diagnosis?
AByssinosis
BFarmer's lung
CSilicosis
DBird fancier's lung
EBerylliosis
Explanation: Byssinosis, from cotton/flax/hemp dust exposure, is not immune-mediated (no sensitization needed) and classically causes chest tightness that worsens by the end of Monday after a weekend away from exposure.
Exam-style question
A patient on amiodarone for 8 months at a dose of 400mg/day develops progressive dyspnea and restrictive lung findings. Which feature of her regimen is most directly implicated in the risk of amiodarone-induced lung toxicity?
AThe specific brand of amiodarone used
BTotal dose per single administration only
CHigh cumulative dose and duration of therapy over months
DConcurrent use of montelukast
ERecent discontinuation of corticosteroids
Explanation: Amiodarone lung toxicity risk relates to high cumulative dose (doses >400mg/day) and duration of therapy over months — a dose-and-duration-dependent phenomenon.
Exam-style question
A patient with known rheumatoid arthritis develops a pleural effusion. Thoracentesis reveals an exudative effusion with a very low glucose level, initially raising concern for empyema, but cultures are negative. What is the most likely explanation?
AOccult tuberculosis
BGoodpasture syndrome
CChronic beryllium disease
DMesothelioma
EA rheumatoid pleural effusion, which classically has very low glucose
Explanation: Rheumatoid arthritis-associated pleural effusions classically have a very low glucose level, a finding otherwise mostly seen in empyema — a well-known diagnostic mimicker in RA patients with negative cultures.
Exam-style question
A 55-year-old man presents with hemoptysis, sinus congestion, and rapidly progressive renal failure. C-ANCA is positive. Which biopsy site is most appropriate to confirm the diagnosis?
ARenal biopsy
BLung or nasal mucosa biopsy
CBone marrow biopsy
DSkin biopsy of an unrelated area
ELiver biopsy
Explanation: Granulomatosis with polyangiitis (Wegener's) is diagnosed by biopsy of the lung or nasal mucosa (showing granulomatous vasculitis), not kidney biopsy, even though renal involvement is prominent clinically.
Exam-style question
A 50-year-old man with a long-standing history of asthma is being weaned off oral corticosteroids and started on a leukotriene receptor antagonist for maintenance control. He subsequently develops eosinophilia, pulmonary infiltrates, and a necrotizing vasculitis. What is the most appropriate first step in management?
AIncrease the dose of the leukotriene receptor antagonist
BStart high-dose cyclophosphamide immediately without addressing medications
CBegin lifelong azithromycin prophylaxis
DStop the leukotriene receptor antagonist
ERefer directly for lung transplantation
Explanation: This is the classic unmasking of eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (Churg-Strauss) in an asthmatic on a leukotriene receptor antagonist while corticosteroids are withdrawn; the first step is to stop the LTRA (mepolizumab is also used).
Exam-style question
A young man presents with hemoptysis and rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis, but no sinus or upper airway symptoms. Serologic testing is positive for antibodies against the glomerular basement membrane. What is the diagnosis?
AGoodpasture syndrome
BGranulomatosis with polyangiitis (Wegener's)
CEosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (Churg-Strauss)
DIgA vasculitis
EIdiopathic pulmonary hemosiderosis
Explanation: Goodpasture syndrome causes a pulmonary-renal syndrome with anti-GBM antibodies and, critically, no nasal/sinus involvement — distinguishing it from Wegener's, which has upper airway disease and is C-ANCA positive.

A middle-aged woman with a history of asthma presents with dyspnea and cough. Chest x-ray shows peripheral infiltrates sparing the central lung fields, described as the 'photonegative of pulmonary edema.' ESR is elevated and BAL shows eosinophils. What is the diagnosis?
AAcute eosinophilic pneumonia
BAllergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis
CChronic eosinophilic pneumonia
DLoeffler syndrome from parasitic infection
EHypersensitivity pneumonitis
Explanation: Chronic eosinophilic pneumonia classically affects middle-aged women (50% with asthma), with peripheral infiltrates sparing the central lung (the 'photonegative of pulmonary edema'), elevated ESR, and eosinophils on BAL.
Exam-style question
A patient with known bronchiectasis develops recurrent pulmonary infiltrates, brownish sputum, and a markedly elevated IgE level. Chest imaging shows 'tram-track' bronchial markings. Despite the fact that the causative organism is non-invasive in this condition, which treatment is indicated?
ANo treatment — the process is self-limited
BImmediate lung resection
CIV amphotericin B
DCyclophosphamide
ELong-term corticosteroids, with itraconazole added for benefit
Explanation: Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis is fundamentally an allergic (Type I/III) reaction to Aspergillus colonizing pre-existing bronchiectasis, so despite the organism being non-invasive, treatment is long-term corticosteroids, with itraconazole as an adjunct.
Exam-style question
A 28-year-old male smoker presents with a spontaneous pneumothorax. Workup reveals small cystic spaces in the upper lung zones with an INCREASED total lung volume (not restrictive), diabetes insipidus, and lytic bone lesions. ALymphangioleiomyomatosis
BPulmonary Langerhans cell histiocytosis
CIdiopathic pulmonary fibrosis
DPulmonary alveolar proteinosis
EAsbestosis
Explanation: Pulmonary Langerhans cell histiocytosis is one of the few non-restrictive ILDs (increased lung volume), occurs in young smoking males, presents with pneumothorax and posterior pituitary involvement (diabetes insipidus, lytic bone lesions), and shows Birbeck granules ('tennis rackets') on EM.
Exam-style question
A patient with a chronic productive cough undergoes CXR showing a 'crazy-paving' pattern. Lung biopsy reveals alveoli filled with a lipid-protein material (surfactant/phosphatidylcholine) due to defective alveolar macrophages. What is the most effective treatment?
ALong-term oral corticosteroids
BCyclophosphamide
CLung transplantation as first-line therapy
DWhole lung lavage
EAvoidance of beryllium exposure
Explanation: Pulmonary alveolar proteinosis results from defective alveolar macrophages allowing a surfactant (phosphatidylcholine) buildup that interferes with gas exchange; the standard treatment is whole lung lavage.

Explanation: Lymphangioleiomyomatosis (proliferation of smooth muscle throughout the lung) affects premenopausal women with spontaneous pneumothorax and chylous effusions; lung transplantation is the only definitive option, though LAM can recur in the transplanted lung.
Exam-style question
A denim-manufacturing worker involved in sandblasting for 20+ years presents with dyspnea and a productive cough. CXR shows a symmetric nodular pattern in the upper lobes with hilar lymph node calcification described as 'eggshell.' What is the most likely diagnosis and an associated infectious risk?
ACoal worker's pneumoconiosis; increased tuberculosis risk regardless of co-exposure
BAsbestosis; increased risk of mesothelioma only
CSilicosis; increased tuberculosis risk
DBerylliosis; no increased infectious risk
EByssinosis; increased risk of bronchitis only
Explanation: Chronic silicosis (≥20 years after exposure, e.g., sandblasting in denim manufacturing) shows upper-lobe nodules with eggshell hilar calcification and carries an increased risk of tuberculosis — unlike coal worker's pneumoconiosis, where TB risk is not increased unless silicosis co-exists.
Exam-style question
An underground coal miner with longstanding rheumatoid arthritis develops several large cavitary pulmonary nodules on chest imaging. What is this combination called, and is his tuberculosis risk increased from coal dust exposure alone?
ALofgren's syndrome; yes, TB risk is increased by coal dust alone
BCREST syndrome; yes, TB risk is markedly increased
CFelty syndrome; no change in TB risk
DSjogren's syndrome; TB risk is decreased
ECaplan syndrome; no, TB is not increased by coal worker's pneumoconiosis alone unless silicosis also present
Explanation: Caplan syndrome is rheumatoid arthritis with large cavitary pulmonary nodules, associated with both silicosis and coal worker's pneumoconiosis; coal worker's pneumoconiosis alone does not increase TB risk unless silicosis is also present.
Exam-style question
A shipyard worker with a 25-year latency since asbestos exposure presents with dyspnea, dry cough, bilateral inspiratory basal crackles, and clubbing. Imaging shows pleural plaques and a rounded area of atelectasis with a 'comet tail' sign. Sputum shows ferruginous bodies. Which malignancy carries the longest latency period in this condition?
ABronchogenic (lung) carcinoma, with a 4-6x increased risk
BMesothelioma, with a 30-40 year latency and incurable course
CRenal cell carcinoma
DHepatocellular carcinoma
ECutaneous squamous cell carcinoma
Explanation: While asbestos exposure increases lung cancer risk 4-6x, mesothelioma has an even longer latency (30-40 years) and, once diagnosed, is incurable with only 9-18 month survival.
Exam-style question
A welder with 22 years of occupational exposure develops a granulomatous restrictive lung disease with hilar and mediastinal lymphadenopathy that closely resembles sarcoidosis. Which test specifically distinguishes this occupational disease from sarcoidosis?
ASerum ACE level
BBronchoalveolar lavage CD4/CD8 ratio
CC-ANCA

EAnti-GBM antibody
Explanation: Chronic beryllium disease is a clinical and radiographic mimicker of sarcoidosis; the beryllium lymphocyte proliferation test (BeLPT) on blood or BAL fluid, combined with a documented beryllium exposure history, is what distinguishes it.
