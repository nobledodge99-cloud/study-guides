    1. What Is Infective Endocarditis?

    Endocarditis is inflammation of the endocardium — clinically, this almost always means infection of a heart valve, usually bacterial. Infective endocarditis (IE) begins with injury to the endocardium (most often the valvular surface), most commonly from turbulent blood flow (a congenital or acquired valvular defect), an indwelling venous catheter, cardiac hardware (pacemakers, ICDs), or injected foreign material in people who inject drugs.

    Acute vs. Subacute IE

    Acute IESubacute IE
    OnsetDays to weeks — rapid, usually not subtleWeeks to months — insidious
    Typical organismStaphylococcus aureusViridans streptococci
    Valve substrateCan colonize normal or damaged native valvesUsually damaged or structurally abnormal valves (e.g., bifid aortic valve)
    CourseLarge vegetations, rapid valve destruction; can cause acute heart failureSmaller vegetations, less destructive; most patients recover with treatment
    High yield Untreated IE is uniformly fatal. Even with appropriate treatment, IE still carries significant morbidity and mortality — acute IE (typically S. aureus) has a mortality rate around 50% despite treatment.

    Classification by Valve

    • Native valve endocarditis — involves the patient's own valves (subdivided into acute vs. subacute by tempo, as above)
    • Prosthetic valve endocarditis — infection of a surgically implanted valve

    Check Your Understanding

    1. Which organism is classically associated with acute infective endocarditis?

    Correct: S. aureus. Its high virulence explains both the acute onset and the ability to colonize previously normal valves, producing large, rapidly destructive vegetations.

    2. What is the natural course of untreated infective endocarditis?

    Correct: uniformly fatal if untreated. Even with modern antibiotic and surgical therapy, IE still carries substantial morbidity and mortality — this is why prompt diagnosis and treatment are emphasized so heavily.

    3. A patient develops IE on a native, previously normal aortic valve, with rapid symptom onset over a few days. Which organism is most likely?

    Correct: S. aureus. Unlike the viridans streptococci (which typically need a previously damaged valve and cause subacute disease), S. aureus is virulent enough to infect even a structurally normal valve and does so acutely.

    2. Pathophysiology

    IE develops through a predictable sequence:

    1. Endothelial injury at a site of turbulent blood flow
    2. Aggregation of platelets, fibrin, and other coagulation molecules at the injury site, forming a sterile vegetation (a nonbacterial thrombotic lesion)
    3. Transient bacteremia seeds the sterile vegetation with organisms (from a dental procedure, IV drug injection, GI/GU procedure, etc.)
    4. Microbial proliferation and invasion of the endocardial surface
    5. Metastatic spread — infection/emboli disseminate to areas of high blood flow such as the kidneys, spleen, and brain
    Key concept Notice that a sterile platelet-fibrin vegetation forms before infection — this is the same substrate seen in nonbacterial thrombotic endocarditis (NBTE, Section 14). The difference between IE and NBTE is simply whether that sterile vegetation goes on to be seeded by bacteria during a bacteremic episode.

    Check Your Understanding

    1. Put the steps of IE pathogenesis in order: (i) transient bacteremia, (ii) endothelial injury, (iii) microbial proliferation/invasion, (iv) sterile platelet-fibrin vegetation formation.

    Correct order: endothelial injury → sterile vegetation → transient bacteremia (seeding) → microbial proliferation/invasion. Metastatic spread to high-flow organs (brain, spleen, kidney) follows last.

    2. What is the substrate that bacteria colonize to cause infective endocarditis?

    Correct. Endothelial injury from turbulent flow first produces a sterile thrombus (platelets + fibrin); a subsequent bacteremic episode seeds this thrombus with organisms, which then proliferate and invade.

    3. Risk Factors

    Underlying Valvular Disease

    • Rheumatic heart disease
    • Bicuspid aortic valve
    • Calcific or sclerotic aortic valve
    • Hypertrophic subaortic stenosis
    • Mitral valve prolapse
    High yield — shifting epidemiology Mitral valve prolapse has replaced rheumatic heart disease as the leading cause of valvular-disease-related endocarditis in the antibiotic era, since rheumatic heart disease itself has become far less common with widespread antibiotic treatment of streptococcal pharyngitis.

    Congenital Disorders

    • Ventricular septal defect
    • Tetralogy of Fallot
    • Coarctation of the aorta
    • Patent ductus arteriosus

    Sources of Transient Bacteremia

    • Dental procedures (including routine cleaning/brushing)
    • Upper respiratory procedures
    • Urologic procedures
    • Lower gastrointestinal procedures
    • Intravascular devices
    • IV drug use

    Check Your Understanding

    1. In the current antibiotic era, which condition is now the most common valvular substrate for endocarditis, having overtaken rheumatic heart disease?

    Correct: mitral valve prolapse. Rheumatic heart disease has declined substantially since streptococcal pharyngitis is now routinely treated with antibiotics, removing the antecedent rheumatic fever that used to damage valves.

    2. Which of the following is a congenital (rather than acquired/valvular) risk factor for endocarditis?

    Correct: tetralogy of Fallot. The other three are acquired/degenerative valvular conditions; congenital risk factors also include VSD, coarctation of the aorta, and PDA.

    4. Etiology by Clinical Setting

    Native Valve Endocarditis

    • S. aureus, viridans streptococci, enterococci
    • HACEK organisms (Haemophilus species, Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, Cardiobacterium hominis, Eikenella corrodens, Kingella kingae) — normal mouth flora, fastidious and slow-growing
    • A small percentage: gram-negative organisms and fungi

    Injection Drug Use

    • S. aureus causes ~60% of cases in this population, and is responsible for 80–90% of tricuspid valve IE specifically
    • Pseudomonas aeruginosa is also notable in this group (contaminates water/injections)

    Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis

    TimingTypical organisms
    Early (first ~2 months post-op)Coagulase-positive and coagulase-negative staphylococci, gram-negative organisms, fungi
    Late (>2 months post-op)Streptococci, coagulase-positive and coagulase-negative staphylococci

    Culture-Negative Endocarditis

    Organisms that are difficult to grow on standard blood culture media:

    • Legionella
    • Bartonella (including Bartonella quintana)
    • Abiotrophia
    • Tropheryma whipplei
    • Coxiella burnetii
    • Chlamydia psittaci (psittacosis)
    • Brucella
    • Anaerobes
    • HACEK organisms
    Special-population associations to memorize
    • S. gallolyticus (formerly S. bovis) endocarditis → think occult colon cancer
    • HACEK organisms → culture-negative IE, often after dental procedures (normal oropharyngeal flora)
    • Pseudomonas → think IV drug use (contaminated water/injections)
    • Fungi (Candida, Aspergillus) → immunocompromised, IV drug use, or post-surgical patients

    Check Your Understanding

    1. In a patient who injects drugs, which organism is responsible for the majority of tricuspid valve endocarditis?

    Correct: S. aureus, accounting for ~60% of IE cases in people who inject drugs and 80–90% of tricuspid valve involvement specifically — direct skin-to-vein inoculation delivers it straight to the first valve encountered.

    2. A patient's blood cultures grow Streptococcus gallolyticus. What workup should this prompt?

    Correct: colonoscopy. S. gallolyticus (formerly S. bovis) is gut flora that reaches the bloodstream through colonic mucosal disruption — most classically an occult colorectal malignancy.

    3. Which organisms are most likely in early (within 2 months of surgery) prosthetic valve endocarditis?

    Correct. Early prosthetic valve infections (first ~2 months) reflect perioperative/nosocomial contamination — staphylococci (both coagulase types), gram-negative rods, and fungi. Late infections (>2 months) shift toward streptococci and staphylococci from routine bacteremic seeding.

    5. Clinical Presentation — Acute vs. Subacute

    Acute (days)Subacute (weeks to months)
    OnsetSudden high fever, life-threateningInsidious
    CardiovascularTachycardia, rapid heart valve damageMaybe some tachycardia
    ConstitutionalFatigueFatigue, mild fever, weight loss, night sweats
    Hematologic—Anemia
    Organ involvementLungs (right-sided IE); other organs (left-sided IE)Most commonly viridans streptococci, on previously damaged valves

    Across both patterns, fever is the most common symptom overall (present in ~90% of patients) but is nonspecific. A new heart murmur is the most common physical exam finding after fever (~85% of patients). Because IE often lacks localizing symptoms (no cough, dysuria, or rash to point to a source), it's a classic cause of fever of unknown origin.

    Check Your Understanding

    1. What is the single most common symptom of infective endocarditis overall?

    Correct: fever (~90% of patients), though it's nonspecific. A new murmur is the most common exam finding (~85%), but fever is the more common overall symptom.

    2. A patient with subacute IE from viridans streptococci most likely has which valve substrate?

    Correct. Viridans streptococci are considerably less virulent than S. aureus and generally require a preexisting valvular abnormality to establish infection, producing the more insidious subacute course.

    3. Why is IE a classic cause of "fever of unknown origin"?

    Correct. Unlike most febrile illnesses, IE frequently lacks a localizing clue (no dysuria, cough, or rash), so clinicians must specifically consider and test for it during a fever-of-unknown-origin workup.

    6. Physical Exam Findings

    Classic findings occur in roughly 25% of patients and take time to develop — they're less commonly seen now that treatment begins earlier.

    FindingDescriptionLocationMechanism
    PetechiaeSmall red spots from capillary bleeding (small emboli)Palate, conjunctiva, beneath fingernailsMicroembolic
    Splinter (subungual) hemorrhagesLinear hemorrhagesUnder the nailsMicroembolic
    Osler nodesPainful, violaceous, raised lesionsFingers, toes, or feetTender — immune complex deposition and/or microembolic phenomena
    Janeway lesionsPainless, erythematous, flat lesionsPalms and solesNontender — microembolic hemorrhagic lesions
    Roth spotsExudative retinal hemorrhages with a white centerRetinaImmune complex/microembolic
    Mnemonic — "Ow for Owsler nodes" Osler nodes and Janeway lesions look similar, but Osler nodes are painful ("Ow") and Janeway lesions are painless — use the pain to tell them apart. Osler nodes relate to immune complexes/microembolic phenomena, while Janeway lesions are purely microembolic hemorrhages.

    Check Your Understanding

    1. A patient with IE has painful, violaceous, raised lesions on the finger pads. What are these called?

    Correct: Osler nodes. Remember "Ow for Owsler" — they're painful/tender, unlike the painless Janeway lesions.

    2. Painless, erythematous macules on the palms and soles of an IE patient are:

    Correct: Janeway lesions. They're nontender microembolic hemorrhages, in contrast to the tender Osler nodes.

    3. A retinal exam shows hemorrhages with pale/white centers. This finding is called:

    Correct: Roth spots. These are exudative retinal hemorrhages with a characteristic white/pale center, seen in IE among other conditions.

    7. Embolic & Immune-Mediated Findings

    Beyond the classic skin/eye findings, IE can produce:

    • Strokes and other major embolic events — occur in roughly 25% of patients, most often during the first week of antibiotic treatment
    • Hematuria and proteinuria — from renal emboli or immune-complex-mediated glomerulonephritis
    • Septic emboli to brain, spleen, kidney, and lung (especially with right-sided IE)
    Timing pearl Major embolic events cluster in the first week after starting antibiotics — don't be reassured that a patient is "improving" just because they've started treatment; embolic risk is still high early on.

    Check Your Understanding

    1. Major embolic events in IE most commonly occur during which time period?

    Correct: the first week of antibiotic treatment. This is a key timing pearl — early treatment doesn't immediately eliminate embolic risk.

    2. Hematuria and proteinuria in a patient with IE most likely reflect:

    Correct. The kidney is a classic target organ for both septic/thrombotic emboli and immune-complex deposition in IE, producing hematuria/proteinuria and glomerulonephritis findings like red cell casts on urine microscopy.

    8. Diagnostic Studies

    Blood Cultures

    Draw three sets of blood cultures before starting antibiotics. About 5% of cases are culture-negative, most often because the patient already received antibiotics — if cultures are negative but suspicion remains high, consider holding antibiotics 2–3 days (if the patient's clinical status allows) and rechecking cultures. Most bacteria grow within 48 hours; HACEK organisms may take up to 5 days.

    Echocardiography

    ModalitySensitivityNotes
    Transthoracic echo (TTE)55–65% (ScholarRx: ~75%)First-line, noninvasive
    Transesophageal echo (TEE)~90%Best for valve ring abscesses and prosthetic valve disease; obtain if TTE is negative but suspicion remains high

    Check Your Understanding

    1. What is the correct sequence when infective endocarditis is suspected?

    Correct. Blood cultures must be drawn before antibiotics are started; giving antibiotics first is a major cause of culture-negative results and can obscure the diagnosis.

    2. Which echocardiographic modality is preferred for detecting a valve ring abscess or evaluating a prosthetic valve?

    Correct: TEE, with ~90% sensitivity versus TTE's 55–65% — TEE is specifically preferred for abscesses and prosthetic valve disease.

    3. Approximately what percentage of IE cases are culture-negative, and what is the most common reason?

    Correct: ~5%, most commonly because the patient already received antibiotics before cultures were drawn. A smaller subset reflects genuinely fastidious/culture-negative organisms (HACEK, Coxiella, Bartonella, etc.).

    9. Modified Duke Criteria

    The modified Duke criteria diagnose IE with roughly 80% accuracy.

    Major Criteria

    • Two positive blood cultures (typical organisms)
    • Endocardial involvement on echocardiogram
    • Development of a new regurgitant murmur

    Minor Criteria

    • Presence of a predisposing condition
    • Fever ≥38°C
    • Vascular phenomena — cutaneous hemorrhage, aneurysm, systemic emboli, pulmonary infarction
    • Immunologic phenomena — glomerulonephritis, Osler nodes, Roth spots, rheumatoid factor
    • One positive blood culture (not meeting major criteria)

    Interpreting the Criteria

    CategoryRequirement
    Definite diagnosis2 major, OR 1 major + 3 minor, OR 5 minor
    Possible diagnosis1 major + 1 minor, OR 3 minor
    RejectedFirm alternative diagnosis; symptom resolution ≤4 days with antibiotics; no evidence at surgery or autopsy

    Check Your Understanding

    1. A patient has two positive blood cultures and a new regurgitant murmur, with no other findings. What is the diagnostic category?

    Correct: definite IE. Two positive blood cultures and a new regurgitant murmur are each independent major criteria — two major criteria alone meet the threshold for a definite diagnosis.

    2. Which of the following is classified as a MAJOR Duke criterion, not a minor one?

    Correct: endocardial involvement on echo. The other three are minor criteria (fever, immunologic phenomena). The three major criteria are: 2 positive blood cultures, endocardial involvement on echo, and a new regurgitant murmur.

    3. A patient has 1 major and 1 minor Duke criterion. This is classified as:

    Correct: possible IE. "Possible" requires 1 major + 1 minor, or 3 minor criteria — short of the "definite" thresholds (2 major; 1 major + 3 minor; or 5 minor).

    10. Valve Involvement

    All four heart valves can be involved, but with different frequency depending on the pressure environment and clinical context:

    • General population: the mitral valve is the most common site (mitral regurgitation is the most common valvular lesion overall)
    • People who inject drugs: the tricuspid valve is the most frequently involved, since it's the first valve encountered by organisms injected into venous blood

    Tricuspid regurgitation from right-sided IE can cause a bounding jugular venous pulse during systole, as right ventricular contraction is transmitted back into the right atrium and jugular veins.

    Why left-sided valves are more often involved overall Higher intrachamber pressure on the left side creates high-pressure jet streams with adjacent low-flow eddies — ideal conditions for clot formation and subsequent bacterial colonization, and high-flow valves are also more prone to repeated mechanical damage.

    Check Your Understanding

    1. In the general population, which valve is most commonly affected by IE?

    Correct: mitral valve — mitral regurgitation is the most common valvular lesion in the general IE population, reflecting the higher pressures and turbulence on the left side of the heart.

    2. In a person who injects drugs, why is the tricuspid valve most often involved?

    Correct. Injected bacteria travel through the venous system and reach the right atrium/right ventricle first — the tricuspid valve — before any other valve, explaining the predominance of right-sided (tricuspid) IE in this population.

    11. Complications

    • Valve destruction and abscess formation
    • Conduction disturbances — especially with aortic valve IE, given its proximity to the AV node
    • Aneurysm formation (e.g., mycotic aneurysm from septic emboli, seen on cerebral angiography)
    • Embolization — brain (stroke), myocardium (MI), spleen, kidney
    • Septic pulmonary emboli → pulmonary infarction, lung abscess (especially right-sided IE)
    • Musculoskeletal: septic arthritis, osteomyelitis (deep bone pain; back pain if vertebral)
    • Heart failure — the most common cause of death from IE, from acute valvular insufficiency due to vegetations/abscesses

    Conduction Abnormalities (Aortic Valve IE)

    Because the AV node sits adjacent to the aortic valve, aortic IE (especially with annular/perivalvular abscess) can produce a spectrum of AV block:

    EKG FindingDescription
    First-degree AV blockPR interval >0.2 seconds, fixed
    Second-degree AV block, type I (Wenckebach/Mobitz I)Progressively lengthening PR interval, then a dropped P wave
    Third-degree (complete) AV blockComplete AV dissociation — atria and ventricles beat independently

    Check Your Understanding

    1. Why is aortic valve endocarditis more likely than mitral IE to cause AV conduction abnormalities?

    Correct. Perivalvular extension of infection (e.g., annular abscess) from the aortic valve can directly involve the nearby AV node and conduction system, producing progressive degrees of AV block.

    2. An EKG shows a progressively lengthening PR interval followed by a dropped QRS complex. This is:

    Correct: Wenckebach (Mobitz I). The progressive PR prolongation followed by a dropped beat is its hallmark, distinct from the fixed prolonged PR of first-degree block and the complete dissociation of third-degree block.

    3. What is the most common cause of death from infective endocarditis?

    Correct: heart failure, arising from acute valvular insufficiency as vegetations and/or abscesses destroy or distort the valve.

    12. Prophylaxis

    Antibiotic prophylaxis before procedures likely to cause transient bacteremia (dental procedures in particular) is reserved for high-risk groups:

    • History of previous endocarditis
    • Prosthetic cardiac valves
    • Certain congenital heart diseases:
      • Unrepaired cyanotic congenital heart disease
      • Repaired within the last 6 months of intervention
      • Repaired with residual defects
    • Cardiac transplant patients who develop valvulopathy

    Check Your Understanding

    1. Which of the following patients should receive antibiotic prophylaxis before a dental procedure?

    Correct: prosthetic valve. Prophylaxis is reserved for high-risk groups — prior IE, prosthetic valves, certain unrepaired/recently repaired/residual-defect congenital heart disease, and cardiac transplant recipients with valvulopathy — not for routine valve findings like uncomplicated MVP.

    2. A patient had a congenital heart defect surgically repaired 3 months ago with no residual defect. Regarding endocarditis prophylaxis before dental work, this patient:

    Correct. Repaired congenital heart disease still qualifies for prophylaxis within the first 6 months after intervention (the prosthetic material hasn't yet been endothelialized) — after that window, prophylaxis is only needed if a residual defect remains.

    13. Treatment

    Empiric Antibiotic Therapy

    Empiric coverage targets the most likely organisms: staphylococci, streptococci, and enterococci.

    Common empiric regimen Vancomycin 1 g IV every 12 hours with ceftriaxone 2 g IV daily, for a total duration of 4–6 weeks — always tailor to the organism once blood cultures result.

    Surgical Indications

    • Acute heart failure unresponsive to medical therapy
    • Left-sided IE caused by S. aureus, fungi, or highly resistant organisms
    • Heart block, annular or aortic abscess
    • Persistent bacteremia lasting >5–7 days despite appropriate antibiotics
    • Infected prosthetic valve
    • Large valvular vegetation

    Anticoagulation

    • Contraindicated in native valve endocarditis — increases the risk of intracerebral hemorrhage (from hemorrhagic transformation of septic emboli)
    • Prosthetic valve endocarditis: anticoagulation should be stopped during the septic phase, and stopped for two weeks following a CNS embolic event

    Additional Considerations

    • Organisms from oral flora (HACEK, viridans strep) → the patient should get a dental exam
    • S. gallolyticus → the patient should get a colonoscopy
    • Infected prosthetic cardiac devices (pacemakers, ICDs) may need to be replaced

    Check Your Understanding

    1. A patient has native valve endocarditis and a recent embolic stroke. Should therapeutic anticoagulation be started?

    Correct: no. Native valve IE with embolic stroke is a classic scenario where anticoagulation should be avoided or held — the risk of hemorrhagic transformation of a septic embolic infarct is too high.

    2. Which of the following is an indication for surgical intervention in IE?

    Correct. Persistent bacteremia beyond 5–7 days despite proper antibiotic therapy suggests a source (abscess, undrained focus) that antibiotics alone won't clear, and is a standard surgical indication — along with refractory heart failure, heart block/abscess, resistant organisms, infected prosthetic valves, and large vegetations.

    3. What is a typical empiric antibiotic regimen and duration for suspected IE?

    Correct: vancomycin + ceftriaxone, 4–6 weeks, covering the most likely empiric organisms (staphylococci, streptococci, enterococci) — narrowed once culture and sensitivity results are available.

    14. Nonbacterial Thrombotic Endocarditis (NBTE)

    NBTE is endocarditis without infection — sterile vegetations form on heart valves, most often in patients with an underlying hypercoagulable state: malignancy, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), or antiphospholipid syndrome (APS).

    Pathophysiology by Cause

    • Malignancy — activates the coagulation system; cancer cells produce procoagulant substances and cytokines that damage the endothelium
    • SLE — mechanism not fully defined, but vegetations contain immune complexes and cellular debris; a subset of pathologists separate this out as Libman-Sacks endocarditis (not universally agreed upon as a distinct entity; sometimes the term is instead reserved for NBTE with especially large thrombi)
    • APS — autoantibodies attack cell membranes, driving excess clot formation; can be idiopathic or associated with SLE or infections

    Presentation

    Patients are often asymptomatic — NBTE is frequently an incidental autopsy finding, since the sterile lesions rarely destroy the valve (unlike IE). When symptomatic, it's almost always because of thromboembolism (stroke/focal neurologic deficits, or dyspnea from pulmonary emboli), not from direct valve damage.

    Gross Pathology

    Vegetations range from small, rounded growths to large, warty verrucous vegetations (verruca = wart). They can occur on any valve, but SLE-associated vegetations favor the mitral and aortic valves, and can form on both sides of the valve leaflet.

    Complications

    Vegetations are loosely adherent and prone to embolization — most commonly to the brain (stroke), but also heart, kidney, and spleen. Rarely, inflammatory valvulitis impairs valve function enough to cause regurgitation.

    Diagnosis

    Echocardiography is the best test to detect vegetations but cannot distinguish infectious from sterile vegetations — blood cultures must be drawn whenever a vegetation is seen. Three sets of blood cultures should be done to exclude IE; if SLE/APS is suspected, obtain ANA, anti-Smith, rheumatoid factor, anti-dsDNA, and an antiphospholipid antibody panel.

    Treatment

    Treat the underlying cause (malignancy, SLE, APS) when possible. Unless contraindicated, patients should be anticoagulated — heparin (unfractionated or LMWH), then transitioned to warfarin — to prevent emboli.

    Check Your Understanding

    1. What is the underlying condition present in most cases of NBTE?

    Correct: a hypercoagulable state — classically malignancy, SLE, or antiphospholipid syndrome. NBTE vegetations are, by definition, sterile (no infectious organisms).

    2. Why is NBTE most often diagnosed incidentally, sometimes only at autopsy?

    Correct. Unlike infective endocarditis, NBTE's sterile vegetations rarely destroy the valve, so there's often no murmur or dysfunction to prompt a workup — when symptoms occur, they're usually from an embolic event rather than valve damage itself.

    3. Why must blood cultures always be drawn when a vegetation is found on echocardiogram, even if NBTE is suspected?

    Correct. A vegetation looks the same on echo whether it's infected or sterile — cultures are the only way to rule infective endocarditis in or out, and not all IE patients have fever to otherwise raise suspicion.

    15. IE vs. NBTE Comparison

    Infective EndocarditisNonbacterial Thrombotic Endocarditis
    Vegetation contentPlatelets, fibrin, and bacteriaPlatelets and fibrin only — sterile
    Underlying substrateEndothelial injury + bacteremiaEndothelial injury/hypercoagulable state, without infection
    Typical triggerTurbulent flow + transient bacteremia (dental work, IVDU, GU/GI procedures)Malignancy, SLE, antiphospholipid syndrome
    Valve destructionCommon — can be rapid and severe (especially S. aureus)Rare — lesions are usually nondestructive
    Typical presentationFever, new murmur, systemic illnessOften asymptomatic; found incidentally or after an embolic event
    DiagnosisBlood cultures + echo + modified Duke criteriaEcho + negative blood cultures + autoimmune/malignancy workup
    TreatmentProlonged culture-directed antibiotics ± surgeryTreat underlying cause + anticoagulation

    Check Your Understanding

    1. What is the key difference in vegetation composition between IE and NBTE?

    Correct. Both start from the same platelet-fibrin thrombus, but IE vegetations go on to be colonized by bacteria during a bacteremic episode, while NBTE vegetations remain sterile.

    2. Which condition is more likely to remain asymptomatic until an embolic event occurs — IE or NBTE?

    Correct: NBTE. Its sterile, nondestructive vegetations rarely produce valve dysfunction, so patients often remain asymptomatic until a piece embolizes. IE, by contrast, usually declares itself with fever and systemic illness well before any embolic event.

    16. Case Connections

    Case 1 — Infective Endocarditis JL, a 31-year-old male who injects drugs, presents with fever, chills, and anorexia for a week. Exam shows scarring/discoloration along several veins, temperature 38.4°C, splinter hemorrhages on his fingernails, and a systolic murmur at the left lower sternal border that intensifies with deep inspiration.

    Interpretation: A murmur that increases with inspiration is classic for a right-sided (tricuspid) murmur — inspiration increases venous return to the right heart, augmenting the murmur. Combined with IV drug use and splinter hemorrhages, this points strongly to tricuspid valve IE, most likely from S. aureus. Management: admit, draw blood cultures before starting empiric antibiotics, and arrange follow-up support (social work, outpatient drug counseling) given the underlying substance use driving his risk.

    Case 2 — Nonbacterial Thrombotic Endocarditis MB, a 46-year-old with SLE, presents with 2 days of left-flank pain followed by hematuria. Vitals are normal; exam reveals a new holosystolic murmur at the apex radiating to the axilla (a mitral regurgitation pattern).

    Interpretation: A new murmur in a patient with SLE raises concern for NBTE (sometimes termed Libman-Sacks endocarditis in this context). A sterile vegetation has likely formed on her mitral valve, and a fragment has embolized to her kidney, explaining the flank pain and hematuria. An echocardiogram (plus blood cultures to exclude IE) confirms the diagnosis.
