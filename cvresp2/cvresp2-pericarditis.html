1. Anatomy & Innervation

The pericardium is a double-walled sac enclosing the heart. Understanding its layers explains both the pain pattern and the fluid dynamics of pericarditis.

  • Fibrous pericardium (outer): dense collagen/elastic fibers; anchored superiorly to the adventitia of the great vessels (aorta, PA, SVC) and inferiorly to the central tendon of the diaphragm
  • Serous pericardium (inner): single layer of mesothelial cells forming two layers:
    • Parietal layer — lines the inner surface of the fibrous pericardium
    • Visceral layer (epicardium) — directly adherent to the heart surface (board pearl: visceral pericardium = epicardium, same structure, two names)
  • Normal pericardial thickness: <1 mm on pathology; >2 mm is considered thickened on imaging (note: normal thickness is up to 4 mm on CMR and 2 mm on CT — imaging thresholds differ from pathology)
  • Two distinct peri-cardiac fat compartments: epicardial fat (between myocardium and visceral pericardium) vs. epipericardial fat (external to the parietal pericardium) — don't mistake either for pericardial fluid

Pericardial Cavity & Fluid

  • Potential space between visceral and parietal layers of the serous pericardium
  • Normally contains <50 mL of serous fluid — an ultrafiltrate of plasma
  • Functions: lubrication for frictionless cardiac motion; fixes the heart within the mediastinum; barrier against infection and contiguous tumor spread
  • Lymphatic drainage → anterior/posterior mediastinal, peribronchial, and tracheobronchial lymph nodes

Pericardial Sinuses (Anatomy Board Favorite)

  • Transverse sinus: posterior to the aorta and pulmonary trunk, anterior to the SVC — surgically important for clamping the great vessels during cardiopulmonary bypass
  • Oblique sinus: a blind-ended cul-de-sac posterior to the left atrium, bounded by pericardial reflections around the pulmonary veins

Blood Supply & Innervation — Why the Pain Refers Where It Does

  • Arterial supply: pericardiophrenic artery (branch of internal thoracic artery), with contributions from bronchial and esophageal arteries
  • Venous drainage: pericardiophrenic veins → brachiocephalic veins; also via the azygos system
  • Sensory/somatic: phrenic nerve (C3–C5) supplies the parietal pericardium
  • Parasympathetic: vagus nerve, left recurrent laryngeal nerve, esophageal plexus
  • Sympathetic: stellate ganglion, first dorsal ganglion, cardiac plexuses
Key pearl: the visceral pericardium has NO pain sensitivity. All pericarditis pain arises from the inflamed parietal pericardium and travels via the phrenic nerve (C3–C5) — which is why pericardial pain classically refers to the trapezius ridge (same dermatome). This is a key distinguishing feature from myocardial ischemia, which refers to the left arm, jaw, or neck.
Quick Check — Anatomy

Why does pericarditis pain classically radiate to the trapezius ridge?

The parietal pericardium (the pain-sensitive layer) is innervated by the phrenic nerve, C3–C5 — the same segments that supply the trapezius ridge dermatome, producing referred pain there.

2. Pathophysiology Cascade — Injury to Shock

PERICARDIAL INJURY ↓
INFLAMMATION ↓
↑ VASCULAR PERMEABILITY → FLUID ACCUMULATION → EFFUSION → ↑ INTRAPERICARDIAL PRESSURE → ↓ CARDIAC FILLING → ↓ PRELOAD → ↓ STROKE VOLUME → ↓ CARDIAC OUTPUT → ↓ ARTERIAL BP → COMPENSATION (SNS → RAAS) → COMPENSATION FAILS → SHOCK

(parallel chronic branch:) INFLAMMATION → FIBROSIS → ↓ COMPLIANCE → CONSTRICTION → feeds back into ↓ CARDIAC FILLING

Mechanism of Pericardial Inflammation (the NLRP3 Pathway)

  • An insult (bacterial/viral infection, cardiac injury, immune derangement) injures pericardial mesothelial cells
  • Danger signals — PAMPs (pathogen components) or DAMPs (tissue-damage molecules) — activate Toll-like receptors on innate immune cells
  • This triggers NF-κB activation → transcription of pro-IL-1β and NLRP3 inflammasome components
  • The NLRP3 inflammasome assembles and activates caspase-1, which cleaves pro-IL-1β into active IL-1β
  • IL-1β drives the inflammatory cascade: vasodilation, ↑ vascular permeability, leukocyte recruitment (neutrophils/macrophages), and further cytokine release (IL-6, TNF-α) — in recurrent pericarditis this becomes a self-amplifying autoinflammatory loop
Cross-topic board pearl: this exact pathway — NLRP3 inflammasome → caspase-1 → IL-1β — is the same mechanism as gout and familial Mediterranean fever. Colchicine inhibits tubulin polymerization, preventing inflammasome assembly; anakinra/rilonacept block IL-1 signaling downstream. Whether the question is about gout, FMF, or pericarditis, colchicine's mechanism is the same.

Why Pain Is Positional and Pleuritic

Movement of the inflamed parietal pericardium causes mechanical irritation; inflammatory mediators sensitize nociceptors. This is why the pain is sharp, worsens supine (more pericardial contact/stretch), and improves leaning forward.

Fluid Accumulation — a Cross-System Starling Concept

Endothelial damage → increased vascular permeability → plasma proteins and leukocytes escape the vasculature → increased interstitial/pericardial oncotic pressure → water follows → fluid accumulates. This is governed by the Starling equation and the same four general mechanisms produce edema/effusion in any organ system:

MechanismEffectOther Examples
↑ Capillary hydrostatic pressurePushes fluid into interstitiumHeart failure, venous obstruction
↓ Plasma oncotic pressure (hypoalbuminemia)Reduced fluid retention in vasculatureNephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis, malnutrition
↑ Capillary permeability (endothelial junction disruption)Protein leaks into interstitium, draws fluidInflammation, burns, sepsis
Lymphatic obstructionImpaired clearance of interstitial fluid/proteinMalignancy, lymph node dissection, radiation, filariasis

Pericardial Compliance & the Tamponade Transition

  • A compliant pericardium accommodates added volume with little pressure rise — but as it stretches, tension rises progressively
  • Eventually a critical transition is reached: a small additional volume causes a large pressure increase
  • Tamponade is not a distinct new process — it's simply the point at which accumulated pericardial fluid raises external pressure enough to interfere with cardiac filling

Why Increased Pericardial Pressure Impairs Filling

Diastolic filling normally depends on venous pressure > right atrial pressure. As pericardial pressure rises, it compresses the low-pressure cardiac chambers — the right-sided chambers are most vulnerable because their diastolic pressures are relatively low.

↑ pericardial pressure → RA/RV compression → ↓ venous return → ↓ ventricular end-diastolic volume → ↓ preload → ↓ stroke volume → ↓ cardiac output → hypotension

Mechanism of Pulsus Paradoxus

  • Normal inspiration → ↓ intrathoracic pressure → ↑ systemic venous return to the right heart
  • In tamponade, total cardiac volume is fixed by the tense pericardial fluid — so ↑ RV filling on inspiration causes the interventricular septum to shift toward the LV
  • This decreases LV filling → ↓ LV stroke volume → systolic BP falls during inspiration
  • This is exaggerated ventricular interdependence — the two ventricles compete for a fixed total space
Quick Check — Pathophysiology

Colchicine's mechanism in pericarditis is best described as:

Colchicine disrupts microtubules, which prevents inflammasome component aggregation and blocks neutrophil chemotaxis — the same mechanism that makes it effective in gout and familial Mediterranean fever.

3. Clinical Presentation & Diagnostic Criteria

Acute pericarditis is a clinical diagnosis. The classic teaching (and JAMA 2024 review) requires ≥2 of 4 findings; the 2025 ACC Concise Clinical Guidance reframes chest pain as mandatory plus ≥1 additional finding. Know both framings — "2 of 4" remains the most commonly tested version.

FindingApprox. FrequencyDescription
Chest pain~90%Sharp, pleuritic, worse supine, improves leaning forward; may radiate to the trapezius ridge
ECG changes~25–50%New widespread ST-segment elevation with PR-segment depression
Pericardial effusion~60%New or worsening, most often small, on echocardiography
Pericardial friction rub<30%High-pitched, may be transient, classically triphasic

Supportive Findings

  • Elevated inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP) — CRP elevated in ~78–90% of acute idiopathic/viral cases (defined as >3 mg/L); a normal CRP may reflect early presentation (~34%) or prior NSAID/colchicine/steroid treatment (~50%)
  • Low-grade fever, fatigue, cough, dyspnea
  • Neutrophilic leukocytosis, elevated neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (more likely with fever/fatigue)
  • Cardiac MRI: pericardial late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) reflects neovascularization/inflammation (normal pericardium is minimally vascular, so any contrast retention is abnormal) — must be interpreted with clinical context, since it can be present in ~44% of post-cardiac-surgery patients with no pericarditis symptoms
Recurrent pericarditis caveat: patients with recurrent pericarditis are less likely than those with a first episode to have characteristic ECG changes or a new/worsening effusion — don't require these for the diagnosis in a patient with a known history and typical pain plus elevated CRP.

The Pericardial Friction Rub

  • Mechanism: inflammation → fibrin deposition + roughened surfaces → cardiac movement causes the inflamed surfaces to rub → sound
  • Triphasic = 3 components (atrial systole, ventricular systole, ventricular diastole) — most specific pattern; can also be biphasic or monophasic
  • Described as high-pitched, scratchy, "like walking on fresh snow" or leather creaking
  • Best heard: left sternal border, patient leaning forward, at end-expiration
  • To distinguish from a pleural rub: have the patient hold their breath — a pleural rub disappears, a pericardial rub persists (the heart keeps beating)
  • A triphasic rub is nearly pathognomonic for pericarditis, but disappears with large effusions (the heart floats away from the chest wall) — losing a known rub plus new hypotension should raise concern for tamponade
Quick Check — Diagnosis

Which maneuver helps distinguish a pericardial friction rub from a pleural friction rub?

Since the heart keeps beating even with breath held, a pericardial rub persists; a pleural rub (dependent on lung movement) disappears with breath-holding.

4. Distinguishing Pericarditis from STEMI

Not all patients progress through all four stages — Stage 1 is by far the most tested.

If you remember nothing else: diffuse ST elevation, no reciprocal changes, PR depression = pericarditis. Territorial ST elevation with reciprocal depression = STEMI → urgent cardiac catheterization.

Other important considerations: distinguish pericarditis ECG changes from J-point elevation (early repolarization — also concave up, but with a notched/slurred, well-demarcated J point and no PR depression); compare with prior ECGs if available; pericarditis can trigger atrial fibrillation/flutter in ~4% of patients; and check cardiac troponin to evaluate for concomitant myocardial ischemia or myopericarditis.

Diffuse (not territorial) concave ST elevation with PR depression and no true reciprocal changes (aVR/V1 mirror changes don't count) is classic for pericarditis, not STEMI.

5. Etiologies

Geographic Overview

  • Developed countries (North America, Western Europe): most cases idiopathic/presumed viral (~55% have no identified cause) — "idiopathic" likely represents undiagnosed viral infection or post-viral immune response
  • Developing countries: tuberculosis is the #1 cause — accounts for 65–70% of pericardial effusions in parts of Africa, often with HIV co-infection

Infectious Causes

  • Viral (most common in the developed world): Coxsackievirus B is the classic board answer (also classic for viral myocarditis); also echovirus, adenovirus, EBV, parvovirus B19, CMV, influenza, HIV
  • Bacterial: Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, M. tuberculosis — purulent pericarditis is a surgical emergency (requires drainage, not antibiotics alone)
  • Fungal: Histoplasma, Coccidioides, Aspergillus — mainly immunocompromised patients
  • Parasitic: Echinococcus, Toxoplasma

Non-Infectious Causes

  • Autoimmune/inflammatory: SLE (pericarditis in up to ~20% of SLE patients), RA, scleroderma, sarcoidosis, familial Mediterranean fever, Behçet syndrome, IBD (rheumatoid arthritis and systemic sclerosis more often cause an asymptomatic effusion [30–50%] rather than active pericarditis)
  • Post-cardiac injury syndrome: weeks after MI (4–5%), cardiac surgery (20–30%), or ablation (~10%) — Dressler syndrome is the specific post-MI form
  • Metabolic: uremia (uremic pericarditis — an indication for dialysis), hypothyroidism/myxedema
  • Neoplastic: lung (~40%), breast (~25%), hematologic malignancies (~20%) — usually cause an effusion rather than classic pericarditis
  • Radiation: mediastinal radiation for lymphoma or breast cancer
  • Drug-induced: hydralazine, procainamide, isoniazid (drug-induced lupus), immune checkpoint inhibitors, doxorubicin; clozapine is a psychiatry-relevant cause worth remembering

Three board pearls: Dressler syndrome = autoimmune pericarditis weeks post-MI (don't label post-op or post-ablation pericarditis as "Dressler" — that term is specifically post-MI). Uremic pericarditis = indication for dialysis. Drug-induced lupus (hydralazine, procainamide, isoniazid) can cause pericarditis.

6. The Spectrum: Acute, Incessant, Chronic, Recurrent + Myopericarditis

TermDefinition
Acute pericarditisResolves within 4–6 weeks
Incessant pericarditisSymptoms persist ≥4–6 weeks but <3 months, without remission
Chronic pericarditisSymptoms >3 months
Recurrent pericarditisRelapse of symptoms after clinical remission of ≥4–6 weeks
  • Recurrence occurs in 15–30% after a first episode of idiopathic/viral pericarditis; of these, 25–50% have subsequent flares; up to 50% recur again after a first recurrence
  • Most recurrences occur within 3–6 months; recurrence beyond 12 months is unusual
  • Autoimmune-disease-associated pericarditis carries an ~88% recurrence risk over a median 35-month follow-up, vs. ~69% for idiopathic disease (roughly a 50% increased relative risk with autoimmune etiology)

Risk Factors for Recurrence

  • Lack of NSAID response
  • Early corticosteroid use (a risk factor, not protective, when used in the first episode)
  • Persistently elevated CRP — CRP remaining high after 1 week of therapy roughly doubles recurrence risk (hazard ratio ~2.4)
  • Severe pericardial late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) on CMR — associated with ~40% higher recurrence risk
  • Autoimmune etiology

Complications

  • Cardiac tamponade: <3% in acute idiopathic pericarditis overall, but much higher with bacterial (~33%) or tuberculous (~20%) etiology
  • Constrictive pericarditis: <0.5% after acute idiopathic pericarditis, but ~20% of patients with incessant pericarditis develop constriction

Myopericarditis vs. Perimyocarditis

  • ~15–20% of pericarditis patients have some degree of myocardial involvement
  • Myopericarditis = pericarditis-dominant, with troponin elevation
  • Perimyocarditis = myocarditis-dominant, with pericardial involvement
  • The distinction matters for prognosis and exercise restriction (more prolonged with significant myocardial involvement)
  • Of patients with an elevated troponin, ~80% do not have LV dysfunction at presentation; of those who do, ~85% have LV recovery by 12 months
  • An elevated troponin without LV dysfunction is not a negative prognostic marker — management is the same as for idiopathic/viral pericarditis
Board pearl: troponin elevation in pericarditis suggests myocardial involvement (myopericarditis); distinguish from STEMI by diffuse (not territorial) ECG changes and the absence of regional wall motion abnormalities.

7. Diagnostic Workup & Risk Stratification

Laboratory Assessment

  • CRP/ESR: elevated in ~80–90% (inflammatory phenotype); CRP is used to guide treatment duration — don't stop therapy until it normalizes
  • CBC: neutrophilic leukocytosis common
  • Troponin: elevated in ~15% — indicates myocardial involvement (myopericarditis)
  • BUN/Cr: rule out uremic pericarditis
  • TSH: rule out hypothyroidism
  • ANA/RF: only if other signs/symptoms suggest a systemic autoimmune disease — low-titer ANA is common and nonspecific in recurrent idiopathic pericarditis
  • Blood cultures: if bacterial/purulent pericarditis suspected
  • TB testing: for patients from/living in endemic areas, or other TB risk factors (unhoused, current/past incarceration, immunocompromised including HIV)
  • Pericardiocentesis (with cell count, Gram stain/culture, cytology) is performed for overt/developing tamponade or suspected bacterial, tuberculous, or malignant effusion — cytology has low sensitivity for malignancy (~25% of "benign" cytology may still have an underlying neoplasm); the Light criteria (used for pleural fluid) should not be applied to pericardial fluid

Imaging

  • Echocardiography (first-line): assesses effusion size/location/rate of accumulation, hemodynamic consequences (tamponade, constrictive physiology), and pericardial thickening
  • Chest X-ray: "water bottle" silhouette with effusions >300 mL; also evaluates for TB or malignancy; pericardial calcification (~20–40%, especially TB) suggests constriction
  • Cardiac MRI (CMR): gold standard for characterizing pericardial inflammation — late gadolinium enhancement (neovascularization) and T2-STIR signal (edema); most useful for recurrent/complicated cases
  • CT: best for pericardial calcification; used for procedural/surgical planning (pericardiocentesis approach, pericardial window, pericardiectomy); not indicated for initial assessment
Distinguishing pearl: the round circle of the descending aorta, seen posterior to the left atrium on echo, is a landmark distinguishing pericardial effusion (anterior to the descending aorta) from pleural effusion (posterior to it). Also distinguish anterior epicardial fat pad from true pericardial fluid.

Risk Stratification — Who Needs Admission?

Most patients are low-risk and managed as outpatients. High-risk features warranting hospitalization:

  • High fever (>38°C)
  • Subacute onset (symptoms developing over days without a clear onset)
  • Large pericardial effusion (>2.0 cm) or tamponade physiology
  • Failure to respond to NSAIDs within ~1 week
  • Elevated troponin (myopericarditis)
  • Immunosuppression
  • Oral anticoagulant use (increased hemorrhagic effusion risk)
  • Suspected malignancy
Quick Check — Workup

Which imaging modality is the gold standard for characterizing active pericardial inflammation?

Echo is first-line for effusion/hemodynamics, but CMR is the gold standard for characterizing the degree of pericardial inflammation via LGE and T2 edema imaging.

8. Cardiac Tamponade

Pathophysiology

  • Fluid accumulates in the pericardial space → once pericardial reserve volume is exceeded, intrapericardial pressure rises sharply (exponentially, not linearly)
  • Rate of accumulation matters more than total volume: rapid accumulation of just 150–200 mL can cause tamponade, while slow accumulation (e.g., malignant effusion) can accommodate >1 L without hemodynamic compromise
  • Rising pericardial pressure → equalization of diastolic pressures across all chambers → impaired filling → ↓ stroke volume and cardiac output
  • Compensatory tachycardia initially maintains output before decompensation

Clinical Findings

  • Beck's triad: hypotension, jugular venous distension, muffled/distant heart sounds
  • Pulsus paradoxus: inspiratory drop in systolic BP >10 mmHg — the most specific clinical sign (mechanism: exaggerated ventricular interdependence, see Section 2)
  • Tachycardia is the most sensitive sign
  • JVP: elevated, with a prominent x descent but absent y descent (the heart can't fill in early diastole)
  • ECG: low voltage, electrical alternans (beat-to-beat QRS amplitude alternation from the heart swinging within the effusion)
  • Echo (most specific → least specific): diastolic RV collapse (most specific); RA inversion >1/3 of the cardiac cycle; dilated IVC without respiratory variation; >30% respiratory variation in mitral inflow velocity
Board pearl: Beck's triad + pulsus paradoxus + electrical alternans + diastolic RV collapse = classic tamponade presentation. A normal BP early in the course does not exclude tamponade — use the full hemodynamic pattern plus echo.

Management

  • Pericardiocentesis is definitive — echocardiography-guided preferred
  • Approaches: subxiphoid (most common in emergencies), apical, parasternal
  • IV fluid resuscitation increases preload — a temporizing measure only, not definitive treatment
  • Avoid diuretics and vasodilators — both reduce preload and worsen hemodynamics
  • Surgical pericardial window for recurrent effusions
Case Snapshot

A 55-year-old woman with metastatic breast cancer has progressive dyspnea. BP 82/60, HR 118, JVD, muffled heart sounds, pulsus paradoxus 18 mmHg. ECG shows low voltage and electrical alternans. Echo shows a large effusion with diastolic RV collapse. What is the most appropriate next step?

Answer: Emergent pericardiocentesis. This is tamponade (Beck's triad + pulsus paradoxus + electrical alternans + diastolic RV collapse). Diuretics and vasodilators are contraindicated because they further reduce preload; IV fluids only temporize.

Quick Check — Tamponade

Which of the following is contraindicated in suspected cardiac tamponade?

Both diuretics and vasodilators reduce preload, which worsens hemodynamics in a heart that is already unable to fill adequately.

9. Constrictive Pericarditis

Pathophysiology

A chronic consequence of pericardial inflammation: fibrosis (± calcification) replaces the normal elastic pericardium, forming a rigid, noncompliant shell. This is the same TGF-β → fibroblast → myofibroblast → collagen I/III deposition pathway seen across organs (see cross-organ fibrosis box below). Early rapid ventricular filling is actually preserved, but is then abruptly halted once the rigid pericardium is reached. Three key hemodynamic concepts:

  1. Dissociation of intrathoracic and intracardiac pressure: normally, ↓ intrathoracic pressure on inspiration transmits to the cardiac chambers, maintaining the gradient driving LA→LV flow. The rigid pericardium blocks this transmission, so LV filling is impaired during inspiration → ↓ preload.
  2. Exaggerated ventricular interdependence: inspiration ↑ RV venous return, but the RV can't expand outward (rigid shell) — so it expands inward, shifting the septum toward the LV and further limiting LV filling.
  3. Equalization of diastolic pressures: the rigid pericardium limits expansion of all four chambers, so RV and LV diastolic pressures become similar (resembles tamponade, but via a different mechanism — fixed rigid shell rather than compressive fluid).

Net result: impaired diastolic filling → ↓ stroke volume → ↓ cardiac output, with blood backing up into the venous system → systemic venous congestion.

Fibrosis: A Common Mechanism, Many Organs

OrganMechanismResult
PericardiumTGF-β activates pericardial fibroblasts → collagen depositionConstrictive pericarditis (Kussmaul sign, pericardial knock, right HF)
MyocardiumTGF-β activates cardiac fibroblasts → collagen between cardiomyocytesRestrictive physiology/diastolic HF (↓ e′ on tissue Doppler)
LungTGF-β activates pulmonary fibroblasts → alveolar interstitial collagenRestrictive lung disease (↓ compliance, ↓ TLC, ↓ FVC, preserved/↑ FEV₁/FVC)
LiverTGF-β activates hepatic stellate cells → collagen in space of DisseCirrhosis (portal hypertension, ascites, varices)
KidneyTGF-β activates mesangial/interstitial fibroblasts → ECM/collagen in mesangium & GBMGlomerulosclerosis (proteinuria, ↓ GFR — final common pathway of diabetic nephropathy)

Shared mechanism: tissue injury → macrophage activation → TGF-β activates fibroblasts, which differentiate into myofibroblasts and deposit type I/III collagen, while matrix metalloproteinases (which normally break down collagen) are suppressed. Compliant tissue becomes rigid.

Clinical Findings

  • Presents as right-sided heart failure: elevated JVP, hepatomegaly, ascites (often disproportionate to peripheral edema), peripheral edema
  • Kussmaul sign: paradoxical rise (or failure to decrease) in JVP with inspiration — relatively specific for constriction. Mechanism: the rigid pericardium prevents the RV from accommodating increased venous return on inspiration
  • Pericardial knock: early diastolic sound (occurs in up to 47% of patients) at the abrupt cessation of ventricular filling when the expanding ventricle hits the rigid pericardium — often confused with S3, but occurs slightly earlier and is higher-pitched
  • JVP waveform: prominent x and rapid y descents (rapid y descent reflects the preserved early rapid filling phase, abruptly halted)
  • CXR: pericardial calcification in 20–40% (especially TB etiology)

Constrictive Pericarditis vs. Restrictive Cardiomyopathy (Classic Board Comparison)

FeatureConstrictive PericarditisRestrictive Cardiomyopathy
Extra heart soundPericardial knockS3 or S4
Myocardial relaxation (tissue Doppler e′)Normal/elevated (myocardium itself is normal)Reduced (myocardium is diseased)
Pericardial calcificationFavors constrictionAbsent
Remember: in tamponade, systemic venous return increases with inspiration (enlarging the right heart and encroaching on the left); in constrictive pericarditis, systemic venous return does not increase normally with inspiration — the mechanism of reduced LV / increased RV volume in constriction is impaired LV filling from a lesser pulmonary-venous-to-LV pressure gradient, not a fluid-mediated compressive process.
Quick Check — Constrictive Pericarditis

A patient has elevated JVP that paradoxically rises with inspiration, an early diastolic sound, and pericardial calcification on CT. Tissue Doppler shows a normal e′. This is most consistent with:

Kussmaul sign, pericardial knock, calcification, and a normal e′ (myocardium itself is fine) all point to constrictive pericarditis rather than restrictive cardiomyopathy (which would show a reduced e′) or tamponade (fluid-mediated, not a rigid shell).

10. Treatment

First-Line: NSAIDs + Colchicine (Dual Anti-Inflammatory Therapy)

  • NSAID: ibuprofen 600–800 mg TID (or per JAMA review, 1600–2400 mg/d) or aspirin 650–1000 mg TID — aspirin preferred if concomitant CAD; taper after chest pain resolves and CRP normalizes (typically 1–2 weeks for a first episode; weeks to months for recurrence); add a PPI for gastric protection
  • Colchicine: 0.5 mg BID (>70 kg) or 0.5 mg once daily (<70 kg); mechanism = inhibits tubulin polymerization → prevents NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and neutrophil chemotaxis
    • 3 months for a first (acute) episode
    • ≥6 months for a first recurrence
    • GI adverse effects (diarrhea ~23%, nausea, vomiting) necessitate dose reduction/cessation in ~10% of patients
  • Exercise restriction: avoidance of vigorous exercise, often for up to 3 months (recurrences linked to exercise have been reported, though rigorous data are limited); one lecture source cites ≥1 month with max HR <100 bpm until remission

After cardiac surgery, a 1-month course of colchicine (starting 48–72 hr pre-op) can be considered to prevent post-cardiac injury syndrome, though GI intolerance and drug interactions may limit use.

Corticosteroids

  • No randomized trial data exist, but observational data show higher-dose corticosteroids (e.g., prednisone 1.0 mg/kg/day) are associated with increased recurrence risk
  • Reserve for patients who don't improve with NSAIDs + colchicine, or who have contraindications/adverse outcomes with those agents
  • Dose: low-to-moderate — prednisone 0.25–0.5 mg/kg/day
  • Additional indications: underlying systemic autoimmune disease, post-cardiac-surgery symptoms unresponsive to colchicine, or contraindications to high-dose NSAIDs (moderate-severe kidney failure, anticoagulation, pregnancy after 20 weeks — low-dose prednisone + colchicine can be used throughout pregnancy)
  • Taper slowly: e.g., starting dose 15–25 mg → decrease 2.5 mg/day every 2–4 weeks; starting dose <15 mg → decrease 1.25–2.5 mg/day every 2–6 weeks (can taper faster, ~8 weeks, if the patient is also on an IL-1 blocker)
  • Adverse effects: hypertension, fluid retention, hyperglycemia, osteoporosis, adrenal insufficiency, cushingoid appearance, psychiatric effects, peptic ulcer, infection risk, myopathy, glaucoma
  • Can be considered for tuberculous constrictive pericarditis specifically, though not recommended routinely for TB pericarditis
  • In severe renal impairment, colchicine is contraindicated — consider dialysis for uremic pericarditis

Interleukin-1 (IL-1) Blockers

  • Anakinra and rilonacept are indicated for patients with multiple recurrent episodes who are colchicine-resistant or corticosteroid-dependent, particularly those with an autoinflammatory phenotype (CRP >10 mg/L, the threshold used in clinical trials)
  • Rilonacept is FDA-approved for treatment and prevention of recurrent pericarditis
  • Trial data: AIRTRIP (anakinra) — 90% recurrence after withdrawal vs. 18% who continued; RHAPSODY (rilonacept) — 74% recurrence after withdrawal vs. 6.7% who continued (median time to recurrence with placebo: 8.6 weeks)
  • Patients on corticosteroids can often transition to an IL-1 blocker and discontinue steroids relatively quickly (~7–8 weeks) — allowing a faster steroid taper (e.g., ↓5 mg/week) than would otherwise be safe
  • Dosing: anakinra 1–2 mg/kg/day SC (max 100 mg/day); rilonacept 320 mg SC loading dose, then 160 mg SC weekly
  • Duration: at least 6–12 months, longer for multiple recurrent disease
  • Most common ADRs: injection site reactions (~33–71%, mostly mild/moderate) and upper respiratory tract infections (~20–48%, mostly mild/moderate)

Treatment by Recurrence Number (Figure 4 Framework)

PresentationTreatment
Initial occurrence of acute pericarditisHigh-dose NSAIDs until chest pain resolves and CRP normalizes + colchicine for 3 months
First recurrenceNSAIDs until chest pain resolves and inflammatory markers normalize + colchicine for ≥6 months
Second recurrenceAdd an IL-1 blocker (if idiopathic or post-cardiac injury syndrome, colchicine-resistant, with a history of CRP elevation) or low-to-moderate-dose prednisone (if underlying systemic autoimmune disease)

Special Situations

  • Tuberculous pericarditis: antituberculosis therapy; corticosteroids considered for associated constrictive TB pericarditis
  • Uremic pericarditis: dialysis (initiate or intensify)
  • Purulent/bacterial pericarditis: IV antibiotics + surgical drainage — a medical/surgical emergency
  • Autoimmune pericarditis: treat the underlying condition first; NSAIDs/colchicine used empirically in practice, though not well studied specifically for autoimmune-associated pericarditis (e.g., SLE)
  • Post-cardiac injury syndrome: NSAIDs + colchicine as in idiopathic disease; perioperative colchicine prophylaxis may reduce incidence (COPPS-2)

Areas of Uncertainty

  • Optimal treatment for incessant pericarditis, a severe first recurrence, or effusive-constrictive pericarditis
  • Efficacy of pericarditis-specific therapies in autoimmune disease
  • Optimal treatment duration in multiple recurrent pericarditis (can last years)
  • Role of radical pericardiectomy in multiple recurrent pericarditis (retrospective data support it for refractory pain, but its role alongside current anti-inflammatory options is undefined)
Quick Check — Treatment

A patient with idiopathic recurrent pericarditis has failed NSAIDs and colchicine, has a second recurrence, and has a history of CRP elevation (autoinflammatory phenotype). What is the preferred next step?

For colchicine-resistant idiopathic (or post-cardiac injury syndrome) pericarditis with a CRP-elevation history, IL-1 blockers are preferred over corticosteroids to resolve active episodes and prevent recurrence.

Key Take-Home Points

  • Pericarditis → pleuritic/positional pain; STEMI → territorial ischemic pattern
  • A complication changes the pathway: troponin = myocardial involvement; hypotension/JVD = think tamponade
  • Echo is first-line; CMR answers the inflammation/tissue-characterization question; CT is especially useful for calcification/anatomic detail
  • Uncomplicated acute pericarditis → NSAID/aspirin + colchicine + exercise restriction
  • Persistent inflammation can progress to a rigid pericardium → constrictive physiology

Common Pitfalls

  • ST elevation alone does not equal STEMI — decide diffuse vs. territorial first
  • A normal BP early does not exclude tamponade — use the full hemodynamic pattern + echo
  • Do not label postoperative/post-ablation pericarditis as "Dressler syndrome" (that term is specifically post-MI)

12. OMM Connection: Why the Anatomy Matters

  • The parietal pericardium is innervated by the phrenic nerve (C3–C5) — this is why pericarditis pain refers to the trapezius ridge (same dermatome)
  • Sympathetic innervation: stellate ganglion and first dorsal ganglion
  • Parasympathetic supply: vagus and left recurrent laryngeal nerve; the visceral pericardium has no pain fibers

Viscerosomatic Reflexes

  • Cardiac visceral afferents travel with sympathetic fibers and synapse at T1–T5 spinal cord segments
  • Myocardial ischemia produces increased paraspinal muscle tone localized to T2–T5 on the left, detectable by palpation — this response is abolished by sympathectomy, confirming the sympathetic afferent pathway
  • In pericarditis, expect somatic dysfunction in the upper thoracic segments (T1–T5) — tissue texture changes, tenderness, restricted motion
  • Chapman reflex points for the heart: anterior — 2nd intercostal space near the sternum; posterior — between T2–T4 transverse processes

13. High-Yield Takeaways

  • Pericardial pain → parietal pericardium → phrenic nerve → trapezius ridge referral
  • Coxsackievirus B = classic viral cause; TB = #1 cause in the developing world
  • Dressler syndrome = post-MI autoimmune pericarditis (weeks later)
  • ECG Stage 1: diffuse concave ST elevation + PR depression (no reciprocal changes = not STEMI)
  • Beck's triad (tamponade): hypotension, JVD, muffled heart sounds
  • Pulsus paradoxus = most specific sign of tamponade (>10 mmHg inspiratory SBP drop); tachycardia = most sensitive
  • Electrical alternans = heart swinging in a large effusion
  • Kussmaul sign = JVP rises with inspiration (constrictive pericarditis)
  • Pericardial knock = early diastolic sound (constriction, not restriction)
  • Treatment: NSAIDs + colchicine (first-line); corticosteroids for failure/contraindication; IL-1 blockers for refractory recurrence
  • NLRP3 inflammasome → caspase-1 → IL-1β = the molecular pathway (shared with gout, FMF)
Board-Style Practice Questions

A 24-year-old man presents with sharp substernal chest pain that worsens supine and improves leaning forward. He had a URI last week. A scratchy triphasic sound is heard at the left sternal border. ECG shows diffuse concave-upward ST elevation and PR depression with PR elevation in aVR. What is the most likely diagnosis?

Positional pleuritic pain, a friction rub, and diffuse ST elevation with PR depression (no reciprocal changes, no Q waves) is classic acute pericarditis. The recent URI suggests a viral etiology. STEMI would show territorial ST changes with reciprocal depression.

A 62-year-old man who underwent CABG 3 weeks ago presents with fever, pleuritic chest pain, and malaise. Echo shows a moderate pericardial effusion. CRP is elevated. Troponin is normal. What is the most likely diagnosis?

Post-cardiac injury syndrome is autoimmune-mediated pericarditis occurring weeks after cardiac surgery (or MI, when it is specifically termed Dressler syndrome). The latent period, pleuritic pain, fever, elevated CRP, and effusion are characteristic. First-line treatment is NSAIDs plus colchicine; corticosteroids increase recurrence risk.
