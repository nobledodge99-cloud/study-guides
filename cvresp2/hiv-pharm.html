Part 1 — HIV Life Cycle, Disease Course & Treatment Goals

HIV life cycle

Combination antiretroviral therapy (ART) prolongs life and prevents disease progression, but must be given lifelong to control viral replication — used improperly, it promotes rapid emergence of permanent drug resistance. Each step of the viral life cycle is a distinct drug target:

StepWhat happens
Attachment & fusionHIV gp120 binds the CD4 receptor on lymphocytes/macrophages, producing a conformational change that exposes the co-receptor binding site (CCR5 or CXCR4); co-receptor binding exposes gp41, which drives membrane fusion
Penetration & uncoatingFollowing fusion, full-length viral RNA enters the cell cytoplasm
Reverse transcriptionRNA is copied to a short-lived RNA-DNA duplex; original RNA is degraded by RNase H, yielding double-stranded DNA. HIV reverse transcriptase is error-prone, allowing frequent mutations
IntegrationViral DNA is transported to the nucleus; viral integrase incorporates it into the host chromosome
Transcription / translationThe virus may remain quiescent until the cell divides; viral RNA is produced and translated into viral proteins
Assembly / budding & releaseEnvelope and structural proteins assemble at the cell surface; nucleocapsid cores bud through the membrane, creating new enveloped (but still immature) particles
MaturationHIV protease cleaves precursor polyproteins to produce final structural/enzymatic proteins, yielding a functional, infectious virion
Clinical pearlIntegration is the single most important mechanistic fact in this whole unit: once viral DNA is stitched into the host chromosome, no current antiretroviral can reach and eliminate it. That one step is the entire reason ART controls but does not cure HIV.

Check yourself

Exam-style question
Which sequence of steps correctly reflects the early stages of the HIV life cycle after a virion encounters a susceptible CD4+ cell?
AIntegration, then reverse transcription, then binding
Bgp41 binds CD4 directly and gp120 mediates membrane fusion
CBinding to CD4 causes a gp120 conformational change that allows binding to a co-receptor (CCR5/CXCR4), followed by gp41-mediated membrane fusion
DThe virus is phagocytosed and reverse transcription occurs in a lysosome
EMaturation occurs before the virus enters the cell
Explanation: HIV's gp120 envelope glycoprotein binds CD4 first, producing a conformational change that exposes the co-receptor binding site (CCR5 or CXCR4). Co-receptor binding produces a further conformational change that exposes gp41, which then mediates fusion of the viral and host cell membranes — allowing viral entry.
Exam-style question
After HIV RNA enters the host cell cytoplasm, what happens during reverse transcription, and why is this step central to the development of drug resistance?
AHost cell DNA polymerase copies viral RNA with high fidelity, so resistance is rare
BReverse transcription occurs in the nucleus using host RNA polymerase II
CReverse transcriptase only degrades viral RNA and does not synthesize new DNA
DReverse transcription cannot occur until after integration
EHIV reverse transcriptase converts viral RNA into double-stranded DNA, but the enzyme is highly error-prone, producing frequent mutations that create the diversity underlying drug resistance
Explanation: HIV reverse transcriptase copies genomic RNA into a short-lived RNA-DNA duplex; the original RNA strand is degraded by RNase H, allowing synthesis of double-stranded DNA. This reverse transcriptase is error-prone, generating the frequent mutations that are the raw material for drug resistance — which is why combination therapy (targeting the virus from multiple angles at once) is required.
Exam-style question
Why can't currently available antiretroviral therapy (ART) eradicate or cure HIV infection, even with fully suppressed plasma viremia?
AART drugs cannot physically reach the bloodstream in sufficient concentration
BA pool of latently infected CD4+ T-cells is established during the earliest stages of acute infection and persists with a long half-life; HIV DNA integrated into the host genome in these resting cells is not targeted by any current drug class
CHIV protease continues to cleave viral proteins even during full ART suppression
DThe virus mutates faster than any regimen can track, even with perfect adherence
EReverse transcriptase remains active in the plasma despite ART
Explanation: Once HIV DNA integrates into the host cell chromosome, a latent reservoir of infected CD4+ T-cells is established very early in infection. These cells persist with a long half-life, and no current antiretroviral targets integrated, transcriptionally silent viral DNA — this is the fundamental reason ART must be taken lifelong rather than as a curative course.

Natural history of untreated HIV & goals of therapy

50-70% of patients experience an acute retroviral syndrome 3-6 weeks after primary infection (fever, pharyngitis, lymphadenopathy, headache, myalgia, malaise, weight loss, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea), coinciding with a sharp rise in plasma HIV RNA (widespread dissemination) and a sharp fall in CD4 count. An immune response then drives partial recovery and a prolonged clinical latency period — though sensitive assays confirm the virus is present in plasma at all times. CD4 counts continue to decline over the following years (median ~10 years to clinical illness) until a critical threshold is reached, below which there is substantial risk of opportunistic disease (constitutional symptoms plus infections such as Pneumocystis pneumonia, CMV, Candida, Toxoplasma, MAC/TB, Cryptococcus, and HSV).

Clinical pearlPatients started on ART before their CD4 count drops below 350 can expect a life expectancy similar to HIV-negative individuals. Left untreated, once CD4 falls below 50 life expectancy is only about 12-18 more months — a stark illustration of why early diagnosis and immediate ART initiation matter so much.

Antiretrovirals will not eradicate HIV, because a pool of latently infected CD4 cells is established during the earliest stages of acute infection and persists with a long half-life despite prolonged viral suppression. Goals of therapy: decrease morbidity and prolong duration/quality of survival; restore and preserve immune function; suppress viral load; prevent transmission.

Exam-style question
At which three time points should HIV drug resistance testing generally be performed?
AOnly once, at the time of AIDS diagnosis
BBaseline (before starting therapy), at initiation of therapy if treatment was deferred, and at virologic failure (any time a regimen needs to be adjusted)
COnly during pregnancy
DOnly if the patient requests it
EEvery single day while on therapy
Explanation: Resistance testing is performed at baseline to guide initial regimen selection (without delaying therapy start while awaiting results), again at initiation of therapy if treatment was previously deferred (since the patient may have acquired a drug-resistant strain in the interim), and at virologic failure — since salvage regimens guided by resistance testing produce better virologic responses than those guided by clinical judgment alone.

Initiating antiretroviral therapy

ART is now recommended for all persons with HIV, regardless of CD4 count — both to reduce morbidity/mortality and to prevent transmission to others. Guidelines recommend initiating ART immediately (or as soon as possible) after diagnosis to increase ART uptake, decrease time to viral suppression, and improve rates of virologic suppression; treatment may reasonably be deferred for clinical or psychosocial reasons but should start as soon as possible. Previous terminology ("HAART") has been replaced with simply "ART."

A standard regimen consists of two NRTIs (an NRTI "backbone," one of which is typically emtricitabine or lamivudine) plus at least one drug from a different class:

  • An integrase strand transfer inhibitor (INSTI), or
  • A non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NNRTI), or
  • A pharmacokinetically-enhanced protease inhibitor (PI)

Preferred initial regimens for the antiretroviral-naive patient

Integrase strand transfer inhibitor (INSTI)-based regimens have surpassed all other categories for initial therapy, due to high virologic efficacy, excellent safety/tolerability, and low risk of drug-drug interactions.

RegimenNotes
Bictegravir/tenofovir alafenamide/emtricitabine [BIC/TAF/FTC]Once-daily, non-boosted, single-tablet co-formulation
Dolutegravir plus tenofovir/emtricitabine (or lamivudine)Single-tablet co-formulation also available; well tolerated
Dolutegravir/abacavir/lamivudine [DTG/ABC/3TC]Requires HLA-B*5701-negative status; moved to "certain clinical situations" when renal/bone toxicity from tenofovir must be avoided

Regimen selection should weigh: virologic efficacy (certain drugs should be avoided at viral loads >100,000 copies/mL), toxicity/adverse effects, pill burden, dosing frequency, drug-drug interaction potential, resistance testing results, comorbid conditions, and cost/access.

Clinical pearlNNRTI-based regimens have been falling out of first-line status over the last several years — efavirenz was downgraded for CNS/psychiatric toxicity (including suicidality), and other agents were downgraded for boosting requirements, higher pill burden, or a lower barrier to resistance relative to INSTI regimens. Just because a drug isn't first-line anymore doesn't mean you won't see patients maintained on it from years ago.

Part 2 — Basic Pharmacology of the Antiretroviral Drug Classes

Nucleoside/nucleotide reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs)

Focus drugs (red font): abacavir, emtricitabine, lamivudine, tenofovir disoproxil fumarate, tenofovir alafenamide

Agents: abacavir (ABC), emtricitabine (FTC), lamivudine (3TC), tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF), tenofovir alafenamide (TAF), zidovudine (ZDV).

MOA: after intracytoplasmic phosphorylation, competitively inhibit HIV-1 reverse transcriptase and incorporate into viral DNA, preventing binding of the next incoming nucleotide (chain termination).

PK: do not undergo CYP450 metabolism — few significant drug-drug interactions.

ADRs: may inhibit mitochondrial DNA polymerase-γ, causing mitochondrial toxicity (anemia, granulocytopenia, myopathy, peripheral neuropathy), lipoatrophy, and rare but potentially fatal lactic acidosis with hepatic steatosis. Emtricitabine, lamivudine, and tenofovir have low affinity for DNA polymerase-γ → less mitochondrial toxicity risk, making them the preferred NRTI backbone components.

AgentUnique feature
AbacavirSerious hypersensitivity reaction in HLA-B*5701 carriers — screen before use; rash; possible increased MI risk; avoid alcohol
EmtricitabineHyperpigmentation
Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF)Renal toxicity, bone loss
Tenofovir alafenamide (TAF)Prodrug of TDF with similar efficacy at 1/10th the dose — smaller doses, potentially less toxicity, but still ↑creatinine/proteinuria/bone loss
LamivudineRare pancreatitis

Resistance develops more slowly than with NNRTIs or PIs, generally requiring a minimum of 3-4 codon substitutions.

Check yourself

Exam-style question
What is the mechanism of action of the nucleoside/nucleotide reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs), and what step is required before they become active?
AThey allosterically inhibit reverse transcriptase without requiring any modification
BAfter intracytoplasmic phosphorylation, they are incorporated into the growing viral DNA chain in place of the natural nucleotide, preventing binding of the next incoming nucleotide and causing chain termination

Explanation: NRTIs require intracytoplasmic phosphorylation to their active triphosphate form. Non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs)

Focus drug (red font): efavirenz

Agents: delavirdine, doravirine, efavirenz (EFV, prototype), etravirine, nevirapine, rilpivirine.

MOA: bind reverse transcriptase near to, but at a site distinct from, the NRTIs; allosterically inhibit RNA- and DNA-dependent DNA polymerase activity. Do not require phosphorylation and do not compete with nucleosides.

PK: undergo CYP450 metabolism → many drug interactions (some agents are inducers, some inhibitors, some — like efavirenz — mixed inducer/inhibitors).

ADRs: varying GI intolerance; skin rash (infrequently serious — Stevens-Johnson syndrome). Efavirenz specifically causes prominent CNS/neuropsychiatric toxicity — abnormal dreams, insomnia, impaired concentration, hallucinations, dizziness/drowsiness, and psychiatric effects including suicidal ideation and suicide attempts (a major reason it was downgraded from first-line status); teratogenic in primates historically limited first-trimester use.

Clinical pearlDoravirine is the newest NNRTI and offers once-daily dosing with fewer adverse effects and drug interactions than efavirenz — a reasonable alternate for patients wanting to switch within the class.

Resistance: high-level resistance may result from a single point mutation in the reverse transcriptase binding pocket — these drugs are highly effective but MUST always be combined with at least two other active agents.

Why must NNRTIs always be used as part of a combination regimen rather than as monotherapy or added carelessly to an existing regimen?

Protease inhibitors (PIs)

Focus drugs (red font): atazanavir, darunavir, lopinavir/ritonavir, ritonavir (as a booster)

Agents: atazanavir (ATV), darunavir (DRV), fosamprenavir, lopinavir/ritonavir (LPV/RTV), nelfinavir, saquinavir, tipranavir.

MOA: competitively inhibit viral protease, the enzyme responsible for cleaving Gag and Gag-Pol precursor polyproteins into functional structural/enzymatic proteins; prevents proper processing → produces immature, noninfectious particles.

PK: undergo extensive CYP450 metabolism, primarily CYP3A4 → many drug interactions.

Clinical pearl“Ritonavir boosting”: ritonavir itself is not used as an antiretroviral at therapeutic doses — instead it's given at low, non-therapeutic doses purely for its potent CYP3A4 inhibitory activity. By inhibiting CYP3A4, it slows metabolism of a co-administered PI, increasing drug exposure, prolonging half-life, and allowing reduced dosing frequency. It's a drug-drug interaction used deliberately as a therapeutic tool.

ADRs: mild-to-moderate nausea/diarrhea, dyslipidemia and altered fat distribution, hyperglycemia/insulin resistance, elevated transaminases, increased cardiovascular disease risk, increased bleeding risk in hemophiliacs.

Resistance develops at a speed intermediate between NRTIs and NNRTIs — high-level resistance requires a minimum of 4-5 codon substitutions.

Entry inhibitors

HIV entry is complex: the virus attaches to the host via the envelope glycoprotein complex gp160 (gp120 + gp41) binding CD4; this produces conformational changes in gp120 allowing access to a chemokine co-receptor (CCR5 or CXCR4); co-receptor binding produces a further conformational change exposing gp41, leading to viral envelope fusion and entry.

Fusion inhibitor — enfuvirtide (T20)

MOA: binds the gp41 subunit, preventing the conformational change necessary for viral/cellular membrane fusion. PK: synthetic 36-amino-acid peptide; only administered subcutaneously (parenteral); undergoes proteolytic hydrolysis, no CYP involvement. ADRs: local injection site reactions (painful erythematous nodules) are common; also insomnia, headache, dizziness, nausea; rare hypersensitivity. Generally reserved for patients with multidrug-resistant HIV.

CCR5 co-receptor antagonist — maraviroc

MOA: selectively binds CCR5, preventing viral entry. Only active against virus that uses CCR5 exclusively — inactive against CXCR4, dual, or mixed-tropic virus (a shift from CCR5 to CXCR4 tropism is associated with advancing disease). Specific tropism testing is required before treatment. PK: substrate of CYP3A4 → caution with drug-drug interactions. ADRs: generally well tolerated — cough, upper respiratory infections, muscle/joint pain, diarrhea, sleep disturbance, elevated aminotransferases; theoretical (unconfirmed) risk of malignancy/infection from CCR5 blockade.

Because CCR5-exclusive tropism cannot be assumed, specific tropism testing is required before initiating maraviroc to ensure the drug will actually be effective.

HIV integrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTIs)

Focus drugs (red font): bictegravir, dolutegravir

Agents: bictegravir (BIC), cabotegravir (CAB-LA — long-acting injectable), dolutegravir (DTG), elvitegravir (EVG), raltegravir (RAL).

MOA: bind HIV integrase, preventing strand transfer and integration of reverse-transcribed viral DNA into the host chromosome.

PK: primarily metabolized by glucuronidation, but are substrates of CYP3A4 and UGT1A1 — low potential to cause significant interactions themselves, but susceptible to known enzyme inducers (e.g., rifampin), requiring dose adjustment.

ADRs: generally very well tolerated — headache and GI effects are the most common; among the best-tolerated antiretrovirals overall, which has driven their move to first-line status.

Clinical pearlLong-acting cabotegravir (CAB-LA), given intramuscularly every 4-8 weeks, was FDA-approved in 2021 as the first injectable option for HIV PrEP. If HIV is acquired despite CAB-LA, resistance may compromise future INSTI options — a more consequential resistance risk than breakthrough infection on oral tenofovir/emtricitabine PrEP.

Other agents for multidrug-resistant HIV

Both agents below are reserved for heavily treatment-experienced adults with multidrug-resistant HIV-1 failing their current regimen — neither is used in treatment-naive patients.

Ibalizumab (post-attachment inhibitor; anti-CD4 monoclonal antibody)

MOA: recombinant, humanized monoclonal antibody; blocks HIV-1 from infecting CD4+ cells by binding domain 2 of the CD4 receptor itself (not the virus), inducing a conformational change that sterically hinders the interaction of gp120 with HIV co-receptors. Active against both CCR5- and CXCR4-tropic isolates. PK: IV loading dose then maintenance every 14 days; t1/2 ~3 days. ADRs: diarrhea, dizziness, nausea, rash. Approved by the FDA in March 2018 with Fast Track/Priority Review/Breakthrough Therapy designations.

Fostemsavir (attachment inhibitor; gp120 attachment inhibitor)

MOA: prodrug hydrolyzed to active temsavir; binds the HIV-1 envelope protein gp120 subunit, selectively inhibiting interactions between the virus and CD4, preventing host cell attachment (may also inhibit post-attachment entry steps). PK: oral; t1/2 ~11 hours; hydrolysis and CYP3A4 oxidation. ADRs: nausea, diarrhea, headache, increased serum creatinine.

Part 3 — Special Populations

Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP)

Goal: reduce the risk of acquiring HIV infection in high-risk individuals — can reduce transmission risk by >90% with adherence. Candidates: serodiscordant couples (HIV-negative partner, until the positive partner's viral load is stably suppressed ≥6 months on ART), high-risk sexually-active MSM and transgender women, high-risk heterosexually-active men and women, and people who inject drugs.

Current HIV infection must be excluded (documented negative HIV Ag/Ab test) before starting PrEP — using the two-drug PrEP regimen in someone already infected risks selecting for a drug-resistant virus.

Regimen: daily oral tenofovir DF or TAF plus emtricitabine, continued as long as risk persists, or long-acting injectable cabotegravir every 8 weeks.

Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP)

Applies to both occupational exposure (healthcare personnel — percutaneous, mucous membrane, or non-intact skin contact with concerning body fluids) and nonoccupational exposure. Timing: administer ASAP, ideally within 1-2 hours; PEP is likely less effective if >72 hours have passed, though the US Public Health Service allows it to be offered up to about one week after exposure.

Regimen (both occupational and nonoccupational): a three-drug regimen — tenofovir/emtricitabine (NRTI backbone) plus an integrase inhibitor (bictegravir OR dolutegravir) — for 4 weeks. PEP is discontinued if the source patient is confirmed HIV-negative.

Antiretroviral therapy during pregnancy & perinatal management

Ongoing reproductive counseling is essential throughout HIV care; achieving and maintaining viral suppression before conception protects maternal health and minimizes perinatal transmission risk (typically <1% with effective ART). Pregnancy should not preclude effective ART unless drug levels are inadequate or risks outweigh benefits — regimens recommended for pregnant people are generally the same as for non-pregnant adults, with attention to specific risks: PIs may contribute to hyperglycemia/premature birth; NRTIs may cause mitochondrial dysfunction.

Clinical pearlGuidance on efavirenz and dolutegravir in pregnancy has shifted: efavirenz's first-trimester restriction (over neural tube defect concern) is no longer enforced by WHO/US/UK experts, and dolutegravir's neural tube defect risk was found to be lower than initially proposed with more data — dolutegravir is now a preferred drug throughout pregnancy and for those trying to conceive.

Antiretroviral management for HIV-exposed infants

All infants exposed to HIV in utero or during delivery should begin ARV prophylaxis within 6 hours of birth, tailored to gestational age/weight and maternal HIV RNA near delivery: high risk (maternal HIV RNA ≥50 copies/mL within 4 weeks of delivery) → three-drug regimen for 2-6 weeks; low risk (maternal HIV RNA <50 copies/mL from 20 weeks gestation through delivery) → zidovudine (ZDV) alone for 2 weeks. During breastfeeding, risk assessment is ongoing based on maternal viral load and ART adherence, with extended prophylaxis (nevirapine or lamivudine) sometimes recommended.

Opportunistic infections

Opportunistic infections (OIs) — infections more frequent or severe because of HIV-related immunosuppression — were the principal cause of morbidity/mortality before widespread ART use. Since then, potent ART has had the most profound influence on reducing OI-related mortality, though chemoprophylaxis and improved management of acute OIs also contributed historically. CD4 count determines OI risk and guides prophylaxis choice:
