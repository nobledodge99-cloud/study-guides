Part 1 · Introduction to TB

1. Mycobacterial Biology, Epidemiology & Transmission

Dr. Waller's own framing for this unit: the top four drugs are isoniazidCore, rifampinCore, pyrazinamideCore, and ethambutolCore — “if these were the only drugs you had the bandwidth to study, this would be an excellent start.” RifabutinCore and rifapentineCore are her paired alternative-agent learning objective. Everything else (streptomycin, second-line agents, atypical mycobacteria drugs) is additional — recognize the category and strategy, don't memorize every regimen table.

Why the cell wall makes TB pharmacology unique

  • Mycolic acids form a thick, waxy, hydrophobic outer layer — the target of isoniazid — that makes the organism acid-fast (retains carbol fuchsin dye despite acid-alcohol wash) and intrinsically impermeable to many antibiotics.
  • Arabinogalactan links the mycolic acid layer to peptidoglycan — the target of ethambutol.
  • Native efflux pumps extrude drug before it can act, contributing to baseline resistance.
  • Very slow generation time (~22–24h vs. E. coli's ~20 min) → slow-growing, dormant/persister populations that standard rapidly-bactericidal drugs don't fully clear → this is why TB regimens run for months, not days.

Epidemiology & transmission

Mycobacterium tuberculosis spreads via airborne droplet nuclei (cough, sneeze, speech) — small enough to stay suspended and reach the alveoli. Risk of transmission rises with closer/longer contact, higher bacillary burden (cavitary/smear-positive disease), and poor ventilation.

Outcomes after exposure

What happens after inhaling M. tuberculosis
Outcome~FrequencyKey feature
Immediate clearanceMinorityInnate/early immune response eliminates the organism
Latent TB infection (LTBI)Majority of infectedContained by granuloma formation; positive TST/IGRA, asymptomatic, non-infectious, normal CXR
Primary progressive (active) disease~5–10% lifetime risk from LTBI (higher if immunocompromised, e.g., HIV)Symptomatic, infectious if pulmonary/cavitary

Clinical presentation of active disease

Constitutional: fever, night sweats, weight loss, fatigue. Pulmonary: productive cough >2–3 weeks, hemoptysis, chest pain. Extrapulmonary disease (lymphatic, skeletal/Pott's disease, meningeal, renal, miliary) is more common with immunosuppression.

The strategic takeaway
LTBI vs. active disease is the single most important branch point in this entire unit — it determines whether a patient needs one drug for months or several drugs in combination, and whether isolation/public health reporting apply.
Check yourself — 3 questions

Which structural feature of the mycobacterial cell wall is directly responsible for the organism's acid-fast staining property and is also the specific target of isoniazid?

Mycolic acids form the thick, waxy outer layer responsible for acid-fastness and are the specific biosynthetic target inhibited by isoniazid.

A patient has a positive tuberculin skin test, no symptoms, a normal chest x-ray, and is not infectious to others. Which stage of TB infection does this describe?

Asymptomatic infection contained by the immune system, with a positive TST/IGRA but a normal chest x-ray and no infectiousness, defines latent TB infection.

Why do standard TB regimens require months of therapy rather than the 1-2 week courses used for many acute bacterial infections?

M. tuberculosis divides far more slowly than typical bacteria, leaving dormant/persister organisms that are not eliminated quickly — the biologic basis for multi-month combination therapy.
Part 2 · First-Line Agent #1

2. Isoniazid (INH)

IsoniazidCore is the backbone of both LTBI treatment and every active-disease regimen — the single most important drug in this unit.

Mechanism & resistance

A prodrug activated by the mycobacterial catalase-peroxidase KatG; the activated form inhibits InhA (enoyl-ACP reductase), blocking mycolic acid synthesis — bactericidal against actively dividing organisms. Resistance arises chiefly from katG mutations (loss of activation) or inhA mutations (reduced drug binding).

Pharmacogenomics — the acetylator phenotype

INH is metabolized by hepatic N-acetyltransferase 2 (NAT2), which is genetically polymorphic:

Rapid vs. slow acetylators
PhenotypeDrug handlingClinical risk
Rapid acetylatorClears INH quicklyRisk of subtherapeutic levels, especially with weekly dosing or malabsorption → treatment failure/relapse
Slow acetylatorAccumulates more parent drug and downstream Phase I metaboliteHigher risk of hepatotoxicity, via the hepatotoxic metabolite acetylhydrazine — risk compounded when rifampin (a CYP2E1 inducer) is co-administered

Key adverse effects

  • Hepatotoxicity — the major dose-limiting toxicity; monitor LFTs, especially in slow acetylators, older patients, alcohol use, or with rifampin co-therapy.
  • Peripheral neuropathy — INH depletes pyridoxine (vitamin B6) by forming an inactive hydrazone complex; supplement B6 in patients at risk (malnutrition, diabetes, HIV, alcoholism, pregnancy) to prevent this.
  • Drug-induced lupus-like syndrome (uncommon); mild CNS effects.
The exam-favorite pairing
INH depletes B6 → give pyridoxine to prevent peripheral neuropathy. Rifampin induces INH's own hepatotoxic pathway (CYP2E1) → the two core drugs interact with each other's toxicity profile, not just their own.
Check yourself — 3 questions

Isoniazid inhibits InhA (enoyl-ACP reductase) after activation by KatG. What cell wall component synthesis pathway does this block?

Activated isoniazid inhibits InhA, a key enzyme in the mycolic acid biosynthetic pathway, which is the drug's bactericidal mechanism.

A patient found to be a slow NAT2 acetylator is started on isoniazid plus rifampin for active TB. Why is this combination of patient and drug factors particularly concerning?

Slow acetylators accumulate more of isoniazid's hepatotoxic metabolite (via CYP2E1-mediated pathways), and rifampin further induces this pathway — together raising hepatotoxicity risk substantially.

A patient with poor nutritional status started on isoniazid is also given pyridoxine (vitamin B6). What is the rationale for this co-administration?

Isoniazid binds and inactivates pyridoxine, and resulting B6 deficiency causes peripheral neuropathy — pyridoxine supplementation prevents this, particularly in patients at higher baseline risk.
Part 2 · First-Line Agent #2 + Paired Alternatives

3. The Rifamycins — Rifampin, Rifabutin & Rifapentine

RifampinCore is core drug #2. RifabutinCore and rifapentineCore are the explicitly-flagged paired alternative agents — same family, same target, different pharmacokinetics.

Shared mechanism & cross-resistance

All three bind the β-subunit of bacterial RNA polymerase (rpoB), blocking RNA transcription — bactericidal, including against slow/intermittently-dividing organisms (a key reason rifamycins shorten therapy duration). Because they share the identical binding site, resistance-conferring rpoB mutations confer complete cross-resistance across all three agents — resistance to one means resistance to all.

What differs: CYP450 induction potency

Rifamycin comparison
AgentCYP450 inductionNotable use caseSignature toxicity
RifampinCoreStrongest inducer (CYP3A4 and others)Standard first-line backboneHarmless orange discoloration of body fluids; hepatotoxicity; flu-like reaction
RifabutinCoreWeakest inducerPreferred over rifampin in patients on antiretroviral protease inhibitors/certain NNRTIs (less interaction with those drugs' metabolism)Uveitis, neutropenia, orange discoloration
RifapentineCoreStrong inducer (intermediate/long half-life)Long half-life enables once-weekly dosing; used in the 3-month once-weekly LTBI regimen and the CDC/WHO 4-month active-disease regimenSimilar to rifampin; orange discoloration, flu-like reaction
Why rifabutin exists
This is the recurring exam trap Dr. Waller flagged: rifampin's potent CYP450 induction can drop protease-inhibitor/NNRTI levels dangerously low in HIV patients. Rifabutin's much weaker induction makes it the substitutable rifamycin in that specific population — same antimycobacterial efficacy, safer drug interaction profile.

Other key adverse effects across the class

Hepatotoxicity (additive with isoniazid), GI upset, and a flu-like/hypersensitivity reaction with intermittent dosing. The orange-red discoloration of urine, tears, sweat, and contact lenses is harmless but should be anticipated for adherence counseling.

Check yourself — 3 questions

Rifampin, rifabutin, and rifapentine all bind the same molecular target. What is that target, and what does sharing it mean for resistance?

All three rifamycins bind rpoB (RNA polymerase beta-subunit); because the binding site is identical, an rpoB mutation conferring resistance to one confers resistance to all three.

An HIV-positive patient on a protease inhibitor develops active TB. Why might rifabutin be selected over rifampin in this specific patient?

Rifampin's potent CYP450 induction can dangerously lower levels of protease inhibitors and some NNRTIs; rifabutin's much weaker induction makes it the preferred rifamycin substitute in patients on those antiretrovirals.

A patient started on rifapentine as part of a once-weekly regimen is counseled about an expected, harmless side effect. What should the patient be told to anticipate?

Rifamycins commonly cause harmless orange-red discoloration of body fluids (urine, tears, sweat) and can permanently stain soft contact lenses — an expected effect worth pre-counseling for adherence.
Part 2 · First-Line Agent #3

4. Pyrazinamide (PZA)

PyrazinamideCore is core drug #3 — unique among the first-line agents for its activity against a population the others largely miss.

Mechanism & resistance

A prodrug converted by mycobacterial pyrazinamidase (PncA) to its active form, pyrazinoic acid, which disrupts membrane function/energetics in an acidic environment. It is specifically active against semi-dormant, intracellular organisms living in the acidic milieu of macrophages/caseous lesions — a population the other first-line drugs don't reliably reach. This is why PZA is included only in the initial intensive phase (first 2 months) of standard therapy, not the continuation phase. Resistance is chiefly via pncA mutations (loss of activation).

Key adverse effects

  • Hepatotoxicity — considered the most hepatotoxic of the first-line agents, which is why it is the drug omitted (or reintroduced last) when rebuilding a regimen after drug-induced hepatitis.
  • Hyperuricemia (competitive inhibition of urate excretion) — can precipitate gout flares; usually managed expectantly unless symptomatic.
  • GI upset, photosensitivity, nonspecific arthralgias.
Match the drug to the niche
Isoniazid and rifampin are the broad bactericidal workhorses. Pyrazinamide's specific value is reaching the acidic, semi-dormant intracellular population — that's why it's front-loaded into the intensive phase rather than carried through the full course.
Check yourself — 3 questions

Pyrazinamide is included specifically during the initial 2-month intensive phase of standard TB therapy rather than the full treatment course. What is the pharmacologic rationale?

Pyrazinoic acid, the active metabolite, specifically targets semi-dormant organisms in the acidic intracellular environment — a niche addressed during the intensive phase, after which the drug is dropped from the continuation phase.

A patient on standard 4-drug TB therapy develops significant hepatotoxicity. When first-line agents are sequentially reintroduced, which drug is typically reintroduced last (or omitted), and why?

Pyrazinamide is considered the most hepatotoxic first-line agent, so it is reintroduced last (or omitted entirely) when rebuilding a regimen after drug-induced hepatitis.

A patient on pyrazinamide develops joint pain and is found to have an elevated uric acid level. What is the mechanism behind this adverse effect?

Pyrazinamide (via its metabolite) competitively inhibits uric acid excretion by the kidney, producing hyperuricemia that can precipitate gout-like arthralgias.
Part 2 · First-Line Agent #4

5. Ethambutol (EMB)

EthambutolCore rounds out the core four — primarily included empirically to protect against unrecognized resistance to the other agents.

Mechanism & resistance

Inhibits arabinosyl transferases, blocking arabinogalactan synthesis in the cell wall — bacteriostatic. Because its only role in the standard regimen is to cover for possible resistance to isoniazid or rifampin until susceptibility results return, it can be dropped once full susceptibility to the other agents is confirmed. Resistance arises via mutations in the embB gene.

Key adverse effect — the one to know cold

Optic neuritis (dose-related): decreased visual acuity, loss of red-green color discrimination, central/peripheral scotomas. Requires baseline and periodic visual acuity and color-vision testing, dose adjustment for renal impairment, and prompt discontinuation if symptoms develop (usually reversible if caught early). Use with caution in patients unable to reliably report visual changes (e.g., young children).

Why ethambutol is in the regimen at all
It isn't there for extra killing power on a susceptible organism — it's there as an empiric fourth agent so that if the isolate later turns out resistant to isoniazid or rifampin, at least two active drugs were still on board from day one. That's also why it's the first drug dropped once full susceptibility is confirmed.
Check yourself — 2 questions

A patient started on ethambutol reports difficulty distinguishing red from green and decreased visual acuity. What is the appropriate next step?

Red-green color discrimination loss and decreased visual acuity are classic signs of ethambutol-induced optic neuritis; the drug should be stopped promptly, as early discontinuation improves the chance of reversibility.

Ethambutol is included in the initial 4-drug empiric regimen for active TB even when isoniazid and rifampin are likely to be effective. What is the primary rationale for including it?

Ethambutol's main role is empiric — ensuring at least two active drugs are on board if resistance to isoniazid or rifampin is later discovered; it is typically discontinued once full susceptibility is confirmed.
Part 2 · First-Line-Adjacent, Not First-Line

6. Streptomycin & the Injectable Agents

StreptomycinAdditional is often grouped with the first-line agents but, per Dr. Waller's explicit clarification, is not actually considered first-line — it's an injectable used selectively, most often for drug-resistant disease or when a first-line oral agent can't be used.

Mechanism & use

An aminoglycoside: binds the 30S ribosomal subunit, disrupting initiation and causing misreading of mRNA — bactericidal. Requires IM/IV administration (no oral bioavailability), which is itself a major practical limitation for months-long therapy.

Key adverse effects (class-typical for aminoglycosides)

  • Ototoxicity (vestibular and/or cochlear, may be irreversible)
  • Nephrotoxicity (usually reversible)
  • Neuromuscular blockade at high doses

Other injectable second-line agents (kanamycinAdditional, amikacinAdditional, capreomycinAdditional) share this same toxicity profile and are reserved for drug-resistant disease — recognize the category rather than memorizing each one individually.

Check yourself — 2 questions

Streptomycin is frequently grouped with the classic first-line TB agents in review materials. According to the lecture's explicit clarification, how should streptomycin actually be classified?

Despite often being lumped in with first-line agents, streptomycin is an injectable that is not truly first-line — it is used selectively, commonly for drug-resistant disease or when an oral first-line agent cannot be used.

A patient on streptomycin develops progressive hearing loss. What is the underlying mechanistic class of this toxicity, and is it typically reversible?

Streptomycin, like other aminoglycosides, carries a risk of ototoxicity (vestibular and/or cochlear) that can be permanent, distinguishing it from the generally reversible nephrotoxicity also seen with this class.
Part 3 · Clinical Pharmacology

7. Combination Therapy Rationale, Resistance Math & DOT

Why TB is never treated with a single active agent

Spontaneous resistance-conferring mutations arise in a mycobacterial population at a frequency of roughly 1 in 106 organisms for any single drug. A cavitary TB lesion can harbor 108–1010 organisms — meaning resistant mutants to any one drug are almost certainly already present before treatment starts. Combine two independently-acting active agents, and the probability that a single organism carries mutations conferring resistance to both drops to roughly 1 in 1012 — below the total bacillary burden. This is the entire rationale for multi-drug therapy: it isn't about broader spectrum, it's about mathematically outrunning spontaneous resistance.

The number to remember
Single agent: ~1 in 10^6 resistant. Two active agents together: ~1 in 10^12 — this is why active TB disease always requires ≥2 drugs the organism is susceptible to, never monotherapy, even briefly.

Directly Observed Therapy (DOT)

A healthcare worker (or approved designee) watches the patient take each dose. Rationale: therapy runs months, adherence directly determines whether resistance emerges, and inconsistent dosing is one of the most common drivers of acquired drug resistance. DOT is standard of care for active TB in most public health programs, and intermittent (e.g., 3×/week or once-weekly with rifapentine-based regimens) dosing schedules were specifically designed to make DOT logistically feasible.

Check yourself — 2 questions

A cavitary TB lesion contains approximately 10^9 organisms. If the spontaneous resistance frequency to a single drug is about 1 in 10^6, why does treating with two independently-acting active drugs (each with the same 1 in 10^6 resistance frequency) prevent treatment failure from resistance?

Combining two drugs with independent resistance mechanisms multiplies the individual mutation frequencies (about 1 in 10^6 each), yielding roughly 1 in 10^12 — below the total organism burden, so essentially no organism is resistant to both.

Why is directly observed therapy (DOT) considered standard of care for active TB treatment rather than simply prescribing self-administered pills?

Because TB therapy runs for months and inconsistent adherence is a leading cause of acquired resistance, directly observing each dose helps ensure consistent treatment and protects against resistance emerging mid-course.
Part 2/3 · Managing a Shared First-Line Toxicity

8. Drug-Induced Hepatitis — Recognition & Restart Algorithm

Isoniazid, rifampin, and pyrazinamide are all independently hepatotoxic, so this complication has to be managed as a class issue, not a single-drug issue.

Definition

Drug-induced hepatitis is generally defined as AST ≥3× the upper limit of normal (ULN) with symptoms (nausea, vomiting, jaundice, abdominal pain) or AST ≥5× ULN without symptoms.

Management algorithm

  1. Stop all first-line hepatotoxic agents (isoniazid, rifampin, pyrazinamide) immediately; a non-hepatotoxic agent (e.g., a fluoroquinolone plus/minus ethambutol, streptomycin) can bridge therapy in the interim so the patient isn't left untreated.
  2. Wait until AST falls to <2× ULN and symptoms resolve.
  3. Reintroduce sequentially, one drug at a time, about a week apart, in this order: rifampin → ethambutol → isoniazid. Pyrazinamide is usually reintroduced last, if at all, since it is considered the most hepatotoxic of the group.
  4. Whichever drug's reintroduction triggers a recurrence in LFTs is presumed the causative agent and is permanently omitted from the regimen going forward (with the rest of the regimen adjusted/extended accordingly).
Why rifampin goes back in first
Rifampin is generally the least hepatotoxic of the three implicated first-line drugs and is also the most valuable for shortening total treatment duration — so it's reintroduced first to preserve regimen efficacy while still testing tolerance stepwise.
Check yourself — 2 questions

A patient on standard 4-drug TB therapy develops AST 6x the upper limit of normal with no symptoms. What is the immediate next step?

An asymptomatic AST ≥5x ULN meets the threshold for drug-induced hepatitis, requiring immediate discontinuation of all hepatotoxic first-line agents, with a non-hepatotoxic bridging regimen considered in the interim.

After resolution of drug-induced hepatitis, first-line TB drugs are reintroduced sequentially about a week apart. What is the correct reintroduction order, and which drug is typically added last or omitted?

The standard sequential restart order is rifampin, then ethambutol, then isoniazid, each about a week apart, with pyrazinamide — considered the most hepatotoxic — reintroduced last or omitted entirely.
Part 3 · Putting the Drugs Together

9. Treatment Regimens — LTBI, Standard Active Disease & the 2022 Four-Month Option

Latent TB infection (LTBI) — single active agent is sufficient

Because bacillary burden is low and the organism is dormant (not the high-burden, mixed-population scenario driving the combination-therapy math above), LTBI can be treated with one active drug:

LTBI regimen options
RegimenDurationNotes
IsoniazidCore daily6–9 monthsClassic option; longest duration, lowest per-dose pill burden
IsoniazidCore + rifapentineCore once weekly3 months (12 doses)Shortest, DOT-friendly; preferred where available
RifampinCore daily4 monthsAlternative when isoniazid can't be used

Standard active-disease regimen

Intensive phase (2 months): isoniazid + rifampin + pyrazinamide + ethambutol (“RIPE”), daily or DOT-friendly intermittent dosing. Continuation phase (4 months): isoniazid + rifampin only, once pyrazinamide's job (acidic/intracellular population) is done and drug susceptibility is confirmed. Total: 6 months for fully-susceptible, uncomplicated pulmonary disease.

The CDC/WHO 2022 four-month regimen

A newer, shorter alternative for select drug-susceptible pulmonary TB: isoniazid + rifapentineCore + pyrazinamide + moxifloxacinCore for 4 months total. Substituting rifapentine (more potent, longer-acting rifamycin) and adding moxifloxacin (a fluoroquinolone with potent bactericidal activity) allows the same efficacy in two fewer months — a direct payoff of the two drugs Dr. Waller flagged as the paired learning objective (rifapentine) and the added core agent (moxifloxacin) for this shortened option.

Don't confuse the two four-month regimens
LTBI's isoniazid+rifapentine 3-month (12-dose) regimen and the newer CDC/WHO 4-month *active-disease* regimen (isoniazid+rifapentine+pyrazinamide+moxifloxacin) both lean on rifapentine's long half-life, but they treat different disease states with different drug counts and durations — don't merge them into one fact.
Check yourself — 3 questions

A patient with a positive IGRA, no symptoms, and a normal chest x-ray is diagnosed with latent TB infection. Why is a single active agent (e.g., isoniazid) considered sufficient here, when active disease always requires combination therapy?

LTBI involves a small, dormant bacillary population, unlike the large, actively dividing populations in active disease where spontaneous resistant mutants are essentially guaranteed to exist — so monotherapy is adequate for LTBI but not for active disease.

A patient begins standard 6-month therapy for drug-susceptible pulmonary TB. After the 2-month intensive phase with four drugs, susceptibility is confirmed and the regimen is narrowed. Which two drugs continue for the remaining 4 months?

The continuation phase of standard therapy consists of isoniazid and rifampin alone, once pyrazinamide's role in the acidic/intracellular population is complete and ethambutol's empiric resistance coverage is no longer needed.

The CDC/WHO 2022 four-month regimen for select drug-susceptible pulmonary TB substitutes and adds specific agents compared to the standard 6-month regimen. Which two drugs distinguish this shortened regimen?

The 4-month regimen substitutes the longer-acting, more potent rifapentine for rifampin and adds moxifloxacin, a potent fluoroquinolone — together allowing the same treatment goal in two fewer months.
Part 3 · MDR/XDR

10. Drug-Resistant TB — Definitions & Management Strategy (Conceptual)

Dr. Waller was explicit here: do not memorize the regimen-building table for drug-resistant TB — understand the overall management strategy.

Definitions

Resistance categories
CategoryDefinition
MDR-TBResistant to at least isoniazid and rifampin
XDR-TBMDR-TB plus resistance to any fluoroquinolone and to at least one of bedaquiline or linezolid

The strategy, not the table

  • Build a regimen of ≥4–5 likely-effective agents the organism has not been shown resistant to, drawn from progressively less-preferred groups (fluoroquinolones and newer agents like bedaquiline first; injectables and older second-line oral agents as later-line options).
  • Longer total duration than drug-susceptible disease (many months to well over a year, depending on regimen).
  • Involve or refer to a TB specialist/public health program — these regimens are individualized, not protocol-driven off a single table.
  • Second-line agents worth recognizing by category (not memorizing individually): later-generation fluoroquinolones (levofloxacinAdditional, moxifloxacinAdditional already core), injectables (kanamycinAdditional, amikacinAdditional, capreomycinAdditional), oral agents (ethionamideAdditional, cycloserineAdditional, PASAdditional), and newer targeted agents (bedaquilineAdditional, linezolidAdditional).
What to actually take away
The exam-relevant fact isn't which specific second-line drug pairs with which resistance pattern — it's that MDR/XDR management means more drugs, longer duration, and specialist-level individualization, built from a shrinking menu of active options as resistance accumulates.
Check yourself — 2 questions

A patient's TB isolate is resistant to isoniazid and rifampin, but susceptible to fluoroquinolones, bedaquiline, and linezolid. How should this be classified?

Resistance to at least isoniazid and rifampin (without the additional fluoroquinolone plus bedaquiline/linezolid resistance required for XDR-TB) defines MDR-TB.

Rather than memorizing a specific regimen-building table for drug-resistant TB, what is the more important conceptual takeaway about its management strategy?

The strategic principle — not a fixed table — is to assemble several (typically ≥4-5) likely-active agents from a hierarchy of drug classes, extend total duration, and individualize care, often with specialist or public health program involvement.
Part 3 · Clinical Pharmacology

11. Special Populations — HIV, Immunomodulating Drugs & Pregnancy

HIV co-infection

  • TB is more likely to progress from LTBI to active disease, and disease is more likely to be disseminated/extrapulmonary.
  • Rifamycin – antiretroviral drug interactions are the central management issue: rifampin's strong CYP450 induction can drop levels of protease inhibitors and some NNRTIs; rifabutinCore's weaker induction is the preferred substitute in that setting (reinforcing the rifamycin comparison from Section 3).
  • Timing of antiretroviral therapy initiation relative to TB therapy is individualized (generally started within weeks of TB treatment, sooner with lower CD4 counts), balancing immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) risk against progression risk.

Immunomodulating drugs (biologics, especially TNF-α inhibitors)

TNF-α is essential for granuloma maintenance/containment of latent infection. Patients starting a TNF-α inhibitor (or other significant immunosuppression) are at markedly increased risk of reactivating LTBI to active disease — hence universal screening (TST/IGRA) before starting these agents, with LTBI treatment initiated first when positive.

Pregnancy

  • Active TB in pregnancy is treated — untreated disease poses greater risk to mother and fetus than treatment.
  • Standard regimen (isoniazid, rifampin, ethambutol) is generally considered safe; pyrazinamide's safety data in pregnancy is more limited and its use varies by guideline/region.
  • Streptomycin (and other aminoglycosides) is contraindicated — associated with fetal ototoxicity/congenital deafness.
  • Pyridoxine supplementation with isoniazid is particularly emphasized in pregnancy.
The connective thread across all three populations
Each special population changes management for a *mechanistic* reason already covered elsewhere in this guide: CYP450 interactions (HIV/rifamycins), TNF-α's role in granuloma containment (immunomodulators → LTBI reactivation), and aminoglycoside fetal ototoxicity (pregnancy) — the populations aren't isolated facts, they're applications of mechanisms you already know.
Check yourself — 3 questions

A patient is about to start a TNF-alpha inhibitor for rheumatoid arthritis. Why is TB screening mandatory before initiating this therapy?

TNF-alpha helps maintain the granulomas that contain latent TB infection; inhibiting it removes that containment and substantially raises the risk of reactivation to active disease, making pre-treatment screening standard.

A pregnant patient is diagnosed with active pulmonary TB. Which first-line-adjacent agent must specifically be avoided due to fetal risk?

Streptomycin and other aminoglycosides are contraindicated in pregnancy due to a well-documented risk of fetal ototoxicity/congenital deafness; isoniazid, rifampin, and ethambutol are generally considered safe.

An HIV-positive patient on a protease inhibitor is diagnosed with active TB. What is the central pharmacologic concern in choosing the rifamycin component of therapy?

Rifampin's strong CYP450 induction risks subtherapeutic protease inhibitor levels; rifabutin's much weaker induction preserves antiretroviral efficacy while still treating TB, making it the preferred substitute in this setting.

12. Atypical Mycobacteria & Leprosy Drugs (Conceptual Overview)

Per Dr. Waller's explicit instruction, the atypical mycobacteria treatment table is not something to memorize — know that it exists as a distinct category and recognize the drugs by name.

Atypical (nontuberculous) mycobacteria

Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC) and related species cause disease distinct from M. tuberculosis (often in structural lung disease or advanced immunosuppression, e.g., low-CD4 HIV). Treatment uses combination regimens built around a macrolide (azithromycinAdditional or clarithromycinAdditional) plus ethambutolCore, sometimes with rifabutinAdditional added — conceptually similar “combine several active agents” logic as TB itself, just with a different core backbone.

A patient with structural lung disease develops pulmonary infection with Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC). Which drug class forms the backbone of typical combination therapy, generally paired with ethambutol?

MAC disease is typically treated with a macrolide (azithromycin or clarithromycin) combined with ethambutol, sometimes with rifabutin added — a distinct regimen from standard M. tuberculosis therapy.

Clofazimine characteristically causes reddish-brown discoloration of the skin and body fluids — an expected effect of the drug rather than a sign of toxicity requiring discontinuation.

14. High-Yield Summary — Core Drugs Only

Only the seven drugs Dr. Waller highlighted in red font: the core four plus the rifabutin/rifapentine/moxifloxacin trio. Everything reviewed above (streptomycin, second-line agents, atypical mycobacteria/leprosy drugs) is fair game as "additional" but master this list first.

High-yield recall — core (red-font) drugs only
DrugOne-line role
IsoniazidCore first-line agent #1; mycolic acid synthesis inhibitor; hepatotoxicity + peripheral neuropathy (give B6); NAT2 acetylator phenotype matters.
RifampinCore first-line agent #2; rpoB/RNA polymerase inhibitor; strongest CYP450 inducer; orange discoloration.
PyrazinamideCore first-line agent #3; targets acidic/intracellular population; intensive phase only; most hepatotoxic first-line agent.
