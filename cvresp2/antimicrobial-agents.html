1. General Principles & Goals of Antimicrobial Therapy

Antimicrobials are classified first by the type of organism they target — antibacterial/antibiotic (bacteria), antiviral (viruses), antifungal (fungi), antiparasitic (parasites) — and then further by the class/spectrum of organism killed, the biochemical pathway interrupted, and chemical structure.

Dr. Waller opens with a blunt warning: antimicrobials are vastly overprescribed, and this overuse is actively driving resistance at a time when therapeutic options are becoming limited. Before prescribing, ask:

  • Is an antimicrobial indicated based on clinical findings?
  • Have appropriate cultures been obtained?
  • What is the likely causative organism?
  • What must be done to prevent secondary exposure?
  • Is there clinical evidence or an established guideline supporting benefit?

Once the pathogen is known, keep re-asking whether therapy is optimized: could a narrower-spectrum agent work? Is monotherapy sufficient, or is combination therapy needed? Is the dose/route/duration optimized? Have the right tests (e.g., susceptibility) been run? Are adjunctive measures (e.g., debridement) also needed?

Definitive therapy = de-escalation
The single most common exam trap here is failing to recognize that “definitive” therapy usually means narrowing, not escalating — you already know the bug, so you streamline to the most targeted, narrowest-spectrum agent that covers it.
Check yourself — 3 questions

A hospitalized patient has known contact with a roommate newly diagnosed with meningococcal meningitis but has no symptoms and no confirmed infection. Antibiotics are started immediately to prevent disease. What type of antimicrobial therapy is this?

Prophylactic therapy is given before any infection is present, to prevent infection or dangerous disease in an at-risk contact — exactly the meningococcal post-exposure scenario described.

A patient with community-acquired pneumonia is started on antibiotics targeting the most likely causative organisms before sputum culture results return. Once the culture identifies a fully susceptible organism, therapy is narrowed to the most targeted agent. This sequence illustrates which two types of therapy, in order?

Treating a symptomatic patient before the organism is confirmed is empiric therapy; narrowing to the most targeted agent once the pathogen and susceptibility are known is definitive therapy.

A patient has a chronically infected orthopedic implant that cannot be surgically removed due to comorbidities. The infection cannot be fully eradicated, so the patient is kept on a lower, ongoing dose of antibiotics indefinitely to control it. This is an example of which type of therapy?

Suppressive therapy continues lower-dose antimicrobial treatment when infection has not been completely eradicated and an anatomic or immunologic defect leading to the original infection cannot be removed.

2. Susceptibility Testing, Spectrum & Killing Kinetics

Susceptibility testing

The single most valuable, time-tested method for immediate bacterial identification is the Gram stain. Once an organism is identified, susceptibility must be confirmed with the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) — the lowest drug concentration that inhibits visible growth. CLSI (Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute) breakpoints translate an MIC or zone diameter into an interpretation:

S/I/R interpretation
CategoryMeaning
Susceptible (S)Isolate is inhibited by the usually achievable concentration of drug at the recommended dose for that infection site
Intermediate (I)MIC approaches usually attainable blood/tissue levels; response rates may be lower
Resistant (R)Isolate is not inhibited by usually achievable concentrations and/or clinical efficacy has not been reliably shown

How MIC is actually measured

  • Dilution tests (broth dilution): serial dilutions of antibiotic in broth; after 18–24h, growth is measured (can be automated via optical density) — yields an actual MIC value.
  • Disk diffusion: antibiotic disks on agar; zone of inhibition measured after 18–24h; standardized zone sizes distinguish susceptible from resistant — qualitative only, no MIC.
  • Gradient diffusion (E-test): a strip with a continuous concentration gradient produces an elliptical zone whose edge gives the MIC directly.

Antibacterial spectrum

  • Narrow-spectrum: acts on a single or limited group of organisms.
  • Extended-spectrum: active against gram-positive bacteria but also a significant number of gram-negative organisms.
  • Broad-spectrum: active against a wide variety of species, both gram-positive and gram-negative.

Bacteriostatic vs. bactericidal, and killing kinetics

Static vs. cidal, and concentration- vs. time-dependent killing
PropertyDefinitionExamples
BacteriostaticArrests growth/replication (limits spread); generally protein synthesis inhibitorsTetracyclines, macrolides, sulfonamides
BactericidalActually kills bacteriaβ-lactams, vancomycin, fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides
Concentration-dependent killingRate & extent of killing increase as concentration increases — dose high, dose infrequentlyAminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones
Time-dependent killingActivity continues only while serum concentration remains above the minimum bactericidal concentration — keep levels above threshold as long as possibleβ-lactams, vancomycin

Antibacterial targets & map of drug classes

  • Cell wall synthesis (peptidoglycan) — penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems, monobactams, vancomycin
  • Protein synthesis (30S and 50S ribosomal subunits) — aminoglycosides, tetracyclines, macrolides, lincosamides, oxazolidinones, pleuromutilins
  • Nucleic acid metabolism / topoisomerase function — fluoroquinolones
  • Folate synthesis — sulfonamides, trimethoprim
Check yourself — 3 questions

A broth microdilution assay is performed on a bacterial isolate, and growth is inhibited at the 32 mcg/mL well but visible at 16 mcg/mL. What does 32 mcg/mL represent?

The MIC is defined as the lowest concentration of drug that inhibits visible bacterial growth — here, 32 mcg/mL is the lowest well without growth.

A gradient diffusion (E-test) strip is placed on an agar plate, producing an elliptical zone of inhibition whose edge crosses the strip at a specific printed concentration. What advantage does this method have over standard disk diffusion?

Unlike disk diffusion, which gives only a qualitative susceptible/resistant interpretation, the continuous concentration gradient on an E-test strip allows a specific MIC value to be read directly from where the ellipse crosses the strip.

An antibiotic's killing effect depends primarily on keeping the serum concentration above the minimum bactericidal concentration for as much of the dosing interval as possible, with little added benefit from higher peak concentrations. Which killing pattern does this describe, and which drug class is a classic example?

Time-dependent killing means bactericidal activity depends on the duration serum levels exceed the minimum bactericidal concentration, not on peak concentration — the classic examples are beta-lactams and vancomycin.

3. Beta-Lactams: The Penicillins

Beta-lactams (penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems, monobactams, and β-lactamase inhibitors) all share a four-membered lactam ring and inhibit the transpeptidation reaction — the last step of peptidoglycan synthesis. Peptidoglycan is built from alternating N-acetylglucosamine and N-acetylmuramic acid sugars, cross-linked via a five-amino-acid peptide that terminates in D-alanyl-D-alanine. Penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs) normally remove the terminal D-alanine while forming this cross-link; β-lactams are structural analogs of D-Ala-D-Ala and covalently bind the PBP active site, blocking cross-linking and ultimately causing cell autolysis.

Resistance arises from altered PBP structure/affinity, inability of the drug to reach its target (especially in gram-negatives), active efflux, and β-lactamase enzymes that destroy the drug.

Classification (by spectrum)

  • Penicillin G, penicillin VAdditional — natural penicillins, narrow-spectrum, effective against gram-positive cocci but easily hydrolyzed by penicillinase. Penicillin G reaches CSF when meninges are inflamed; t½ 30 min, 90% renally eliminated. Use: Streptococcus pneumoniae pneumonia/meningitis (pen G); S. pyogenes pharyngitis, viridans strep endocarditis, syphilis (pen V) — no alternative in pregnancy, so allergic patients must be desensitized.
  • Oxacillin, dicloxacillin, nafcillinAdditional — anti-staphylococcal, penicillinase-resistant; first choice for methicillin-susceptible S. aureus/S. epidermidis. Nafcillin achieves adequate CSF concentration for staphylococcal meningitis.
  • AmpicillinCore / AmoxicillinCore — aminopenicillins, extended-spectrum: retain gram-positive activity and extend to gram-negatives (H. influenzae, E. coli, Proteus mirabilis), Listeria monocytogenes, susceptible meningococci, enterococci, anaerobes. Use: upper respiratory infections, sinusitis, otitis media, enterococcal infections.
  • Ampicillin-sulbactamCore / Amoxicillin-clavulanic acidCore — aminopenicillin + β-lactamase inhibitor combination, extending coverage to β-lactamase–producing organisms.
  • TicarcillinAdditional / PiperacillinCore (± piperacillin-tazobactamCore) — antipseudomonal penicillins, broad-spectrum: extend coverage to Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterobacter, Proteus. Piperacillin is superior for Pseudomonas; piperacillin-tazobactam is often given as an extended infusion to maximize its time-dependent killing, and reaches high biliary concentrations. Use: serious gram-negative infections, hospital-acquired pneumonia, immunocompromised patients, bacteremia, burns, UTI.
The exam-emphasized aminopenicillins/antipseudomonal agents
Dr. Waller highlights ampicillin, amoxicillin, and their beta-lactamase-inhibitor combinations, plus piperacillin-tazobactam, as the prototype penicillins to know cold — these are the ones you'll see driving CAP/HAP/VAP guideline choices.

Adverse effects (class-wide)

Generally well tolerated; most serious reactions relate to hypersensitivity — allergic reactions (0.7–10%), anaphylaxis (0.004–0.04%), rare interstitial nephritis; high oral doses can cause nausea, vomiting, mild-to-severe diarrhea, and pseudomembranous colitis.

Check yourself — 3 questions

A patient with a penicillin allergy is diagnosed with primary syphilis and is pregnant. Despite the allergy history, what is the recommended course of action given the information in this lecture?

Penicillin (penicillin V/G) has no acceptable alternative for treating syphilis in pregnancy, so an allergic pregnant patient should be desensitized and treated with penicillin rather than switched to another class.

A patient with a hospital-acquired pneumonia due to suspected Pseudomonas aeruginosa is started on an extended-infusion beta-lactam to maximize the drug's time-dependent bactericidal activity. Which antipseudomonal penicillin, often combined with a beta-lactamase inhibitor, is the prototype used this way?

Piperacillin, usually combined with tazobactam, is the antipseudomonal penicillin highlighted for hospital-acquired/ventilator-associated pneumonia and is often given as an extended infusion to maximize time-dependent killing.

A patient with known methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia needs a narrow-spectrum, penicillinase-resistant penicillin for definitive therapy. Which of the following is an appropriate agent for this indication?

Nafcillin is an anti-staphylococcal (penicillinase-resistant) penicillin and remains a first-choice, narrow-spectrum agent for methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus.

4. Cephalosporins, Carbapenems, Monobactam & β-Lactamase Inhibitors

Cephalosporins

Same MOA/resistance mechanisms as penicillins (PBP binding, transpeptidation inhibition). None of the cephalosporins cover MRSA, Listeria, or enterococci (exception: ceftaroline has some MRSA and enterococcal activity). ~1% cross-reactivity risk with penicillin allergy (patients with true anaphylaxis to penicillin should avoid 1st/2nd generation).

  • 1st generation cefazolin, cephalexinAdditional — good gram-positive, modest gram-negative (Moraxella, E. coli, Klebsiella, P. mirabilis); oral anaerobes. Use: skin/soft-tissue infections, surgical prophylaxis.
  • 2nd generation cefoxitin, cefuroximeAdditional — increased gram-negative activity vs. 1st gen; subset (cefoxitin) covers Bacteroides fragilis. Use: mixed facultative/anaerobic infections (intra-abdominal, pelvic inflammatory disease, diabetic foot).
  • 3rd generation CeftriaxoneCore, ceftazidimeCore — less gram-positive, much more gram-negative activity (Klebsiella, Proteus, Providencia, Serratia, Haemophilus); ceftazidime uniquely covers Pseudomonas. Use: drug-of-choice for serious gram-negative infections; ceftriaxone is DOC for all forms of gonorrhea and severe Lyme disease, and for meningitis.
  • 4th generation CefepimeCore — extends beyond 3rd gen, good activity against P. aeruginosa, Enterobacteriaceae, S. aureus, S. pneumoniae; stable against hydrolysis by many β-lactamases; excellent CSF penetration. Use: empiric treatment of serious nosocomial infections.
  • 5th generation ceftarolineAdditional — unique coverage of MRSA. Use: SSTIs, CAP.
  • Ceftolozane-tazobactam, ceftazidime-avibactamAdditional — combined with β-lactamase inhibitors for resistant gram-negative infections (complicated intra-abdominal/UTI, HABP/VABP; avibactam combo an option for carbapenemase producers).
  • CefiderocolAdditional — siderophore cephalosporin, actively transported into the cell via iron transporters; potent activity against β-lactamase–producing Enterobacteriaceae, Pseudomonas, Acinetobacter baumannii.

Carbapenems

Agents: imipenem/cilastatinAdditional (cilastatin blocks renal dehydropeptidase that would otherwise destroy imipenem), meropenemCore, ertapenemCore. Broad spectrum — gram-positive (Streptococcus, enterococci, Staphylococcus, Listeria) plus excellent Enterobacteriaceae, Pseudomonas, Acinetobacter coverage; very resistant to hydrolysis until the emergence of KPC carbapenemase. Stenotrophomonas maltophilia is resistant; ertapenem has inferior Pseudomonas/Acinetobacter activity but its longer half-life allows once-daily dosing. Use: infections resistant to other available drugs — UTI, lower respiratory tract infection, intra-abdominal, gynecologic, SSTI, bone/joint. ADRs: nausea/vomiting (1–20%), seizures (1.5%), hypersensitivity.

Monobactam

AztreonamAdditional — gram-negative coverage only (Enterobacteriaceae, Pseudomonas, H. influenzae, gonococci), no gram-positive or anaerobic activity. Patients allergic to penicillins/cephalosporins generally tolerate aztreonam, making it a useful option for gram-negative infections in a β-lactam-allergic patient (use caution if documented severe reaction to ceftazidime, given structural similarity).

β-lactamase inhibitors

Clavulanic acid, sulbactam, tazobactam, avibactam, vaborbactamAdditional — potent inhibitors of many (not all) β-lactamases; very weak antibacterial action alone, but protect their companion β-lactam from hydrolysis and extend its spectrum. Always given as fixed combinations (e.g., piperacillin-tazobactam, amoxicillin-clavulanate, ampicillin-sulbactam).

Check yourself — 3 questions

A patient develops a Pseudomonas aeruginosa urinary tract infection. Which third-generation cephalosporin, unlike most other agents in its generation, provides reliable coverage against this organism?

Ceftazidime is the third-generation cephalosporin specifically noted to cover Pseudomonas aeruginosa, unlike ceftriaxone and the other third-generation agents.

A patient requires empiric therapy for a serious nosocomial infection and needs a beta-lactam that is stable against hydrolysis by many beta-lactamases, penetrates CSF well, and covers Pseudomonas, Enterobacteriaceae, S. aureus, and S. pneumoniae. Which fourth-generation cephalosporin fits this description?

Cefepime, the fourth-generation cephalosporin, is stable against hydrolysis by many beta-lactamases, has excellent CSF penetration, and provides broad gram-positive and gram-negative coverage — used empirically for serious nosocomial infections.

A patient with a documented history of anaphylaxis to penicillin needs treatment for a gram-negative Enterobacteriaceae infection and cannot receive a cephalosporin or carbapenem. Which beta-lactam class provides gram-negative-only coverage and is generally well tolerated even in penicillin-allergic patients?

Aztreonam, the only monobactam, covers gram-negative organisms exclusively and is generally tolerated by patients allergic to penicillins and cephalosporins, making it useful in this exact scenario.
Part 2 · Cell Wall Synthesis Inhibitors

5. Glycopeptides: Vancomycin

VancomycinCore inhibits cell wall synthesis by a mechanism distinct from the β-lactams: rather than binding a PBP, it binds with high affinity directly to the D-alanyl-D-alanine terminus of cell wall precursor units, sterically blocking the transglycosylation/cross-linking machinery. Its large size prevents penetration of the gram-negative outer membrane, so it has no gram-negative activity.

Resistance: alteration of the D-Ala-D-Ala target to D-Ala-D-lactate or D-Ala-D-serine, which binds glycopeptides poorly (this is the VRE/VRSA mechanism); intermediate resistance can also occur with abnormally thick cell walls or a small resistant subpopulation.

Spectrum: broad gram-positive coverage — S. aureus (including MRSA), S. epidermidis (including MRSE), streptococci, ampicillin-resistant enterococci, Bacillus, Corynebacterium spp., Actinomyces, Clostridium; all gram-negatives and mycobacteria are resistant.

PK: poorly absorbed orally (given IV, never IM); 90% eliminated by glomerular filtration, t½ ~6 hours, accumulates in renal failure (dose adjust). Appears in CSF when meninges inflamed, and in bile, pleural, pericardial, synovial, and ascitic fluid.

Therapeutic use: osteomyelitis, endocarditis, MRSA, streptococcal/enterococcal infections, CNS infections, bacteremia; given orally for Clostridium difficile colitis (the only indication for oral vancomycin, since it isn't absorbed systemically from the gut). When given IV, monitor serum trough levels at steady state (typically before the 4th dose): target trough 15–20 mcg/mL for endocarditis/osteomyelitis/MRSA pneumonia, 10–15 mcg/mL for SSTIs.

ADRs: macular skin rash, chills, fever; vancomycin infusion reaction (formerly called "red man syndrome") with rapid infusion — extreme flushing, tachycardia, hypotension — this is a direct, non-IgE-mediated mast cell/histamine effect, not a true allergy; ototoxicity; nephrotoxicity (up to 33% with initial trough >20 mcg/mL).

Vancomycin infusion reaction ≠ true allergy
The classic exam trap: the flushing/hypotension reaction to rapid vancomycin infusion is a direct toxic effect on mast cells causing histamine release — it is managed by slowing the infusion, not by labeling the patient penicillin/vancomycin-allergic.

Additional glycopeptides (mentioned briefly)

TelavancinAdditional (once-daily, complicated SSTI/HAP), dalbavancinAdditional and oritavancinAdditional (once-weekly, complicated SSTI) share the same core cell-wall-synthesis-inhibiting mechanism but may also disrupt cell membrane permeability/potential (telavancin, oritavancin) and inhibit RNA synthesis (oritavancin).

Check yourself — 3 questions

A patient receiving a rapid IV infusion of vancomycin develops sudden flushing of the face and torso, tachycardia, and mild hypotension. IgE-mediated skin testing is negative. What is the most appropriate immediate management?

The flushing/hypotension reaction with rapid vancomycin infusion (historically called "red man syndrome") is a direct, non-IgE-mediated toxic effect on mast cells causing histamine release, not true anaphylaxis — it is managed by slowing the infusion rate.

A patient with Clostridium difficile colitis is prescribed vancomycin by mouth rather than intravenously. Why is the oral route effective for this specific indication despite vancomycin's generally poor oral bioavailability?

Vancomycin is poorly absorbed after oral administration, so it stays within the GI lumen and achieves high local concentrations in the colon — ideal for treating C. difficile colitis, but useless for treating systemic infections by that route.

A vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecium isolate is found to have altered cell wall precursor units. Which specific structural change most directly explains its resistance to vancomycin?

Vancomycin resistance classically results from altering the D-alanyl-D-alanine target on cell wall precursor units to D-alanyl-D-lactate or D-alanyl-D-serine, which vancomycin binds much more weakly.
Part 3 · Nucleic Acid / DNA Synthesis Disruptors

6. DNA Disruptors: Fluoroquinolones

Agents: ciprofloxacinCore, levofloxacinCore, moxifloxacinAdditional. MOA: concentration-dependent, bactericidal; target bacterial DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV. DNA strands must be separated to permit replication or transcription; anything that separates strands introduces excessive positive supercoiling, and DNA gyrase is responsible for continuously relieving this by introducing negative supercoils. By inhibiting gyrase/topoisomerase IV, fluoroquinolones prevent proper DNA replication and transcription.

Resistance: mutation in the chromosomal genes encoding DNA gyrase or topoisomerase IV, or active transport (efflux) out of the cell.

Spectrum: broad gram-negative (E. coli, Salmonella, Shigella, Enterobacter, Campylobacter, Neisseria, and particularly P. aeruginosa), S. aureus (not MRSA), atypicals (mycoplasmas, chlamydiae), other intracellular pathogens (e.g., Legionella, mycobacteria), and limited Streptococcus coverage — except levofloxacin and moxifloxacin, the "respiratory fluoroquinolones," which do cover Streptococcus spp. (including S. pneumoniae).

PK: well absorbed, distributed widely (urine, kidney, prostate, lung, stool, bile, macrophages, neutrophils); divalent/trivalent cations impair absorption; cleared renally (dose-adjust in renal impairment) except moxifloxacin, which is hepatically metabolized.

Therapeutic use: UTI (more effective than TMP-SMX in this lecture's framing), prostatitis; sexually transmitted infections (chlamydia, though ceftriaxone is DOC for gonorrhea); traveler's diarrhea, shigellosis; bone/joint/SSTI; diabetic foot infections; neutropenic prophylaxis. Respiratory FQs (levofloxacin/moxifloxacin) cover S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, Moraxella, S. aureus, M. pneumoniae, C. pneumoniae, Legionella — making them CAP/HAP workhorses. Ciprofloxacin is the most active agent in the class against Pseudomonas.

ADRs: generally well tolerated; GI 3–17% (nausea, vomiting, abdominal discomfort); CNS 0.9–11% (headache, dizziness, delirium, rare hallucinations); rash; photosensitivity; Achilles tendon rupture; contraindicated under age 18 for this reason.

Ciprofloxacin vs. the respiratory fluoroquinolones
Know the split: ciprofloxacin is the Pseudomonas workhorse but has weak Streptococcus coverage, while levofloxacin and moxifloxacin sacrifice a little Pseudomonas potency for reliable pneumococcal coverage, making them the CAP/HAP choices instead of cipro.
Check yourself — 3 questions

A patient is treated for a Pseudomonas aeruginosa urinary tract infection with a fluoroquinolone. Which agent in this class is the most active against Pseudomonas, though it provides only limited coverage of Streptococcus species?

Ciprofloxacin is the fluoroquinolone with the most potent activity against Pseudomonas aeruginosa, but unlike the "respiratory fluoroquinolones" (levofloxacin, moxifloxacin), it provides only limited Streptococcus coverage.

A 16-year-old patient with a complicated UTI is being considered for fluoroquinolone therapy. Which well-known adverse effect specifically limits fluoroquinolone use in patients under 18?

Fluoroquinolones carry a risk of Achilles tendon rupture and tendinopathy, which is a key reason they are generally avoided in patients under 18 years old.

A patient with community-acquired pneumonia due to suspected Streptococcus pneumoniae and possible Legionella co-infection is started on a fluoroquinolone that reliably covers both. Which mechanism underlies this drug class's bactericidal activity?

Fluoroquinolones (here, a respiratory fluoroquinolone like levofloxacin or moxifloxacin) act by inhibiting bacterial DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV, enzymes required to manage DNA supercoiling during replication and transcription.
Part 3 · Nucleic Acid / DNA Synthesis Disruptors

7. DNA/Folate Disruptors: Sulfonamides & Trimethoprim

Sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprimCore (SMX-TMP, Bactrim) is formulated in a fixed 5:1 mg ratio, achieving roughly 20:1 serum concentrations because trimethoprim is 20–100× more potent — this ratio is designed to give a synergistic, sequential block of the same pathway.

MOA: sulfonamides are bacteriostatic, competitive inhibitors of dihydropteroate synthase, the enzyme that incorporates para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA) into dihydropteroic acid, the immediate precursor of folic acid — this blocks bacterial use of PABA for folate synthesis. Trimethoprim synergistically inhibits microbial dihydrofolate reductase, the enzyme that reduces dihydrofolate (DHF) to tetrahydrofolate (THF), the reduced folate form required for one-carbon transfer reactions (purine/pyrimidine synthesis). Given together, the two drugs introduce sequential blocks in the same folate pathway.

Resistance: lower affinity of the target enzymes for drug, decreased bacterial permeability, active efflux, an alternative pathway for the essential metabolite, or increased production of the essential metabolite (PABA) itself.

Spectrum: broad — Chlamydia diphtheriae, N. meningitidis, S. aureus, S. epidermidis, S. pyogenes, viridans streptococci, E. coli, Proteus spp., Enterobacter, Salmonella, Shigella, Serratia, Klebsiella, Brucella, Pasteurella, Yersinia pseudotuberculosis, Nocardia.

PK: trimethoprim achieves roughly 20:1 concentration relative to SMX; t½ of SMX ~10h, TMP ~11h; 25–50% of SMX and 60% of TMP excreted in urine within 24h.

Therapeutic use: UTI, bacterial prostatitis, bronchitis, shigellosis, traveler's diarrhea, Salmonella; Pneumocystis jirovecii (fungal) pneumonia prophylaxis in neutropenic/immunocompromised patients; Nocardia, Stenotrophomonas maltophilia infections. Do not use for Streptococcus pharyngitis.

ADRs: allergic skin rashes (including Stevens-Johnson syndrome), nausea, vomiting, CNS effects (headache, depression), photosensitivity, renal dysfunction. DDI: potentiates the effect of warfarin.

Sequential blockade, one pathway
Picture the folate pathway as PABA → dihydropteroic acid → dihydrofolate (DHF) → tetrahydrofolate (THF) → purines/pyrimidines. Sulfonamides block step 1 (dihydropteroate synthase); trimethoprim blocks step 3 (dihydrofolate reductase). Two blocks in one pathway is why the combination is synergistic rather than merely additive.
Check yourself — 3 questions

A patient is started on sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim for a urinary tract infection. Which enzyme does the sulfamethoxazole component inhibit?

Sulfonamides (sulfamethoxazole) competitively inhibit dihydropteroate synthase, blocking incorporation of PABA into dihydropteroic acid, the immediate precursor of folic acid.

Why are sulfamethoxazole and trimethoprim combined in a fixed 5:1 ratio rather than given as sulfamethoxazole alone?

Sulfamethoxazole blocks dihydropteroate synthase while trimethoprim blocks dihydrofolate reductase — two sequential steps in the same folate synthesis pathway — producing a synergistic combined block.

A patient on long-term warfarin therapy is prescribed sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim for a UTI. What is the most important drug interaction to monitor for?

Sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim is well known to potentiate the effects of warfarin, increasing anticoagulant effect and bleeding risk, so INR should be monitored closely when the combination is used.
Part 4 · Protein Synthesis Inhibitors

8. Protein Synthesis Inhibitors: Aminoglycosides

Bacterial ribosomes (70S = 30S + 50S subunits) differ structurally from human cytoplasmic ribosomes (80S = 40S + 60S), which is what allows selective bacterial toxicity for this entire drug family. During translation, the ribosome cycles tRNAs through the A (aminoacyl), P (peptidyl), and E (exit) sites as the growing peptide chain is translocated along the mRNA.

Agents: amikacinAdditional, tobramycinAdditional, gentamicinCore. MOA: concentration-dependent, bactericidal; bind the 30S ribosomal subunit and disrupt the normal cycle of ribosomal function by interfering with initiation of protein synthesis. This causes misreading of the mRNA template, so abnormal initiation complexes and aberrant proteins accumulate; these aberrant proteins insert into the cell membrane and alter its permeability. Aminoglycosides reach their site of action by diffusing through outer-membrane porin proteins (gram-negatives) and via electron-transport/oxygen-dependent movement across the cytoplasmic membrane — cell-wall-active drugs (β-lactams or vancomycin) enhance this uptake, producing a synergistic bactericidal effect. Aminoglycosides also exhibit a post-antibiotic effect (PAE): residual bactericidal activity persists even after the serum concentration falls below the MIC — the pharmacologic basis for extended-interval, once-daily-style dosing.

Resistance: aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes (adenylation, acetylation, phosphorylation), impaired drug transport into the cell, altered ribosome target.

Spectrum: aerobic gram-negative bacteria; limited gram-positive action alone, but produces synergistic bactericidal effects against gram-positives when combined with a cell-wall-active agent. Kanamycin (more limited; avoid for Serratia or P. aeruginosa); tobramycin more active against P. aeruginosa and some Proteus; gentamicin more active against Serratia.

PK: highly polar, <1% orally absorbed, volume of distribution approximates extracellular fluid volume, poor adipose penetration; excreted by glomerular filtration proportional to creatinine clearance. Low tissue concentrations everywhere except renal cortex, endolymph, and perilymph of the inner ear — the reservoir behind their signature nephro- and ototoxicity. Tobramycin can be given via inhalation in cystic fibrosis for high local sputum concentration with low serum levels.

Therapeutic use: UTI (complicated), infections resistant to other agents or in seriously ill patients, hospital-acquired pneumonia (ineffective against S. pneumoniae and anaerobes alone), peritonitis with peritoneal dialysis, synergy in bacterial endocarditis (combined with a cell-wall-active drug); tobramycin inhalation in CF; amikacin used when resistance to gentamicin/tobramycin is high.

ADRs: ototoxicity (as high as 25%), nephrotoxicity (8–26%), neuromuscular blockade and apnea.

Why synergy with cell-wall agents matters clinically
Because aminoglycosides need active transport across the cytoplasmic membrane to reach the 30S ribosome, and cell-wall-active drugs make the bacterial envelope leakier, combining a beta-lactam or vancomycin with an aminoglycoside is the classic strategy for treating enterococcal or staphylococcal endocarditis — neither drug reliably kills these organisms as well alone.
Check yourself — 3 questions

A patient with enterococcal endocarditis is treated with a combination of ampicillin and gentamicin, achieving a bactericidal effect neither drug reliably produces alone against this organism. What is the pharmacologic basis for this synergy?

Cell-wall-active drugs like ampicillin disrupt the bacterial envelope, enhancing aminoglycoside penetration to its 30S ribosomal target — this combination produces a synergistic bactericidal effect classically used for enterococcal or staphylococcal endocarditis.

A patient receiving gentamicin for a serious gram-negative infection is found to have persistent, above-MIC bactericidal activity in serum drawn after the measured concentration has already fallen below the MIC. What phenomenon explains this observation, and how does it influence dosing strategy?

Aminoglycosides exhibit a post-antibiotic effect — residual bactericidal activity persisting after serum levels fall below the MIC — which supports extended-interval (less frequent, higher-dose) dosing strategies.

A patient on gentamicin for two weeks develops elevated serum creatinine and new-onset difficulty with balance and hearing. Which two organ systems are classically most vulnerable to aminoglycoside toxicity, explaining this presentation?

Aminoglycosides concentrate in the renal cortex and in the endolymph/perilymph of the inner ear, which is why nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity (vestibular and cochlear) are their signature, dose-limiting adverse effects.
Part 4 · Protein Synthesis Inhibitors

9. Protein Synthesis Inhibitors: Tetracyclines & Glycylcyclines

Agents: DoxycyclineCore, minocyclineAdditional, tigecyclineAdditional (a glycylcycline). MOA: bacteriostatic; bind the 30S bacterial ribosome and prevent access of aminoacyl-tRNA to the acceptor (A) site on the mRNA-ribosome complex, preventing addition of amino acids to the growing peptide. Enter the outer membrane by passive diffusion through porin proteins, then cross the cytoplasmic membrane via active, energy-dependent transport.

Resistance: decreased influx, acquisition of an energy-dependent efflux pump, ribosomal protection proteins, enzymatic inactivation.

Spectrum: wide range, aerobic and anaerobic, gram-positive and gram-negative, plus classic coverage of intracellular/atypical organisms — Rickettsia, Coxiella burnetii, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydia spp., Legionella, atypical mycobacteria, Plasmodium, Borrelia burgdorferi (Lyme disease), Treponema pallidum (syphilis), Bacillus anthracis, L. monocytogenes, MRSA, H. influenzae, Helicobacter pylori. All strains of Pseudomonas are resistant. Tigecycline is equally or more active in vitro than the tetracyclines but does not cover Pseudomonas, Proteus, or Providencia; it has very broad activity including vancomycin-intermediate/resistant S. aureus and vancomycin-resistant enterococci.

PK: incomplete oral absorption on an empty stomach for tetracycline (60–80%); doxycycline (95%) and minocycline (90–100%) are much better absorbed. Absorption is impaired by concurrent divalent/trivalent cations (Ca2+, Mg2+, Al3+, Fe2+/3+, Zn2+) — classic teaching point: avoid dairy/antacids/iron near dosing. Distributes widely including CNS; concentrates in liver, spleen, bone marrow, and in bone/teeth of unerupted teeth; renal elimination but also concentrates in and is excreted via bile. Doxycycline/minocycline t½ 16–18h; no renal dose adjustment required for doxycycline.

Therapeutic use: CAP and atypical CAP coverage, community-acquired SSTIs and MRSA, acne, rickettsial infections (Rocky Mountain spotted fever), Q fever, anthrax, used in combination for H. pylori, plague, tularemia, brucellosis.

ADRs: GI (epigastric burning, discomfort, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea), C. difficile superinfection, photosensitivity, teeth discoloration/enamel dysplasia — avoid in pregnancy and young children, thrombophlebitis (tigecycline: also GI, and it carries a boxed warning for increased mortality vs. comparators).

Doxycycline's exam-favorite reason to memorize
Doxycycline is the go-to for tick-borne illness (Rocky Mountain spotted fever, Lyme disease) and atypical CAP pathogens (Mycoplasma, Chlamydia, Legionella) — a single drug covering both a classic zoonotic vignette and a classic pulmonary vignette.
Check yourself — 3 questions

A patient is diagnosed with Rocky Mountain spotted fever after a tick bite in a wooded area. Which antibiotic class, and specific agent, is first-line therapy for this rickettsial infection?

Doxycycline (a tetracycline) is the classic, first-line agent for rickettsial infections such as Rocky Mountain spotted fever, along with other tick-borne illnesses like Lyme disease.

A patient is instructed to avoid taking doxycycline with dairy products, antacids, or iron supplements. What is the pharmacologic reason for this instruction?

Tetracyclines, including doxycycline, chelate with divalent and trivalent cations (calcium, magnesium, aluminum, iron, zinc) found in dairy, antacids, and iron supplements, which significantly impairs their oral absorption.

A 6-year-old child is prescribed doxycycline for a presumed atypical pneumonia. What is the primary concern that should prompt reconsideration of this choice in a young child?

Tetracyclines, including doxycycline, are generally avoided in young children (and pregnancy) because they incorporate into developing bone and teeth, causing permanent tooth discoloration and enamel dysplasia.
Part 4 · Protein Synthesis Inhibitors

10. Protein Synthesis Inhibitors: Macrolides, Clindamycin, Linezolid & Lefamulin

Macrolides & ketolides

Agents: erythromycin, clarithromycinAdditional, azithromycinCore, telithromycinAdditional (ketolide). MOA: bacteriostatic; bind reversibly to the 50S ribosomal subunit and inhibit translocation, the step where the newly synthesized peptidyl-tRNA moves from the acceptor site to the peptidyl donor site on the ribosome.

Resistance: drug efflux, ribosomal protection proteins, hydrolysis, ribosomal mutations.

Spectrum: aerobic gram-positive cocci and bacilli (Clostridium perfringens, Corynebacterium diphtheriae, L. monocytogenes); Staphylococcus not reliably susceptible; inactive against most gram-negatives, with modest activity against H. influenzae, N. meningitidis, N. gonorrhoeae, Pasteurella, Bordetella pertussis, and atypicals (Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Legionella, Chlamydophila).

PK: azithromycin is absorbed rapidly with wide tissue distribution (except CNS), high intracellular concentration, hepatic metabolism to inactive metabolites, biliary excretion, t½ 40–68 hours (allowing short courses/once-daily dosing). Clarithromycin undergoes first-pass metabolism (bioavailability 50–55%) to an active metabolite; renal elimination, dose-adjust if CrCl <30.

Therapeutic use: respiratory tract infections (covers S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, and atypicals — Mycoplasma, Chlamydophila, Legionella), alternative for otitis media/sinusitis/bronchitis/SSTIs, pertussis, gastroenteritis, H. pylori regimens, mycobacterial infections.

ADRs: GI (epigastric distress), hepatotoxicity, arrhythmia/QT prolongation. DDI: CYP3A4 inhibition prolongs effects of digoxin, valproate, warfarin, and others (azithromycin's structure makes it less likely to cause these interactions, but caution is still advised).

Lincosamide: clindamycin

ClindamycinCore binds exclusively to the 50S subunit and suppresses protein synthesis by inhibiting aminoacyl translocation — its binding site is identical to that of erythromycin (macrolides), so the two classes can be cross-resistant. Resistance: ribosomal methylation. Spectrum: pneumococci, S. pyogenes, viridans streptococci, MSSA, anaerobes (B. fragilis); all aerobic gram-negative bacilli are resistant. PK: t½ 2.9h, 90% protein bound, accumulates in PMNs/alveolar macrophages/abscesses, inactivated hepatically (dose adjust in hepatic failure). Use: SSTIs, necrotizing SSTIs, penetrating wounds, lung abscesses, anaerobic lung/pleural infections, topical for acne. ADRs: GI diarrhea (2–20%), pseudomembranous colitis (0.01–10%, classically associated with clindamycin), skin rash (10%), may potentiate neuromuscular blockade.

Oxazolidinone: linezolid

LinezolidCore inhibits protein synthesis by binding the P site of the 50S subunit, preventing formation of the larger ribosomal fMet-tRNA initiation complex — a distinct step from the macrolides/clindamycin. Resistance: ribosomal mutation. Spectrum: gram-positive — Staphylococcus (MSSA, MRSA, VRSA), Streptococcus (including penicillin-resistant S. pneumoniae), enterococci (including VRE), gram-positive anaerobic cocci, gram-positive rods; poor gram-negative activity. Bacteriostatic against enterococci/staphylococci, bactericidal against streptococci. PK: well absorbed orally, 100% bioavailability, t½ 4–6h. Use: VRE faecium, MSSA/MRSA nosocomial pneumonia, CAP, complicated SSTI — but bacteriostatic against enterococci/staphylococci, so should not be first-line for suspected endocarditis, and should be reserved for multi-drug-resistant organisms rather than used when other agents would work. ADRs: myelosuppression (thrombocytopenia 2.4%, anemia, leukopenia), minor GI, headache. DDI: weak, nonspecific MAO inhibitor — concomitant serotonergic drugs (SSRIs) risk serotonin syndrome; short courses (10–14 days) with monitoring are generally reasonable.

Pleuromutilin: lefamulin

LefamulinCore binds the 50S subunit, causing the binding pocket to close around the drug, which in turn prevents bacterial tRNA from binding appropriately. Low resistance risk so far (possible ribosomal alteration, active efflux). Spectrum: bactericidal against lower respiratory tract pathogens — S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, atypicals (L. pneumophila, M. pneumoniae, C. pneumoniae); also aerobic gram-positive (S. pyogenes, S. aureus, E. faecium) and some STI organisms (M. genitalium, N. gonorrhoeae, C. trachomatis). PK: 95–97% protein bound, hepatic (CYP3A4) metabolism, t½ 8h. Use: community-acquired pneumonia (CAP). ADRs: infusion-site reactions (IV), GI nausea/diarrhea (oral); avoid in pregnancy (possible congenital malformations).

Same ribosomal subunit, four different jobs
All four of macrolides, clindamycin, linezolid, and lefamulin bind the 50S subunit, but at different functional steps: macrolides/clindamycin block translocation (and share a binding site, hence cross-resistance); linezolid blocks formation of the initiation complex; lefamulin closes the pocket around itself to block tRNA binding. Knowing which step each blocks is a common way exams distinguish them.
Check yourself — 3 questions

A patient is treated empirically for community-acquired pneumonia with azithromycin, chosen partly for its long half-life of 40 to 68 hours that permits a short treatment course. What ribosomal subunit and functional step does azithromycin target?

Macrolides like azithromycin bind reversibly to the 50S ribosomal subunit and inhibit translocation, the step where peptidyl-tRNA moves from the acceptor to the donor site.

A patient develops profuse watery diarrhea and is diagnosed with pseudomembranous colitis several days after starting an antibiotic for an anaerobic lung abscess. Which antibiotic is classically most strongly associated with this complication?

Clindamycin is the antibiotic classically most strongly associated with Clostridioides difficile-associated pseudomembranous colitis, occurring in up to 10% of patients.

A patient with vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecium bacteremia is being considered for linezolid therapy, but the care team is concerned because linezolid is only bacteriostatic (not bactericidal) against enterococci. In which condition should linezolid specifically be avoided as first-line therapy for this reason?

Because linezolid is bacteriostatic rather than bactericidal against enterococci and staphylococci, it should not be used as first-line therapy for suspected endocarditis, where bactericidal activity is generally preferred.
Part 4 · Wrap-Up

11. Bacterial Resistance — Mechanisms Summary

Two broad factors drive the development of antimicrobial resistance: evolution (selection pressure favoring resistant clones) and clinical/environmental practices (overprescribing, incomplete courses, agricultural use). Across every drug class covered in this lecture, resistance mechanisms fall into a short list of recurring themes:

Four (or six) categories of resistance mechanism, with recurring examples
CategoryDescriptionExamples from this lecture
Reduced entryDecreased permeability/influx of drug into the pathogenGram-negative outer membrane blocking vancomycin; decreased porin expression affecting beta-lactams, tetracyclines
Enhanced effluxActive efflux pumps export the drug out of the cell before it actsFluoroquinolones, tetracyclines, macrolides
Enzymatic inactivationMicrobial enzymes chemically destroy or modify the drugβ-lactamases (penicillins/cephalosporins), carbapenemases (KPC), aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes (adenylation/acetylation/phosphorylation)
Altered targetThe drug's binding site is structurally changed so it no longer binds effectivelyAltered PBPs (MRSA, penicillin-resistant pneumococci); D-Ala-D-Lac/D-Ser instead of D-Ala-D-Ala (VRE/VRSA); mutated DNA gyrase/topoisomerase IV (fluoroquinolones); ribosomal mutation/methylation (macrolides, clindamycin, linezolid)
Alternative pathwayThe organism develops or upregulates a bypass pathway not inhibited by the drugSulfonamide/trimethoprim resistance via an alternate folate-pathway enzyme
Increased target/substrate productionOverproduction of the natural substrate overwhelms competitive inhibitionIncreased PABA production overcoming sulfonamide blockade
The recurring exam pattern
Whenever a vignette describes a specific molecular change (an altered penicillin-binding protein, a modified D-Ala-D-Ala terminus, a gyrase mutation, a new efflux pump, a beta-lactamase), first sort it into one of these mechanism buckets, then match the bucket to the drug class being asked about — the mechanism categories repeat across essentially every drug class in this lecture.
Check yourself — 3 questions

A gram-negative organism acquires a plasmid encoding an enzyme that hydrolyzes the beta-lactam ring of ampicillin before it can reach its target. Which broad category of resistance mechanism does this represent?

Beta-lactamase production, which chemically destroys the drug before it can act, is a classic example of enzymatic inactivation as a resistance mechanism.

Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) expresses an altered penicillin-binding protein (PBP2a) with markedly reduced affinity for beta-lactam antibiotics. Which resistance mechanism category does this illustrate?

An altered PBP with reduced drug-binding affinity is a structurally changed target — the altered target category of resistance, as seen classically in MRSA.

A Enterococcus faecium isolate becomes resistant to vancomycin by replacing the normal D-alanyl-D-alanine terminus of its cell wall precursors with D-alanyl-D-lactate, which vancomycin binds poorly. Which resistance mechanism category best classifies this change?

Replacing the D-alanyl-D-alanine binding target with D-alanyl-D-lactate is a structural alteration of the drug's molecular target, classifying this as an altered-target resistance mechanism.
