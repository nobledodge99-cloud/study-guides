What Is Pulmonary Embolism?

1. Definition & epidemiology

Pulmonary embolism (PE) is an obstruction within the pulmonary arteries, usually due to a large blood clot (thrombus) that forms elsewhere in the body and travels (embolizes) to the lung. PE rarely results from other embolized material (fat, air, tumor, amniotic fluid, septic emboli).

Epidemiologic factValue
Incidence of acute PE~39-115 per 100,000 persons annually (often cited as ~100/100,000)
Rank among cardiovascular diseases3rd most common, after coronary artery disease and stroke
Sex predilectionHigher incidence in males than females
US annual deaths from DVT/PE60,000-100,000 (CDC)
RecurrenceOne-third of survivors have another VTE event within 10 years
Mortality, untreated vs. treated~30% untreated; ~8% with timely therapy
Clinical pearlPE and DVT together comprise venous thromboembolism (VTE). Most PEs originate as lower-extremity DVTs, so PE risk factors are the same as DVT risk factors.

2. Risk factors & etiology

The Virchow triad — hypercoagulability, venous stasis, and endothelial injury — explains most VTE/PE risk factors.

CategoryExamples
Genetic (thrombophilia)Factor V Leiden mutation, prothrombin gene mutation, protein C deficiency, protein S deficiency, hyperhomocysteinemia
AcquiredImmobilization >3 days, travel >4 hours, recent orthopedic surgery, malignancy, indwelling venous catheter, obesity, pregnancy/postpartum, smoking, oral contraceptives/hormone replacement therapy
Other predisposing factorsLower-limb fracture, hospitalization for heart failure or AFib/flutter (within 3 months), hip/knee replacement, major trauma, prior VTE, central venous lines, chemotherapy, CHF/respiratory failure, infection (pneumonia, UTI, HIV)

Malignancy and VTE risk

Cancer carries a high risk for thrombus formation. Pancreatic cancer, hematologic malignancies, lung cancer, gastric cancer, and brain cancer carry the highest VTE risk, with metastatic disease conferring the highest risk of all.

Site of origin

The most common site of the originating thrombus is the deep veins of the lower extremities (popliteal, femoral, iliac veins) — deep vein thrombosis (DVT). Upper-extremity thrombosis is less common, usually following venous catheterization or procedures. Compared with DVT, other causes of PE (fat, air, septic, tumor, amniotic fluid emboli) are rare.

3. Clinical presentation

PE may be asymptomatic or cause symptoms ranging from mild discomfort to cardiovascular collapse, depending on the severity of pulmonary arterial obstruction. Since symptoms are so nonspecific, PE should always be suspected in patients with unexplained tachycardia or respiratory complaints — clinicians must also weigh VTE risk factors, not symptoms alone.

4. Pathophysiology

Hypoxemia: increased dead space ventilation

An embolus within a pulmonary artery obstructs blood flow to alveoli that remain normally ventilated. The inhaled air reaching these alveoli is functionally useless because no blood passes by to exchange gas — this is increased dead space ventilation, and it results in hypoxemia.

Compensation and its effect on pH

The body compensates for hypoxemia by increasing minute ventilation — breathing faster (tachypnea) and often deeper (increased tidal volume), producing dyspnea. This hyperventilation causes hypocarbia and respiratory alkalosis initially. Hypercarbia, common in other hypoxemic lung diseases, is notably uncommon in PE — likely because of persistent hyperventilation. (In massive PE with obstructive shock/respiratory arrest, respiratory or lactic acidosis can occur instead.)

Hemodynamic complications

ComplicationMechanism
Decreased cardiac output / obstructive shockPulmonary vascular resistance rises from mechanical obstruction plus hypoxic vasoconstriction → increased RV afterload → impaired RV outflow, RV dilation, septal bowing into the LV → reduced LV preload → reduced LV stroke volume/cardiac output → systemic hypotension
Right heart failureA failing RV cannot pump forward → blood backs up into the RA/systemic veins → exercise intolerance, distended neck veins, ascites, hepatomegaly, lower-extremity edema. Acute RV failure from pressure overload is the primary cause of death in severe PE.
Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH)1%-5% of PE patients develop persistent thrombi causing pulmonary artery hypertension, possibly from an underlying hypercoagulable state; high risk for right heart failure

Pulmonary infarction

Large emboli obstruct the main pulmonary artery, causing a saddle embolus with severe cardiovascular consequences. Smaller emboli block peripheral arteries and can cause pulmonary infarction (intra-alveolar hemorrhage). Because the lung parenchyma receives oxygen from three sources (pulmonary arterial blood, bronchial arterial blood, and direct alveolar diffusion), infarction requires impedance of more than one source. Pulmonary infarction shows a strong predilection for the lower lobes (especially the right lower lobe), thought to relate to gravity's effect on alveolar/pulmonary/bronchial arterial pressure relationships.

Check yourself

Exam-style question
The hypoxemia of PE is classically described as resulting from which mechanism?
ADecreased FiO2 from breathing at altitude
BPrimary alveolar hypoventilation from respiratory center depression
CRight-to-left cardiac shunting through a patent foramen ovale in all patients
DDecreased minute ventilation from respiratory muscle fatigue

Explanation: PE is a classic example of increased dead space ventilation: an embolus obstructs blood flow to a region of otherwise normally ventilated alveoli. Air still reaches those alveoli, but with no blood flow to pick up oxygen or drop off CO2, that ventilation is functionally wasted — producing hypoxemia despite normal or even increased overall ventilation.
Exam-style question
How does the body initially compensate for the hypoxemia caused by PE, and what effect does this have on blood pH?
AIt hypoventilates to conserve energy, causing hypercapnia and respiratory acidosis
BIt increases minute ventilation (tachypnea, increased tidal volume), producing hypocarbia and respiratory alkalosis
CIt shunts blood away from the lungs entirely, normalizing pH
DIt retains bicarbonate renally within minutes, producing metabolic alkalosis
EThere is no compensatory response to PE-related hypoxemia
Explanation: The body compensates for PE-related hypoxemia by increasing total minute ventilation — breathing faster (tachypnea) and often deeper (increased tidal volume), which is experienced as dyspnea. This hyperventilation lowers PaCO2 (hypocarbia) and raises blood pH (respiratory alkalosis). Hypercarbia is notably uncommon in PE, likely because of this persistent compensatory hyperventilation.
Exam-style question
How does an obstructing pulmonary embolus lead to reduced cardiac output and systemic hypotension?
AIt directly damages the left ventricular myocardium
BIt causes a reflex bradycardia that reduces cardiac output
CIt increases left atrial pressure directly, causing pulmonary edema
DIncreased pulmonary vascular resistance raises right ventricular afterload, impairing right ventricular outflow and RV dilation, which in turn reduces left ventricular preload and stroke volume
EIt has no hemodynamic effect unless the patient is already hypovolemic
Explanation: Pulmonary vascular resistance rises from the mechanical obstruction plus hypoxic vasoconstriction, increasing right ventricular afterload. This impedes RV outflow, causing RV dilation and interventricular septal bowing into the LV; the resulting reduction in LV preload (filling) reduces LV stroke volume and cardiac output — producing systemic hypotension and, in severe cases, obstructive shock. Acute RV failure from this pressure overload is the primary cause of death in severe PE.
Exam-style question
Pulmonary infarction (tissue necrosis from PE) shows a strong predilection for which location, and why?
AThe lower lobes, thought to be due to gravity's effect on the relationship between alveolar, pulmonary, and bronchial arterial pressures
BThe upper lobes, due to their greater blood flow at rest
CThe right middle lobe exclusively, due to its unique bronchial anatomy
DEqually distributed with no lobar predilection
EThe apex of the lung, mirroring the distribution of reactivated tuberculosis
Explanation: Pulmonary infarction shows a strong predilection for the lower lobes (particularly the right lower lobe), thought to relate to gravity's influence on the pressure relationships between alveolar, pulmonary arterial, and bronchial arterial blood flow. The lung parenchyma normally receives oxygen from three sources (deoxygenated pulmonary arterial blood, oxygenated bronchial arterial blood, and direct alveolar diffusion), so infarction requires a sufficient insult to more than one of these routes.

How Is PE Diagnosed?

5. Risk-stratification tools

Not all patients with chest pain or dyspnea warrant a full PE evaluation. Risk-stratification tools quantify a patient's PE risk and the need for further testing.

Modified Wells score

Clinical characteristicPoints
Clinical signs/symptoms of DVT3.0
PE is the most likely diagnosis (or equally likely)3.0
Heart rate >100/min1.5
Immobilization or surgery in the past 4 weeks1.5
Previous DVT or PE1.5
Hemoptysis1.0
Malignancy (treated within 6 months, or palliative)1.0

Modified/2-level Wells: ≤4 → PE unlikely (<3% risk); >4 → PE likely, further testing needed. Traditional 3-level Wells: low <2, moderate 2-6, high >6.

Revised Geneva score

An alternative clinical prediction rule using similar criteria (previous PE/DVT, heart rate, recent surgery/fracture, hemoptysis, active cancer, unilateral leg pain, pain on deep palpation with unilateral edema, age >65). Three-level score: low, intermediate, high; two-level score: PE unlikely vs. PE likely.

PERC rule (PE rule-out criteria)

For patients with a low pretest PE risk, PERC identifies who can be discharged without further testing (not even a D-dimer) if all 8 criteria are met (probability of PE then <2%) — the absence of:

  • Age ≥50 years
  • Heart rate ≥100/min
  • Oxygen saturation on room air <95%
  • Hemoptysis
  • Exogenous estrogen use
  • Prior DVT or PE
  • Unilateral leg swelling
  • Recent surgery/trauma requiring hospitalization (within 4 weeks)
Clinical pearlPERC is only valid in settings with a low PE prevalence (<15%) — do not apply it to patients with intermediate or high clinical suspicion of PE.

Exam-style question
Using the modified Wells score, a PE is considered unlikely (with a probability of less than 3%) below what total score, and what score prompts further testing?
AUnlikely below 10; testing needed above 10

Explanation: In the modified (2-level) Wells score, a total of 4 or less classifies PE as unlikely (<3% risk), while a score above 4 classifies PE as likely and warrants further testing. BThe revised Geneva score alone
CThe CHA2DS2-VASc score

Exam-style question
In the diagnostic approach to a hemodynamically stable patient with a low-to-intermediate clinical probability of PE (Wells score <2 to 6) and a negative D-dimer, what is the next step?
AProceed directly to CT pulmonary angiography regardless of the D-dimer result
BPE can be excluded; no further imaging is required
CProceed to pulmonary angiography as the gold standard
DRepeat the D-dimer test in 24 hours
EObtain a V/Q scan regardless of D-dimer result
Explanation: A normal (negative) D-dimer has a high negative predictive value; in a patient with low or intermediate pretest probability, a negative D-dimer is sufficient to exclude PE without further imaging. If the D-dimer is positive, or if pretest probability is high, CT pulmonary angiography (or a V/Q scan if CTPA is contraindicated/inconclusive) is the appropriate next step.
Exam-style question
Why are age-adjusted D-dimer cutoffs sometimes used in older patients?
AD-dimer sensitivity increases with age, requiring a lower cutoff
BAge-adjusted cutoffs are used only in pediatric patients
CD-dimer testing is not recommended at any age
DD-dimer specificity decreases with age, so a fixed cutoff (eg, 500 ng/mL) leads to more false positives in older adults; an age-adjusted cutoff (age in years x 10 ng/mL for patients over 50) improves the test's ability to safely rule out PE without more false negatives
EThe age-adjusted cutoff replaces the need for any imaging in patients over 65
Explanation: D-dimer specificity falls substantially with age (to roughly 10% by age 80), because many age-related conditions elevate D-dimer independent of thrombosis. An age-adjusted cutoff (age x 10 ng/mL for patients over 50 — e.g., 750 ng/mL for a 75-year-old) increases the proportion of patients in whom PE can be safely excluded without additional imaging, without increasing false-negative results.

8. CT angiography & imaging selection

CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA) is the diagnostic imaging test of choice for PE. Contrast dye is injected into an arm vein and the pulmonary arteries are examined for filling defects, visualized down to the subsegmental level.

PIOPED II findingValue
Sensitivity83%
Specificity96%
Negative predictive value, low/intermediate pretest probability96% / 89%
Negative predictive value, high pretest probabilityOnly 60% — consider further testing if discordant with clinical suspicion
Positive predictive value, intermediate/high pretest probability92%-96%
Positive predictive value, low pretest probabilityOnly 58%

CTPA may be relatively contraindicated in moderate-severe iodinated contrast allergy or renal insufficiency (eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73m2); weigh the contraindication's risk against the clinical value of the test, and consider premedication/IV hydration or an alternative modality.

9. Echocardiography & compression ultrasound

Echocardiography can rarely definitively diagnose PE by directly visualizing a clot in the proximal pulmonary arteries or right heart. More often, it supports the diagnosis by showing signs of right ventricular strain: RV dilation, interventricular septal bowing/flattening, McConnell sign (depressed RV free-wall contractility with a normal apex), reduced TAPSE (<16 mm), or a shortened pulmonary ejection acceleration time with the "60/60" sign.

A negative echo cannot exclude PE (negative predictive value only 40%-50%), and RV strain findings can result from other cardiopulmonary disease, not just acute PE. In a hemodynamically unstable patient where CTPA is not feasible, bedside echocardiography can help justify emergency thrombolytic therapy.

Compression ultrasonography of the lower extremities has >90% sensitivity and ~95% specificity for proximal DVT. Finding a proximal DVT in a patient with suspected PE is considered sufficient to warrant anticoagulation without further PE-specific imaging, but because sensitivity for PE overall is limited, it's mainly reserved for patients in whom CTPA/V/Q imaging is contraindicated or indeterminate.

How Is PE Treated?

10. Treatment — hemodynamically stable PE

All patients with hypoxemia should receive supplemental oxygen. Stable patients have stable vital signs, low bleeding risk, and normal kidney function, and are immediately anticoagulated — as they would be for isolated DVT.

11. Treatment — hemodynamically unstable PE

Patients who are unstable (or have very large PEs) require hospitalization with prompt anticoagulation and oxygen support to prevent hemodynamic collapse. IV fluids help maintain cardiac output/blood pressure, but should be used cautiously — aggressive volume resuscitation can over-distend an already-strained right ventricle and worsen cardiac output. Vasopressors may be needed for severe hypotension/shock.

Reperfusion strategies

OptionNotes
Systemic thrombolysis (eg, tissue plasminogen activator/TPA)Primary treatment of choice for hemodynamically unstable PE; most effective within 48 hours of symptom onset, some benefit up to 14 days

Absolute contraindications to thrombolysis

  • Prior intracranial hemorrhage
  • Known structural intracranial cerebrovascular disease (eg, AVM)
  • Known malignant intracranial neoplasm
  • Ischemic stroke within 3 months
  • Suspected aortic dissection
  • Active bleeding or bleeding diathesis
  • Recent surgery encroaching on the spinal canal
  • Recent significant closed-head/facial trauma with radiographic evidence of bony fracture or brain injury

Indicated for an absolute contraindication to anticoagulation, or recurrent VTE despite adequate anticoagulation. 12. Prognosis & complications

Shock and right ventricular dysfunction at diagnosis confer a poor prognosis and predict mortality. The PESI (Pulmonary Embolism Severity Index) and its simplified version (sPESI) predict 30-day mortality and are mainly useful for identifying low-risk patients (PESI class I-II, or sPESI = 0) who may be candidates for outpatient management.

13. Differential diagnosis

Because PE's presentation ranges from mild dyspnea to sudden cardiac arrest, its differential diagnosis is broad and must be systematically excluded, especially in unstable patients:

Case connection

VignetteYou are called to the orthopedic surgery unit to see a postoperative patient. DM, a 58-year-old female, underwent a left hip replacement procedure 10 days ago. She has new onset of chest pain and shortness of breath. She appears anxious. Her pulse is 120 beats/min, blood pressure 100/70 mmHg, respiratory rate 30 breaths/min, and pulse ox 92%. Her lungs are clear on exam. Her left calf is swollen and tender.

DM's recent surgery (a major VTE risk factor from immobilization and vascular injury) combined with new cardiopulmonary symptoms and a swollen, tender calf strongly suggests a pulmonary embolism arising from a DVT. Consistent with this guide's teaching points: her lung exam is clear (the lung exam is often normal in PE), and her presentation — tachycardia, tachypnea, mild hypoxemia, anxiety — reflects the compensatory hyperventilation driven by dead-space-related hypoxemia.

Explaining it to the patient and her husband: "People are at risk for developing blood clots after major surgery. Being somewhat immobile after surgery may add to the risk; that's why there's so much emphasis on getting up and moving. Today there is a chance that a blood clot has drifted into the lungs, where it would block some blood flow and cause the chest pain and shortness of breath. This can be a serious problem, but we can get it treated right away. I'd like to obtain a CT scan right now to see if there are clots in the lung; if so, we'll need to start you on blood thinner medication immediately."

Given the high clinical suspicion (tachycardia, recent surgery/immobilization, DVT signs, and PE as the most likely diagnosis — a Wells score well above 4), anticoagulation should not be delayed for imaging. A CT pulmonary angiogram is obtained to confirm the diagnosis while treatment proceeds.
