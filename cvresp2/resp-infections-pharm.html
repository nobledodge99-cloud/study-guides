Part 1 · Community-Acquired Pneumonia

1. CAP — Severity Assessment & Site-of-Care

The single costliest decision in CAP is outpatient vs. inpatient — hospitalization costs ~25× more, and outpatient treatment gets patients back to normal life faster while avoiding hospital-acquired risks (thromboembolism, superinfection). Severity scores support — but don't override — clinical judgment (e.g., a borderline score plus poor access to care may still warrant a short observation admission).

CURB-65 (1 point each)

  • Confusion · Urea (BUN) elevated · Respiratory rate ≥30 · Blood pressure low (SBP <90 or DBP ≤60) · age ≥65
CURB-65 score → disposition
Score30-day mortalityDisposition
0–1~2.1%Outpatient
1 (borderline)—Bridges outpatient/admission — depends which criterion was met
≥3Rising sharplyUsually ICU

The Pneumonia Severity Index (PSI) uses more variables (including demographics) and better captures patients safe for outpatient therapy without raising mortality risk. Risk classes I–II → outpatient; III is borderline; IV–V → admit.

Severe CAP → ICU

  • Major criteria (either one = ICU): septic shock needing vasopressors, or respiratory failure needing mechanical ventilation.
  • Minor criteria (≥3 = ICU): RR ≥30, confusion, elevated BUN, hypotension, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, hypothermia, multilobar infiltrates.

Diagnosis & cultures

Diagnosis = clinical findings (cough, fever, sputum, chest pain) plus an infiltrate on CXR; if imaging is negative but suspicion is high, still start empiric antibiotics. Don't chase serial CXRs to judge response — radiographic improvement lags clinical improvement. Outpatient cultures rarely change management (results return too late); blood/sputum cultures are recommended for all inpatients, especially if covering MRSA or Pseudomonas.

Check yourself — 3 questions

A 70-year-old man presents with cough, fever, and a new infiltrate on chest x-ray. He has a respiratory rate of 32/min, new confusion, and a systolic BP of 85 mmHg. Using CURB-65, what disposition is most appropriate?

He scores points for confusion, respiratory rate ≥30, low blood pressure, and age ≥65 — a CURB-65 of 4, indicating severe disease warranting hospital/ICU admission.

A patient with community-acquired pneumonia is being treated empirically. Serial chest x-rays are obtained every 2 days to confirm the antibiotic is working, but the patient is otherwise improving clinically (afebrile, eating, ambulating). Why is this imaging strategy not recommended?

Radiographic clearing lags well behind clinical improvement in CAP, so serial chest x-rays are not used to track antibiotic response — clinical status is the guide, and repeat imaging is reserved for patients who worsen or fail to improve as expected.

A hospitalized patient with CAP has a respiratory rate of 32, new confusion, and a platelet count of 85,000. No vasopressors or mechanical ventilation are required. Based on IDSA/ATS minor severity criteria, how many of the listed minor criteria does this patient meet, and what does this suggest?

Elevated respiratory rate (≥30), new confusion, and thrombocytopenia are three minor severity criteria; meeting ≥3 minor criteria supports ICU-level care even without a major criterion (vasopressors or mechanical ventilation).

2. CAP — Etiology & Drug-Resistant S. pneumoniae

Typical CAP pathogens by site of care
OutpatientHospitalized, non-ICUICU
S. pneumoniae, Mycoplasma, H. influenzae, Chlamydophila, respiratory virusesS. pneumoniae, Mycoplasma, C. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, Legionella, aspiration, virusesS. pneumoniae, S. aureus, Legionella, gram-negative bacilli, H. influenzae

Modifiers that change the likely pathogen

  • Underlying bronchopulmonary disease → H. influenzae, Moraxella catarrhalis (+ S. aureus during an influenza outbreak)
  • Chronic oral steroids, alcoholism, frequent antibiotic use → Enterobacteriaceae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa
  • Aspiration (seizure, altered consciousness, poor dentition) → anaerobes
  • Influenza is the dominant viral cause; endemic fungi (Histoplasma, Coccidioides, Blastomyces) matter only with a matching travel/exposure history

Drug-resistant S. pneumoniae (DRSP) risk factors

β-lactam, macrolide, or fluoroquinolone use in the past 3 months is the single biggest flag — and it drives which class you avoid, not just which you add. Also: age <2 or >65, alcoholism, comorbidities, immunosuppression, daycare exposure.

The recurring exam trap
Recent antibiotic exposure to any class (even for an unrelated infection, e.g., a fluoroquinolone for a UTI) raises DRSP risk and should push you to a *different* class for the current CAP episode.
Check yourself — 3 questions

A patient with COPD who was recently hospitalized on chronic oral corticosteroids develops CAP. Which organism group should be specifically considered that would not be a top concern in a previously healthy outpatient?

Chronic oral steroid use, underlying bronchopulmonary disease, and frequent antibiotic exposure raise the likelihood of gram-negative organisms including Enterobacteriaceae and Pseudomonas aeruginosa.

A patient treated with a fluoroquinolone for a urinary tract infection two months ago now presents with CAP. Why is this history relevant to antibiotic selection for the current pneumonia?

Recent exposure to a beta-lactam, macrolide, or fluoroquinolone — for any infection, not just a respiratory one — is a risk factor for drug-resistant S. pneumoniae and should steer the current regimen toward a different antibiotic class.

A patient aspirates gastric contents during a seizure and subsequently develops pneumonia with poor dentition noted on exam. Which class of organisms is the classic concern in this aspiration scenario?

Classic aspiration pneumonia, especially with poor dentition or esophageal motility issues, raises concern for anaerobic oropharyngeal flora.

3. CAP — Empiric Therapy Ladder & Duration

The regimens build on the same blocks — a β-lactam, a macrolide, or a respiratory fluoroquinolone — simply escalating in agent, dose, and route as severity rises.

CAP empiric therapy by site of care (2019 ATS/IDSA)
SettingRegimen
Outpatient, healthy
(no comorbidities/DRSP/MRSA/Pseudomonas risk)		AmoxicillinCore PO —OR— doxycyclineCore PO —OR— a macrolide PO (azithromycinCore; only if local pneumococcal macrolide resistance <25%)
Outpatient, comorbidities or DRSP riskRespiratory fluoroquinolone PO (levofloxacinCore, moxifloxacinAdditional) —OR— high-dose amoxicillin/amoxicillin-clavulanateAdditional PO plus a macrolide PO
Nonsevere inpatientRespiratory FQ IV/PO —OR— β-lactam IV (ampicillin-sulbactamAdditional, cefotaximeAdditional, ceftriaxoneCore, ceftarolineAdditional) plus macrolide IV
Severe inpatient (ICU)β-lactam IV (same list) plus azithromycinCore IV —OR— β-lactam IV plus respiratory FQ

Macrolide-containing regimens are generally preferred for severe CAP over fluoroquinolone-only regimens, partly for the macrolide's immunomodulatory effect and partly because fluoroquinolones carry a higher risk of C. difficile and downstream resistance in colonizing organisms. A history of β-lactam allergy pushes you toward the respiratory-FQ arm instead.

Adding MRSA / Pseudomonas coverage

Only add if there's a specific risk factor: prior isolation of the organism (especially respiratory) or recent hospitalization with parenteral antibiotic exposure.

  • Pseudomonas: antipseudomonal β-lactam (piperacillin-tazobactamCore, cefepimeCore, ceftazidimeCore, imipenemAdditional, meropenemCore, aztreonamAdditional) — add ciprofloxacinCore or an aminoglycoside (gentamicinCore) for double coverage if indicated.
  • MRSA: vancomycinCore or linezolidCore.

Duration

Minimum 5 days (most patients get 7–10). Stop once afebrile 48–72h, hemodynamically stable, eating, and mentating normally. Confirmed/suspected MRSA or Pseudomonas → treat 7 days.

Check yourself — 3 questions

A previously healthy 40-year-old with no comorbidities is diagnosed with mild CAP as an outpatient, with no recent antibiotic exposure. Which regimen is guideline-recommended?

For previously healthy outpatients without comorbidities or resistance risk factors, monotherapy with amoxicillin, doxycycline, or a macrolide (if local resistance is low) is recommended.

A patient is admitted to the ICU with severe CAP. Which combination is guideline-preferred, and why is it often favored over a fluoroquinolone-only approach?

A beta-lactam plus an advanced macrolide (e.g., azithromycin) is generally preferred for severe CAP, partly for the macrolide's immunomodulatory benefit and because fluoroquinolones carry a higher risk of C. difficile and resistance in colonizing flora.

A patient with CAP has a prior respiratory isolation of Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Which antibiotic addition is most appropriate to ensure adequate empiric coverage?

Prior isolation of Pseudomonas, especially from the respiratory tract, is a specific risk factor that should prompt addition of an antipseudomonal beta-lactam (e.g., piperacillin-tazobactam, cefepime, ceftazidime, imipenem, meropenem, or aztreonam).
Part 2 · HAP/VAP

4. Hospital-Acquired & Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia

HAP develops ≥48h after admission; VAP develops ≥48–72h after intubation. “Healthcare-associated pneumonia (HCAP)” was removed from the 2016 guidelines — simple healthcare contact alone doesn't predict multidrug-resistant (MDR) risk as well as specific validated risk factors do.

Common pathogens

Aerobic gram-negatives (E. coli, Klebsiella, Enterobacter, Pseudomonas, Acinetobacter), gram-positive cocci (S. aureus incl. MRSA), and oropharyngeal flora. Infections are often polymicrobial.

Use the local antibiogram

Local susceptibility data should guide empiric choice — the 2016 guidelines specifically push this to reduce unnecessary dual gram-negative and empiric MRSA coverage, minimizing harm and resistance pressure rather than reflexively broadening.

Do not memorize every risk factor — know the logic

General empiric strategy for VAP/HAP
Risk profileStrategy
No MDR risk factorsOne agent with good gram-negative/Pseudomonas coverage: piperacillin-tazobactamCore, cefepimeCore, imipenemAdditional, meropenemCore, or levofloxacinCore
MDR risk factors present (e.g., recent IV antibiotics, septic shock, ≥5 days hospitalization, high local resistance rates)Two agents against Pseudomonas (an antipseudomonal β-lactam plus either an antipseudomonal FQ, an aminoglycoside, or polymyxin/colistinAdditional) plus one MRSA agent (linezolidCore or vancomycinCore) if MRSA risk is also present
MRSA risk only (no MDR-Pseudomonas risk)One antipseudomonal agent plus one MRSA agent

Risk factors worth recognizing rather than memorizing: recent (90-day) IV antibiotic use, septic shock at diagnosis, structural lung disease, a unit where >10–20% of relevant isolates are resistant (or resistance rates are simply unknown).

Duration

7 days for both HAP and VAP, adjusted for clinical/radiographic/lab trajectory.

Check yourself — 3 questions

Which classification, previously used to flag community patients at risk for resistant pathogens based on healthcare contact alone, was removed from the 2016 HAP/VAP guidelines?

Healthcare-associated pneumonia (HCAP) was removed from the 2016 guidelines because healthcare contact alone was found to be a poor predictor of multidrug-resistant pathogen risk compared to specific validated risk factors.

A patient develops VAP with no risk factors for multidrug-resistant organisms. Which empiric approach is appropriate?

Without MDR risk factors, a single antipseudomonal agent (piperacillin-tazobactam, cefepime, imipenem, meropenem, or levofloxacin) is sufficient — routine dual gram-negative and empiric MRSA coverage should be avoided.

A hospital's antibiogram shows that 25% of Pseudomonas isolates in the ICU are resistant to the antibiotic being considered for empiric HAP monotherapy. What is the recommended approach for a patient with HAP in this unit?

A unit resistance rate above roughly 10% to an agent being considered for monotherapy is itself a risk factor favoring two-agent antipseudomonal coverage rather than single-agent empiric therapy.
Part 2 · Once the Organism Is Known

5. Pathogen-Directed (Definitive) Therapy

Once culture and susceptibility results return, narrow to the most targeted agent. Recognize the mechanism (cell wall inhibitor + protein synthesis inhibitor, etc.) rather than memorizing every drug name — exam questions are framed around basic pharmacology, not brand recall.

Drug-first vs. pathogen-first
This table flips the earlier empiric-therapy tables around: instead of starting from the antibiotic and asking what it covers, you start from the known organism and ask which mechanism/class targets it. Both views test the same underlying facts.
Check yourself — 3 questions

A sputum culture confirms methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus as the cause of a patient's pneumonia, previously treated empirically with vancomycin. What is the most appropriate definitive therapy?

Once methicillin susceptibility is confirmed, therapy should be narrowed to an antistaphylococcal penicillin like nafcillin, the more targeted, narrower-spectrum option compared to vancomycin.

A patient with confirmed Legionella pneumonia is being started on definitive therapy. Which two drug classes are appropriate choices?

Legionella pneumonia is classically treated with a respiratory fluoroquinolone or a macrolide such as azithromycin, both of which achieve good intracellular penetration.

A patient with confirmed Pneumocystis pneumonia needs definitive therapy. Which agent is the drug of choice?

Sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim is the drug of choice for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, an association that becomes especially important in HIV/AIDS patients.
Part 3 · Other Lower Respiratory Infections

6. Acute/Chronic Bronchitis & Bronchiolitis

Acute bronchitis

Usually viral (rhinovirus, coronavirus, influenza, adenovirus). Routine antibiotics are not recommended. Consider antibiotics (azithromycinAdditional or levofloxacinAdditional) only if secondary bacterial infection is suspected — persistent fever/symptoms beyond 4–6 days, or a predisposed patient (elderly, immunocompromised). During an influenza outbreak, consider early antiviral therapy instead.

Chronic bronchitis / acute exacerbation of COPD (AECB)

Antibiotics help when ≥2 of 3 cardinal symptoms are present: increased dyspnea, increased sputum volume, purulent sputum.

AECB classification & empiric therapy
ClassDefinition1st-line
SimpleNo risk factorsMacrolide, cephalosporinAdditional, or TMP-SMX
Complicated≥1 risk factor (age ≥65, severe COPD, ≥2 exacerbations/yr, recent hospitalization, comorbidities)Amoxicillin-clavulanateCore or a respiratory fluoroquinolone (avoid ciprofloxacin unless Pseudomonas risk)
Severe complicatedComplicated criteria + hospitalization; Pseudomonas risk (chronic colonization, very severe COPD, bronchiectasis, recent broad-spectrum antibiotics)No Pseudomonas risk: levofloxacinCore/moxifloxacinAdditional/ceftriaxoneCore. Pseudomonas risk: cefepimeCore/ceftazidimeCore/piperacillin-tazobactamCore

Bronchiolitis (infants)

RSV causes up to 75% of cases (parainfluenza, adenovirus, influenza account for the rest; bacteria are a minor secondary cause). Most healthy infants are managed as outpatients with supportive care. Severe disease with respiratory failure/dehydration needs admission; aerosolized ribavirinAdditional may be considered but is limited by toxicity/variable efficacy. High-risk infants (prematurity, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, congenital heart disease) may receive palivizumabAdditional prophylaxis — a monoclonal antibody, not a treatment.

Check yourself — 3 questions

A healthy young adult presents with a 3-day history of cough and mild fever, no underlying lung disease. Chest exam is unremarkable. What is the recommended management for suspected acute bronchitis?

Acute bronchitis is usually viral, and routine antibiotic use is not recommended; supportive care (reassurance, antipyretics) is first-line unless secondary bacterial infection or a high-risk feature is present.

A COPD patient with severe airflow limitation (FEV1 <40%), 3 exacerbations in the past year, and known chronic Pseudomonas colonization develops an acute exacerbation requiring hospitalization. Which empiric regimen best fits this severe complicated presentation?

Severe complicated AECB with Pseudomonas risk factors (chronic colonization, severe COPD) requires an antipseudomonal agent such as cefepime, ceftazidime, or piperacillin-tazobactam.

A premature infant with bronchopulmonary dysplasia is being considered for an agent to reduce the risk of severe RSV disease before any infection occurs. Which agent is appropriate, and is it treatment or prophylaxis?

Palivizumab is a monoclonal antibody used as prophylaxis (not treatment) against RSV in high-risk infants such as those born prematurely or with bronchopulmonary dysplasia or congenital heart disease.
Part 4 · Influenza & COVID-19

7. Influenza — Virology & Antiviral Pharmacology

Influenza spreads via respiratory droplets/aerosols and fomites; incubation 1–4 days. Viral shedding starts just before symptoms, peaks ~24–48h, and is mostly gone by day 5–10 (longer in older/immunocompromised patients). Classic presentation: abrupt fever, nonproductive cough, myalgia.

Hemagglutinin binds sialic acid on respiratory epithelium to initiate infection; after replication, neuraminidase cleaves sialic acid to release progeny virions — this cleavage step, not attachment, is what the neuraminidase inhibitors block.

Anti-influenza agents
Class / AgentMOANotes

OseltamivirCore (PO), zanamivirCore (inhaled), peramivirAdditional (IV)		Sialic acid analogs; block neuraminidase-mediated release of progeny virionsOseltamivir: GI upset, rare neuropsychiatric events (mostly pediatric). Zanamivir: bronchospasm — avoid in underlying airway disease.

Timing: most effective within 48h of symptom onset, though severe disease may still warrant treatment later. Prophylaxis (before infection) is an option for high-risk contacts or long-term care outbreaks.

Watch for the confusion trap
Neuraminidase inhibitors structurally resemble sialic acid, which sounds like it should block *attachment* — but attachment is already done by the time these drugs act. They block *release* of newly made virus by preventing neuraminidase from cleaving sialic acid off the host cell.
Check yourself — 3 questions

A patient is started on oseltamivir for influenza. What is the mechanism by which this drug limits viral spread within the respiratory tract?

Oseltamivir is a sialic acid analog that inhibits neuraminidase, preventing the enzyme from cleaving sialic acid residues that anchor newly formed virions to the infected cell — blocking their release, not their initial attachment.

A patient with asthma is diagnosed with influenza. Which neuraminidase inhibitor should generally be avoided due to a risk of bronchospasm and decreased pulmonary function?

Zanamivir is administered by inhalation and carries a risk of bronchospasm and decreased pulmonary function, so it is generally avoided in patients with underlying airway disease such as asthma or COPD.

Amantadine and rimantadine were once used for influenza A treatment and prophylaxis. Why are they no longer recommended?

M2 channel blockers like amantadine and rimantadine are no longer recommended for influenza A due to high rates of resistance that have developed over time.

Check yourself — 2 questions

A hospitalized COVID-19 patient requiring supplemental oxygen (but not mechanical ventilation) is started on an IV antiviral that gets incorporated into viral RNA, causing delayed chain termination. Which drug is this?

Remdesivir is an adenosine nucleotide analog that, after intracellular triphosphorylation, is incorporated into viral RNA and causes delayed chain termination, inhibiting the SARS-CoV-2 RNA-dependent RNA polymerase.

A patient at high risk for severe COVID-19 is prescribed Paxlovid (nirmatrelvir-ritonavir). What is the specific role of ritonavir in this combination?

Ritonavir contributes no antiviral activity against SARS-CoV-2 in this combination — it acts solely as a pharmacokinetic booster, inhibiting the metabolism of nirmatrelvir to maintain therapeutic drug levels.

Check yourself — 2 questions

A patient with genital herpes is prescribed valacyclovir. Why does this class of antiviral achieve selective toxicity against HSV-infected cells rather than uninfected human cells?

Acyclovir/valacyclovir require an initial phosphorylation step catalyzed by viral thymidine kinase, which is far more efficient at activating these drugs than host cell enzymes — providing selective toxicity toward infected cells.

A patient receiving ganciclovir for CMV retinitis develops significant bone marrow suppression. What is the mechanistic basis for choosing ganciclovir at all, given this toxicity?

Ganciclovir is an acyclic guanosine analog that, once triphosphorylated, inhibits CMV DNA polymerase and terminates DNA chain elongation — myelosuppression is its main dose-limiting adverse effect despite this targeted mechanism.
Part 5 · Antifungals

10. Antifungal Pharmacology

Fungi are eukaryotic with a rigid cell wall (β-glucans) and ergosterol (not cholesterol) in their membrane — these differences are the selective-toxicity targets. Most fungal infections are opportunistic, not primary.

Match the target to the drug
Cell membrane target (ergosterol) → azoles and amphotericin B. Cell wall target (β-glucan) → echinocandins. This mirrors the antibacterial cell-wall-vs-membrane logic from the prior session.
Check yourself — 3 questions

A patient with invasive aspergillosis is treated with voriconazole. What is the mechanism of action of this drug?

Azoles like voriconazole inhibit the fungal cytochrome P450 enzyme lanosterol 14-alpha-demethylase, reducing ergosterol production and compromising the fungal cell membrane.

A patient with a life-threatening systemic fungal infection is started on amphotericin B despite its significant toxicity. What justifies choosing this agent over other options?

Amphotericin B has the broadest spectrum of any antifungal and is generally reserved for severe or life-threatening infections specifically because its toxicity (infusion reactions, cumulative nephrotoxicity) limits routine use.

Caspofungin is used to treat invasive candidiasis. Which fungal structure does this echinocandin target, distinguishing its mechanism from the azoles?

Echinocandins like caspofungin inhibit beta(1,3)-glucan synthase, disrupting fungal cell wall synthesis — a distinct target from the ergosterol-focused mechanism of the azoles and amphotericin B.
