1. Overview & Classification of Angina

Angina pectoris is the cardinal symptom of myocardial ischemia, affecting nearly 8 million Americans. It results from an imbalance between myocardial O₂ supply and demand.

  • Myocardial infarction → irreversible cell death
  • Angina → reversible stage of ischemia
  • Ischemic heart disease remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide

Stable (Effort) Angina

  • Due to fixed atherosclerotic narrowing of a coronary artery
  • Predictable symptoms with exertion/emotional stress (↑ O₂ demand); relieved by rest or nitroglycerin

Unstable Angina (ACS Spectrum)

  • Plaque rupture + platelet aggregation/thrombosis → abrupt ↓ coronary flow
  • By definition, no myocardial necrosis → troponin normal (though ST depression/T-wave inversion may be seen on ECG)
  • High risk of progression to NSTEMI/STEMI
  • Initial Tx: anti-ischemic therapy, antiplatelets, anticoagulation to prevent progression to infarction

Variant (Prinzmetal / Vasospastic) Angina

  • Focal coronary vessel spasm, typically occurring at rest (not with exertion)
  • Associated with transient ST-segment elevation during attacks
  • Responds to vasodilators: nitrates and calcium channel blockers

Microvascular Angina

  • More common in women and patients with diabetes
  • Reflects impaired coronary microvascular vasodilator function rather than epicardial stenosis — angiography may look normal
  • Tx: beta-blockers, nitrates, CCBs (variable response); ranolazine sometimes used when refractory
Ischemic heart disease branches into chronic CAD (stable angina) and acute coronary syndromes (unstable angina, NSTEMI, STEMI).
Quick Check — Classification

A patient reports chest pain occurring at rest, with transient ST-elevation on ECG that resolves with symptom resolution. Troponins are negative. This is most consistent with:

Rest-onset pain with transient ST-elevation is the hallmark of coronary vasospasm — variant/Prinzmetal angina — which responds to nitrates and CCBs; beta-blockers should be avoided.

2. Pathophysiology

Angina occurs when myocardial O₂ demand > O₂ supply.

Determinants of Oxygen Demand

  • Heart rate
  • Contractility
  • Wall stress (preload + afterload)

Determinants of Oxygen Supply

  • Coronary blood flow
  • Vascular tone & endothelial function
  • Blood oxygen-carrying capacity

Mapping Clinical Type to Mechanism

TypeCore Mechanism
Stable angina↑ demand against a fixed, limited supply
Unstable anginaAcute ↓ supply from thrombosis
Variant anginaDynamic ↓ supply from vasospasm
Pearl: Almost every antianginal drug class works by shifting one side of this equation — most lower demand (↓ HR, contractility, wall stress); nitrates and CCBs can also raise supply via coronary vasodilation.

3. Therapeutic Goals & Symptom Relief vs Mortality Benefit

Stable Angina

  • Restore balance between O₂ supply and demand
  • Most drugs ↓ demand (↓ HR, BP, contractility, wall stress)
  • Some drugs ↑ supply (coronary vasodilation) — especially nitrates and CCBs

Variant (Prinzmetal) Angina

Goal: reverse vasospasm (nitrates, DHP-CCBs).

Unstable Angina (ACS)

Goal: prevent thrombus progression — this is not just symptom relief; antiplatelets/anticoagulants/revascularization take priority over antianginal drugs.

Symptom Relief (No Survival Benefit)

Nitrates, CCBs, ranolazine — improve symptoms and exercise tolerance but do not reduce mortality in stable angina/ACS.

Mortality Benefit (Disease-Modifying Therapies)

  • Beta-blockers (post-MI, HFrEF)
  • Statins
  • ACE inhibitors / ARBs
  • Antiplatelet agents (aspirin, P2Y₁₂ inhibitors)
Clinical strategy: Combine symptomatic therapy + preventive/disease-modifying therapy for optimal care — an antianginal regimen alone is incomplete management of CAD.
Quick Check — Symptom vs Mortality

Which of the following drug classes used in angina management has been shown to reduce mortality, rather than only relieve symptoms?

Beta-blockers are disease-modifying — proven survival benefit post-MI and in HFrEF. Nitrates, CCBs, and ranolazine provide symptom relief only.

4. Organic Nitrates

Class: Nitroglycerin CORE, Isosorbide dinitrate & Isosorbide mononitrate ADDITIONAL

Mechanism of Action

  • Prodrugs → release nitric oxide (NO) in vascular smooth muscle → activates guanylyl cyclase → ↑ cGMP → dephosphorylates myosin light chains → smooth muscle relaxation
  • Venodilation predominates at therapeutic doses → ↓ preload → ↓ myocardial O₂ demand
  • Arteriolar dilation at higher doses → modest ↓ afterload
  • Coronary vasodilation → improved perfusion, especially useful in vasospastic angina

Therapeutic Effects

  • ↓ ventricular filling pressures & ↓ myocardial O₂ consumption → relief of angina
  • Coronary dilation → ↓ ischemia in vasospastic angina
  • Acute HF: IV nitroglycerin ↓ LV filling & pulmonary pressures → relieves dyspnea
  • Chronic therapy: symptom relief only — no survival benefit in stable angina/ACS

Adverse Effects, Tolerance, & Interactions

  • ADRs: headache (meningeal vessel dilation), dizziness, orthostatic hypotension, syncope, reflex tachycardia (especially short-acting forms)
  • Tolerance/tachyphylaxis: develops with continuous exposure — mechanisms include sulfhydryl/cofactor depletion, reactive oxygen species accumulation, and neurohormonal counter-regulation. Prevented clinically with a 10–12 hour nitrate-free interval (often overnight)
  • Contraindications: concurrent PDE-5 inhibitors (profound hypotension risk), severe hypotension, shock, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
  • Drug interactions: potentiated by alcohol and other antihypertensives

Nitroglycerin CORE

  • Used for both acute angina relief and prophylaxis
  • Sublingual/spray: rapid onset (1–3 min) — the gold standard for acute relief
  • Oral sustained-release & transdermal: prevention of recurrent angina
  • Extensive first-pass metabolism → low oral bioavailability (hence SL/spray/transdermal routes preferred)
  • Transdermal patches require a daily nitrate-free interval to prevent tolerance

Isosorbide Dinitrate ADDITIONAL

  • Angina prophylaxis; combined with hydralazine for HFrEF (particularly in Black patients)
  • Low oral bioavailability (~25%) due to first-pass metabolism
  • Converted to isosorbide mononitrate, its active metabolite
  • Immediate-release: 4–6 hr duration; sustained-release forms last longer

Isosorbide Mononitrate ADDITIONAL

  • Used exclusively for chronic prophylaxis — not effective for acute relief
  • Nearly complete oral bioavailability, long half-life → once/twice-daily dosing
  • Extended-release forms provide 12–24 hr coverage
Case Snapshot

A patient using sildenafil for erectile dysfunction develops chest pain and takes sublingual nitroglycerin. What's the concern?

Answer: Co-administration of nitrates with PDE-5 inhibitors can cause profound, life-threatening hypotension via excessive cGMP accumulation — this combination is contraindicated.

Quick Check — Organic Nitrates

Which nitrate is used exclusively for chronic angina prophylaxis and is not effective for acute symptom relief?

Isosorbide mononitrate has nearly complete oral bioavailability and a long half-life, making it ideal for chronic prophylaxis, but its slow onset makes it unsuitable for acute relief.

What is the primary strategy to prevent nitrate tolerance during chronic therapy?

A daily nitrate-free interval (often overnight) allows restoration of sulfhydryl groups/nitrate responsiveness and prevents tachyphylaxis.

5. Beta-Blockers

Class: Metoprolol CORE, Atenolol & Carvedilol ADDITIONAL

Mechanism of Action

  • β₁-receptor blockade → ↓ HR, contractility, and BP → ↓ myocardial O₂ demand
  • Prolongs diastole → improves coronary perfusion (more time for coronary filling)

Therapeutic Effects & Use

  • ↓ frequency/severity of exertional angina, ↑ exercise tolerance
  • Improves survival post-MI and in chronic HFrEF → disease-modifying, not just symptomatic
  • First-line option in stable angina (often combined with nitrates)
  • Ineffective / may worsen vasospastic (Prinzmetal) angina — unopposed alpha-mediated vasoconstriction can worsen coronary spasm

Adverse Effects, Contraindications, Interactions

  • ADRs: bradycardia, fatigue, ↓ exercise tolerance, dizziness, sexual dysfunction
  • CI: severe bradycardia, AV block, acute decompensated HF, severe asthma/COPD (cardioselective β₁-blockers may be used cautiously in mild/moderate bronchospasm)
  • Caution: avoid abrupt withdrawal — risk of rebound ischemia/MI
  • DI: additive bradycardia/hypotension with other negative chronotropes (CCBs, antiarrhythmics)
Pearl: Metoprolol succinate, carvedilol, and bisoprolol are the agents with proven survival benefit specifically in HFrEF — know these by name.
Quick Check — Beta-Blockers

Beta-blockers are generally avoided as monotherapy in which type of angina, and why?

Blocking beta-2 mediated vasodilation while leaving alpha-mediated vasoconstriction unopposed can worsen coronary vasospasm in Prinzmetal angina.

6. Calcium Channel Blockers

Block L-type calcium channels in vascular smooth muscle and cardiac myocytes → reduces myocardial O₂ demand and improves O₂ supply.

Non-Dihydropyridines (NDHPs): Verapamil, Diltiazem CORE

  • MOA: more pronounced cardiac effects — slow HR, ↓ contractility, prolong diastolic filling → ↓ O₂ demand & ↑ perfusion
  • Therapeutic use: alternative to beta-blockers in stable angina; rate control in AFib/flutter; not recommended in vasospastic angina (DHPs preferred)
  • ADRs: bradycardia, AV block, hypotension, constipation (particularly verapamil), can worsen HFrEF
  • CI: advanced AV block, severe LV dysfunction, acute decompensated HF
  • DI: additive risk with beta-blockers or other negative chronotropes (bradycardia/AV block)

Dihydropyridines (DHPs): Amlodipine CORE, Nifedipine ADDITIONAL

  • MOA: potent arterial vasodilation → ↓ afterload, ↓ O₂ demand, ↑ coronary perfusion, relieves vasospasm
  • Therapeutic use: first-line for vasospastic (Prinzmetal) angina; chronic stable angina when beta-blockers insufficient/contraindicated; often combined with nitrates for additive benefit
  • ADRs: headache, flushing, dizziness, peripheral edema (vasodilation-related); reflex tachycardia (especially immediate-release nifedipine, which may worsen angina/HF)
  • HF safety: DHPs are generally neutral in HF — amlodipine is the safest choice in reduced ejection fraction
  • DI: other antihypertensives, CYP3A4 modulators

A patient with vasospastic (Prinzmetal) angina and no other cardiac comorbidities needs a CCB. Which is preferred?

Dihydropyridines are first-line for vasospastic angina because of their potent arterial/coronary vasodilating effect; non-DHPs are not preferred here.

Both agents are negative chronotropes/dromotropes; combining them additively risks bradycardia and AV block.

7. Ranolazine ADDITIONAL

Late Na⁺ current inhibitor — no "core" agent listed for this class, but ranolazine itself needs full mechanistic understanding per the lecture.

Mechanism of Action

  • Inhibits the late sodium current (INa) in cardiac myocytes → ↓ intracellular Na⁺ accumulation → ↓ Ca²⁺ entry via the Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger
  • Net effect: ↓ diastolic wall tension, improved ventricular relaxation, ↓ myocardial O₂ consumption
  • Minimal effect on heart rate or blood pressure — mechanistically distinct from other antianginals

Therapeutic Use

  • Improves exercise tolerance and reduces angina frequency, independent of hemodynamic change
  • Second-line therapy for chronic stable angina when first-line agents are inadequate/not tolerated
  • Particularly useful when HR/BP limit use of beta-blockers or CCBs
  • Often combined with beta-blockers, CCBs, or nitrates

Adverse Effects & Contraindications

  • ADRs: dizziness, headache, nausea, constipation
  • Prolongs the QT interval (rarely associated with torsades)
  • CI: pre-existing QT prolongation, hepatic impairment, concurrent strong CYP3A4 inhibitors
  • PK/DI: metabolized by CYP3A4 — strong inhibitors ↑ levels, inducers ↓ efficacy
Quick Check — Ranolazine

Which feature makes ranolazine uniquely useful in a patient with symptomatic bradycardia who still has anginal symptoms?

Because ranolazine works via the late sodium current rather than hemodynamics, it can be added for symptom control without further lowering HR or BP.

8. Acute Relief vs Chronic Prophylaxis — Clinical Algorithm

Acute Symptom Relief

  • Short-acting sublingual nitroglycerin: the gold standard
  • Onset: 1–3 minutes
  • Mechanism: vasodilation → ↓ preload → rapid chest pain relief
  • Provides no long-term protection

Chronic Prophylaxis of Stable Angina

  • First-line: beta-blockers (especially with HTN or prior MI)
  • Alternatives: calcium channel blockers (non-DHP: ↓ HR/contractility; DHP: ↓ afterload)
  • Adjunctive: long-acting nitrates (require 10–12 hr nitrate-free interval); ranolazine for persistent symptoms despite standard therapy

Variant (Prinzmetal) Angina

  • Coronary vessel spasm — not increased demand — drives ischemia
  • Mainstays: DHP-CCBs and nitrates
  • Beta-blockers contraindicated (risk of worsening spasm via unopposed alpha vasoconstriction)

Unstable Angina / ACS

  • Nitrates and beta-blockers may relieve ischemic symptoms
  • CCBs used selectively
  • Cornerstone of therapy: antiplatelet & antithrombotic treatment to address the underlying thrombus — not just symptomatic antianginal therapy
Pearl: "Which drug, which angina" is a classic exam pattern — stable = beta-blocker first; variant = CCB/nitrate, no beta-blocker; unstable/ACS = antiplatelet/anticoagulant first, antianginals adjunctive.

9. Preventive & Adjunctive Interventions

Goal: reduce risk of MI, cardiovascular death, and adverse remodeling in CAD — in addition to angina symptom relief.

Lipid-Lowering Therapy (Statins)

  • First-line for all CAD patients
  • Inhibit HMG-CoA reductase → ↓ hepatic cholesterol synthesis, ↑ LDL receptor expression → ↓ circulating LDL
  • Beyond lipid-lowering: stabilize plaques, improve endothelial function, ↓ vascular inflammation
  • Aggressive therapy ↓ MI, stroke, and CV death risk (primary & secondary prevention)
  • ADRs: myopathy, hepatotoxicity (uncommon); many are CYP3A4 substrates

Antiplatelet Therapy

  • Aspirin (75–100 mg/day): irreversibly inhibits platelet COX-1 → ↓ thromboxane A2-mediated aggregation → ↓ CV events & mortality
  • P2Y₁₂ inhibitors (clopidogrel, prasugrel, ticagrelor): block ADP-mediated platelet activation; used with aspirin as DAPT after ACS or PCI
  • DAPT ↓ recurrent ischemic events, including stent thrombosis

Anticoagulants

  • Acute ACS adjuncts: UFH, LMWH (enhance antithrombin, inhibit thrombin & factor Xa); fondaparinux (selective factor Xa inhibition); bivalirudin (direct thrombin inhibitor, used in select PCI settings)
  • Prevent thrombus propagation in UA/NSTEMI; monitor bleeding risk
  • DOACs (rivaroxaban, apixaban): reserved for CAD with coexisting AFib or select post-ACS indications

ACE Inhibitors / ARBs

  • Block angiotensin II formation (ACEi) or its receptor (ARB) → ↓ afterload, attenuate remodeling, improve endothelial function
  • ↓ MI, stroke, CV death — especially beneficial with diabetes, prior MI, LV dysfunction, or CKD
  • ADRs: cough (ACEi), hyperkalemia; contraindicated in pregnancy and bilateral renal artery stenosis

Beta-Blockers (Cardioprotective Role)

  • ↓ HR & myocardial O₂ demand
  • Proven mortality benefit post-MI and in LV dysfunction
  • Recommended long-term unless contraindicated
Pearl: Statins + antiplatelets + anticoagulants (acute) + ACEi/ARB + beta-blockers form the evidence-based foundation of CAD management — this is the disease-modifying layer that sits on top of symptomatic antianginal therapy.
Quick Check — Preventive Therapy

A patient post-MI with diabetes and mild CKD would derive particular benefit from which class, beyond statins and antiplatelets?

ACEi/ARBs are especially beneficial in diabetes, prior MI, LV dysfunction, or CKD, reducing MI, stroke, and CV death risk.

10. Pharmacologic Stress Testing

Used in patients unable to exercise adequately, to evaluate coronary blood flow and detect ischemia. Two approaches: coronary vasodilation or increased myocardial O₂ demand.

Coronary Vasodilators

Adenosine CORE · Dipyridamole, Regadenoson ADDITIONAL

  • MOA: A₂A adenosine receptor agonism → ↑ cAMP → coronary vasodilation. Healthy vessels dilate more than stenotic vessels → "coronary steal" unmasks ischemia on perfusion imaging
  • Adenosine (CORE): extremely short half-life (<10 sec), continuous IV infusion required
  • Dipyridamole (Additional): inhibits adenosine uptake → ↑ extracellular adenosine; longer half-life, less titratable
  • Regadenoson (Additional): selective A₂A agonist, half-life 2–4 min, allows convenient single IV bolus dosing, fewer off-target effects
  • ADRs: flushing, chest discomfort, hypotension, AV block, bronchospasm
  • CI: severe asthma/COPD, high-grade AV block, hypotension

Increased Oxygen Demand Agent

Dobutamine CORE

  • MOA: β₁-adrenergic agonist → ↑ HR & contractility → provokes ischemia in stenotic regions
  • Reserved for patients with contraindications to vasodilators (e.g., severe bronchospasm, high-grade AV block)
  • ADRs: tachycardia, palpitations, angina, hypertension, arrhythmias
  • Toxicity managed primarily by reducing/stopping the infusion (short half-life, ~2 min); beta-blockers blunt its effect

Adenosine Antidotes

Theophylline, Caffeine ADDITIONAL

  • Competitive antagonists at adenosine receptors — reverse bradycardia, AV block, hypotension, bronchospasm from vasodilator stress agents
  • These methylxanthines can also blunt/reverse the intended test effect if given prematurely
Pearl: Vasodilators are preferred for most patients (regadenoson favored for convenient bolus dosing); dobutamine is the fallback when vasodilators are contraindicated. Methylxanthines are both a confound (blunt the test) and an antidote (reverse toxicity) — context matters.
Quick Check — Stress Testing

A patient scheduled for adenosine stress testing has severe asthma. What is the best alternative agent?

All A2A agonists (adenosine, regadenoson) and dipyridamole risk bronchospasm and are contraindicated/cautioned in severe asthma/COPD. Dobutamine, which works via a different mechanism (β1 agonism), is the appropriate alternative.

Which agent would reverse persistent hypotension and bronchospasm after an adenosine stress test?

Theophylline (or caffeine) competitively antagonizes adenosine receptors, reversing its adverse hemodynamic and bronchospastic effects.

12. High-Yield Clinical Takeaways

  • Acute angina: sublingual nitroglycerin for rapid symptom relief
  • Chronic angina: beta-blocker and/or CCB commonly used first-line; long-acting nitrates added based on HR, BP, comorbidities, and contraindications
  • Variant angina: CCBs and nitrates; avoid beta-blockers
  • Nitrate safety: PDE-5 inhibitor interaction + nitrate-free interval with chronic therapy
  • CCBs: distinguish DHP vascular effects from non-DHP cardiac effects; avoid combinations that increase bradycardia/AV block risk
  • Ranolazine: useful when additional symptom control is needed, especially when HR/BP limit other agents; watch for QT/CYP3A concerns
  • Symptoms ≠ outcomes: most antianginal therapy controls symptoms only; separate risk-reduction therapies (statins, antiplatelets, ACEi/ARB, beta-blockers) address future CV events
  • Stress testing: vasodilator agents vs dobutamine — know the basic mechanistic distinction and when each is used
