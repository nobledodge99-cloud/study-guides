Part 1 — Cystic Fibrosis

Cystic fibrosis — overview, inheritance & incidence

Cystic fibrosis (CF) is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations in the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) protein. Organs affected include the lungs, pancreas, intestines, liver, sweat glands, sinuses, and the vas deferens.

Lung disease leads to death in 80% of patients, with a median expected survival of 37 years.

Incidence by population

PopulationIncidence
White patients1 in 2,500
Hispanic patients1 in 7,000
Native American patients1 in 10,900
African American patients1 in 15,000
Asian patients1 in 35,000
Clinical pearlCF is far more common in White patients than in any other group listed — a favorite epidemiology fact, and a reminder that CF should still be considered in any ethnicity when the clinical picture fits, just with a lower prior probability.

Pathogenesis

The CFTR protein is a chloride channel. In CF, defective CFTR causes decreased chloride secretion from epithelial lining cells in the airway lumen, which thins the liquid lining surface of the airway less than normal — producing thick, dehydrated mucus and impaired mucociliary clearance. This is the root mechanism behind CF's chronic infections, bronchiectasis, bronchiolectasis, bronchial stenosis, fibrosis, and mucous plugging.

Two broad categories of CFTR mutation

Mutation typeExampleProblem
Function defect onlyG551D ("door-jamming" mutation)CFTR reaches the cell surface, but the channel itself doesn't open properly — chloride cannot pass through even though it reaches the channel
Location + function defectDelta F508 (the most common CF mutation)The CFTR protein is made, but never reaches the cell surface at all — it floats inside the cell instead
Clinical pearlThis two-category framework (a channel that's there but broken, vs. a channel that never gets to the surface) is exactly the logic behind modern CFTR modulator therapy: corrector drugs help boost the protein to the surface, and potentiator/doorman drugs help the channel open once it's there — different fixes for different molecular problems.

Clinical pearlNotice the mechanism is the same underlying chloride-transport defect everywhere, but the direction differs by tissue: in the airway and pancreatic ducts, defective CFTR means too little chloride/fluid secretion into the lumen (thick, dehydrated secretions); in the sweat duct, defective CFTR means failure to reabsorb chloride out of the lumen back into the cell (so sweat stays salty instead of being diluted as it travels the duct).

Diagnosis

The standard diagnostic pathway:

  1. Screening: immunoreactive trypsinogen (IRT) — a marker of pancreatic injury
  2. Genetic mutation analysis
  3. Confirmatory sweat chloride testing

The sweat test measures the chloride concentration in sweat that has been stimulated by pilocarpine iontophoresis. Notably, 5% of diagnoses are made after age 18 — CF is not exclusively a pediatric diagnosis.

Clinical pearlDon't skip straight to the sweat test in your mental algorithm — IRT screening (usually as part of newborn screening) comes first, then genetic testing, with the sweat test as the confirmatory step. And remember that a small but real fraction of CF is diagnosed in adulthood.

Treatment

Airway pathogen & antibiotic prophylaxis

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is the primary lung pathogen in CF. Staphylococcus aureus (including MRSA) is also common. Other lung pathogens include allergic bronchopulmonary mycoses and nontuberculous mycobacterial infections.

RouteExamples
Oral antibioticsAzithromycin (patients ≥6yr with Pseudomonas aeruginosa meet criteria — 250mg PO Mon/Wed/Fri if 25-40kg, 500mg if >40kg); trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole; ciprofloxacin
IV antibioticsAztreonam, cefepime, ceftazidime, ciprofloxacin, meropenem, piperacillin/tazobactam, tobramycin (higher-dose extended-interval dosing may be more effective and less nephrotoxic), vancomycin
Inhaled antibioticsTobramycin (300mg inhaled twice daily, 28-day cycle); aztreonam (75mg inhaled 3x daily, 28-day cycle)

Other supportive therapies

Pancreatic enzymes, vitamin supplementation, bronchodilators, hypertonic saline inhalation, dornase alfa (breaks down DNA in thick mucus), ibuprofen, oxygen, and lung transplant for end-stage disease. Chest percussion and postural drainage help mobilize secretions.

Clinical pearlThe inhaled and oral antibiotic regimens are dosed in cycles/scheduled days (28-day inhaled cycles; 3x-weekly azithromycin) rather than continuous daily dosing — this pattern itself is a testable detail distinguishing chronic CF suppression therapy from treatment of an acute exacerbation.

Part 2 — Bronchiectasis

Definition

Bronchiectasis is the abnormal, permanent dilatation of the bronchi and bronchioles, caused by repeated cycles of airway infection and inflammation. Abnormalities of cilia, mucous clearance, mucus rheology, airway drainage, and host defenses can all lead to bronchiectasis. Patients develop chronic infections that progressively destroy lung tissue.

Check yourself

Exam-style question
What is the standard clinical definition of bronchiectasis?
AReversible bronchospasm triggered by allergens
BTransient bronchial wall thickening seen only during acute infection
CA restrictive lung disease caused by alveolar fibrosis
DIsolated dilation of the trachea without bronchial involvement
EAbnormal, permanent dilatation of the bronchi and bronchioles from repeated cycles of airway infection and inflammation
Explanation: Bronchiectasis is the abnormal, permanent dilatation of the bronchi and bronchioles, driven by repeated cycles of airway infection and inflammation together with abnormalities of cilia, mucus clearance, mucus rheology, airway drainage, or host defenses.

Etiologies

In about 1/3, the etiology is unknown. About 1/2 of all bronchiectasis is due to cystic fibrosis — making CF the single most common identifiable cause.

Infectious (about 1/3 of cases, often years before disease onset)

Childhood pertussis, TB, Mycobacterium avium-intracellulare (MAI) — MAI classically involves the right middle lobe and lingula.

Genetic

Cystic fibrosis, primary ciliary dyskinesia, alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency.

Anatomic

Esophageal dysfunction with aspiration, COPD, allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis, endobronchial tumors, extrinsic compression by lymph nodes, foreign bodies.

Immune & autoimmune

Primary hypogammaglobulinemia, immunoglobulin G deficiencies, HIV, Sjögren's syndrome, rheumatoid arthritis.

Clinical pearlGiven how often CF underlies bronchiectasis (~half of cases), a sweat chloride test belongs in essentially every adult bronchiectasis workup, not just in pediatric patients — remember 5% of CF is diagnosed after age 18.

Symptoms & clinical course

Symptoms: chronic cough with purulent sputum, dyspnea, intermittent hemoptysis, pleuritic chest pain, weight loss, and fatigue.

Physical exam: wheezing and crackles. Presentation is variable — some patients have few symptoms, others have a daily cough productive of sputum.

Clinical course: usually a slow decline in pulmonary function, but decline is more rapid in patients with Pseudomonas aeruginosa colonization/infection.

Imaging

High-resolution CT (HRCT) is used to make the diagnosis. Diagnostic findings:

  • Lack of bronchial tapering
  • Bronchi visible in the peripheral 1 cm of the lung
  • Internal bronchial diameter greater than the diameter of the accompanying bronchial artery

Lobar distribution as an etiology clue

DistributionSuggests
Upper lobe predominanceCystic fibrosis
Lower lobe predominanceAspiration
Right middle lobe & lingular predominanceMAI infection
Central bronchiectasisAllergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA)
Clinical pearlLobar distribution on CT is a high-yield shortcut to etiology: upper lobe → think CF; lower lobe → think aspiration; right middle lobe/lingula → think MAI; central → think ABPA.

Treatment

A stepwise approach:

  1. Treat the underlying condition
  2. Specific antimicrobials for active infection
  3. Anti-inflammatory therapy — inhaled steroids, macrolide antibiotics
  4. Mobilization of secretions — pharmacologic or mechanical (e.g., chest percussion/postural drainage, as in CF)
  5. Surgery for localized or refractory disease
  6. Transplantation for end-stage disease
Clinical pearlMacrolide antibiotics appear twice in this list conceptually — once as an antimicrobial and again as an anti-inflammatory agent. Their chronic, low-dose anti-inflammatory use is a separate rationale from treating an acute bacterial infection.

Check yourself

Exam-style question

AInhaled short-acting beta agonists
BSystemic high-dose vitamin D
CNSAIDs
DMacrolide antibiotics

Explanation: Macrolide antibiotics (e.g., azithromycin) are used in bronchiectasis for their anti-inflammatory properties, alongside inhaled corticosteroids — a use distinct from their basic antimicrobial action. Bronchiectasis treatment overall follows a stepwise approach: treat the underlying condition, use specific antimicrobials for active infection, add anti-inflammatory therapy, mobilize secretions (pharmacologically or mechanically), consider surgery for localized/refractory disease, and consider transplantation for end-stage disease.

Part 3 — Tying CF & Bronchiectasis Together

How cystic fibrosis and bronchiectasis relate

Bronchiectasis is best understood as a shared structural endpoint reached by many different diseases — and cystic fibrosis is the single most common identifiable cause, accounting for roughly half of all bronchiectasis cases. The two conditions share several features worth recognizing together:

FeatureCystic fibrosisBronchiectasis (in general)
PFT patternObstructiveObstructive
Key pathogen driving faster declinePseudomonas aeruginosa (primary CF pathogen)Pseudomonas aeruginosa (accelerates decline in any bronchiectasis)
Imaging distributionUpper lobe predominant bronchiectasisDistribution overall is an etiology clue (upper=CF, lower=aspiration, RML/lingula=MAI, central=ABPA)
Diagnostic test linkSweat chloride (pilocarpine iontophoresis) confirms CFSweat chloride is part of the standard bronchiectasis etiology workup
Airway clearance therapyChest percussion/postural drainage, hypertonic saline, dornase alfaMobilization of secretions (pharmacologic/mechanical) is a core treatment step
Clinical pearlIf a question describes bronchiectasis without stating the cause, cystic fibrosis should be high on your differential by base rates alone — and Pseudomonas aeruginosa is the organism to know for both accelerated decline in general bronchiectasis and as the primary pathogen driving CF lung disease specifically.
