1. Lung Anatomy & Bronchopulmonary Segments

Right lung3 lobes (upper, middle, lower); larger and shorter — the liver sits beneath it
Left lung2 lobes (upper, lower); smaller — accommodates the heart, which lies slightly left of midline
Total5 lobes, 18 bronchopulmonary segments (10 right, 8 left)

Each bronchopulmonary segment is a functionally and anatomically discrete unit with its own bronchus and arterial branch — its own independent blood supply and drainage.

Why segmental anatomy matters clinically — Because each segment is functionally independent, surgeons can remove a single segment (segmentectomy) or a whole lobe (lobectomy) for localized disease like lung cancer without sacrificing the rest of the lung. The segments also serve as surgical landmarks to help avoid critical structures.

Key Neighboring Structures

  • Aorta — arches over the left lung, descends near the vertebral column; injury during thoracic surgery risks life-threatening hemorrhage
  • Esophagus — posterior to the trachea and heart; relevant to esophageal cancer metastasis/compression and surgical injury risk
  • Heart — adjacent to the left lung (lingular segments lie close to it); relevant during cardiothoracic procedures like CABG, where lung retraction must be careful

Disease Localization by Segment (High-Yield)

TuberculosisApical segments (upper lobes)
Aspiration pneumoniaRight lower lobe — gravity-dependent distribution, and the right main bronchus is more vertical/wider than the left

Understanding segmental anatomy is also essential for interpreting chest X-rays/CT (localizing lesions, tumors, infections) and for assessing traumatic injury (localized pneumothorax, hemothorax, pulmonary contusion to a specific segment).

USMLE-Style Question

A bedridden patient with a diminished gag reflex aspirates gastric contents while lying supine. Which lung segment is most likely to be affected first?

A. Right lower lobe
B. Left upper lobe, apical segment
C. Lingula
D. Left lower lobe, superior segment only
E. Right middle lobe exclusively
Answer: C. Aspiration pneumonia classically localizes to the right lower lobe due to the gravity-dependent distribution of aspirated material combined with the right main bronchus being wider, shorter, and more vertical than the left.

2. Conducting vs. Respiratory Portions & Key Cells

Conducting portionNasal passages, pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi, bronchioles — transports air, no gas exchange
Respiratory portionRespiratory bronchioles, alveolar ducts, alveoli — site of actual gas exchange

Conducting Portion — Cell Types

  • Ciliated cells (trachea/bronchi) — rhythmic ciliary beating propels mucus and trapped debris/pathogens up and out
  • Goblet cells — interspersed among ciliated cells; secrete mucus that traps dust/microbes
  • Seromucous glands — in the submucosa; secrete both mucus and serous fluid, contributing to the protective mucus layer
  • Cartilage — C-shaped rings (trachea) / irregular plates (bronchi) maintain airway patency, preventing collapse
  • Smooth muscle — controls airway diameter (bronchoconstriction/dilation); central to asthma pathophysiology
  • Club (Clara) cells — non-ciliated, dome-shaped, abundant in bronchioles (replace goblet cells there); secrete a protein-rich fluid that acts like surfactant to reduce surface tension and prevent bronchiolar collapse; also have a detoxifying function against inhaled harmful substances

Respiratory Portion — Cell Types

  • Respiratory bronchioles — the transitional structure where conducting ends and respiratory portion begins; scattered alveoli along their walls allow some gas exchange; club cells still present here
  • Type I pneumocytes — thin squamous cells forming the blood-air barrier; make up ~95% of alveolar surface area but are a minority of alveolar cell number; cannot divide/regenerate
  • Type II pneumocytes — cuboidal cells covering only ~5% of alveolar surface area; secrete surfactant (stored in lamellar bodies prior to secretion); also serve as the progenitor cell that can divide to replace damaged Type I cells after injury
  • Alveolar macrophages — resident immune cells; phagocytose dust, bacteria, and other foreign material

Alveolar Septum

Thin wall between adjacent alveoli: a layer of Type I pneumocytes on each side, with capillary endothelial cells sandwiched between them, sharing a fused basal lamina — this minimal-diffusion-distance arrangement is what makes efficient gas exchange possible.

  • Pores of Kohn — small openings in the alveolar septa allowing collateral ventilation between adjacent alveoli (helps maintain ventilation if an airway becomes blocked)
  • Canals of Lambert — pathways between terminal bronchioles and adjacent alveoli, also aiding collateral air distribution
Clinical tie-ins — Asthma/COPD primarily affect the conducting portion (smooth muscle constriction + goblet cell mucus hypersecretion → airway narrowing). Neonatal RDS and ARDS both involve surfactant/alveolar dysfunction, just via different mechanisms (production failure vs. injury-driven loss, respectively — covered in detail in Section 9). Pulmonary fibrosis thickens/scars the alveolar septa, reducing elasticity and impairing gas exchange.

USMLE-Style Question

Following a diffuse alveolar injury, which cell type is primarily responsible for repopulating the alveolar lining and eventually differentiating to restore the blood-air barrier?

A. Type II pneumocytes
B. Alveolar macrophages
C. Club (Clara) cells
D. Type I pneumocytes
E. Goblet cells
Answer: D. Type II pneumocytes are the progenitor cells of the alveolar epithelium — they can divide and differentiate into Type I pneumocytes to repair the blood-air barrier after injury, which is why type II pneumocyte hyperplasia is a hallmark finding during the healing phase of diffuse alveolar damage.

USMLE-Style Question

A researcher blocks collateral airflow between adjacent alveoli in an animal model by chemically sealing small interalveolar openings. Which structure has been disrupted?

A. Canals of Lambert
B. Lamellar bodies
C. Pores of Kohn
D. Fused basal lamina
E. Respiratory bronchiole ostia
Answer: C. Pores of Kohn are small openings in the alveolar septa that allow collateral ventilation directly between adjacent alveoli. (Canals of Lambert instead connect terminal bronchioles to adjacent alveoli — a related but distinct collateral pathway.)

FeatureTrachea & BronchiBronchioles
EpitheliumPseudostratified ciliated columnar, with abundant goblet cellsSimple cuboidal to simple columnar; fewer ciliated cells; goblet cells largely absent (especially in terminal bronchioles) — replaced by club (Clara) cells
Submucosal glandsAbundant seromucous glandsAbsent
CartilageC-shaped rings (trachea, connected posteriorly by the smooth muscle trachealis) or irregular plates (bronchi)None — this absence is what allows bronchioles to be far more flexible and to change diameter more dramatically

Board-favorite distinguishing fact — The absence of cartilage and submucosal glands is what formally defines a bronchiole as distinct from a bronchus, not merely airway size.

USMLE-Style Question

A histology specimen shows an airway lined by simple cuboidal epithelium with scattered dome-shaped, non-ciliated secretory cells, no submucosal glands, and no cartilage, but a relatively prominent smooth muscle layer for its size. This airway is best classified as which structure?

A. Trachea
B. Main bronchus
C. Segmental bronchus
D. Alveolar duct
E. Bronchiole
Answer: D. Lack of cartilage and submucosal glands, cuboidal epithelium, club cells instead of goblet cells, and a proportionally prominent smooth muscle layer are all defining features of a bronchiole.

4. How X-Rays Work

  1. Generation: in the X-ray tube, a heated cathode (–) emits electrons, accelerated by a high-voltage field toward the anode (+, usually tungsten). Electron collision with the anode converts kinetic energy into X-rays.
  2. Interaction with the body: tissues absorb X-rays to varying degrees. Dense tissue (bone) absorbs more → appears white. Soft tissue (muscle/organs) absorbs less → intermediate gray. Air-filled regions (lungs) absorb the least → appear darkest.
  3. Detection/image formation: remaining X-rays strike a detector (film or digital). More X-rays hitting film = darker film (in traditional systems, this produces a "negative" image — bone white, air-filled areas dark). Digital detectors convert X-rays to electrical signals processed into a digital image.
  4. Interpretation: the image is a map of radiographic densities that physicians read systematically to detect fractures, infection, tumors, etc.
Density-to-appearance rule — Bone (white) > soft tissue/fluid (gray) > fat (darker gray) > air (black). Every abnormal finding on a CXR is ultimately explained by tissue of the "wrong" density showing up somewhere.

X-rays involve ionizing radiation — lead aprons and minimizing unnecessary exposure protect both patients and staff.

5. Systematic CXR Approach

Always use a systematic approach (the way pilots use a checklist) so nothing gets missed. One structured mnemonic: A³BCDEFGHI.

LetterCheck
A — Assessment of qualityPatient position (PA/AP/lateral); inspiration (should see 10–11 posterior ribs); exposure (good lung detail, spine visible); rotation (clavicle-to-vertebra distance roughly equal bilaterally)
B — Bone & soft tissueSymmetry, fractures, osteoporosis, metastatic lesions; soft tissue for foreign bodies, edema, subcutaneous air
C — CardiacHeart size <50% of chest diameter on PA film (<60% on AP); shape, calcifications, prosthetic valves
D — DiaphragmsPosition (right normally slightly higher than left, due to the liver); shape (flat in COPD); free air beneath the diaphragm
E — EffusionCostophrenic angle sharpness; lateral film for small posterior effusions; infiltrates (interstitial vs. alveolar), masses, consolidation, air bronchograms, pneumothorax, bronchovascular markings; fissures for thickening/fluid
F — (continued, fissures/fields)Evaluate major/minor fissures
G — Great vesselsAortic size/shape; pulmonary vessel outlines
H — Hilar & mediastinumHilar lymphadenopathy, calcifications, masses (left hilum normally higher than right); mediastinal widening (aortic dissection); tracheal deviation (mass effect/tension pneumothorax)
I — ImpressionSynthesize all findings into a diagnostic impression — also serves as a built-in double-check

PA vs. AP Film

PA (posteroanterior)Performed in radiology; beam travels posterior→anterior to the detector; heart is close to the film → smaller, more accurate cardiac shadow
AP (anteroposterior)Bedside, for sick/immobile patients; beam travels anterior→posterior; heart is farther from film → magnified/larger cardiac shadow; often lower quality (poor cooperation/inspiration)
Board trap — Never call "cardiomegaly" confidently on an AP film alone — the AP projection inherently magnifies the cardiac silhouette. Always check the film type first.

Good Inspiration vs. Poor Inspiration

Good inspiration: 10–11 posterior ribs visible, taller/narrower cardiac silhouette, lungs appear darker (more aerated). Poor inspiration: fewer visible ribs, wider/shorter cardiac silhouette (falsely suggests cardiomegaly), lungs appear denser/whiter (can mimic infiltrates).

Lateral & Lateral Decubitus Views

  • Lateral film: adds a second (orthogonal) plane — CXR is inherently a 2D study, so PA + lateral together give a 3D sense of lesion location. Evaluate the retrosternal airspace, heart, aorta, hemidiaphragms (right normally higher), and posterior costophrenic angles (small effusions often seen here first).
  • Lateral decubitus: useful for detecting a small pneumothorax (look for absent lung markings in the nondependent lung) or confirming a small pleural effusion (fluid layers out along the dependent surface when free-flowing).

USMLE-Style Question

A critically ill ICU patient receives a portable chest film at the bedside. The cardiac silhouette appears enlarged compared to a film taken one week earlier in the radiology department. What is the most likely explanation?

A. New pericardial effusion
B. The bedside film was an AP projection, which magnifies the cardiac shadow
C. The patient developed new cardiomyopathy
D. Rotation artifact from patient positioning
E. Poor film exposure technique
Answer: B. Bedside films are AP projections; because the heart is farther from the detector, its shadow is magnified compared to a PA film taken in the radiology department — a common source of apparent (but false) cardiomegaly in portable films.

7. Atelectasis (Collapsed Lung)

In adults, acquired atelectasis falls into three mechanisms — know the mediastinal shift direction for each, since it's a classic testable discriminator.

TypeMechanismMediastinal Shift
ResorptionAirway obstruction — mucus plug, foreign body, tumorToward the involved (collapsed) lung
CompressionExternal pressure — e.g., elevated diaphragm, pleural effusion, pneumothoraxAway from the involved lung
ContractionFibrosis of lung or pleuraIrreversible
Logic, not memorization — Resorption atelectasis shrinks lung volume, so surrounding structures (mediastinum) get pulled toward the loss of volume. Compression atelectasis is caused by something pushing in from outside (fluid, air, a raised diaphragm), which pushes the mediastinum away from that added volume/pressure. All atelectasis carries a risk of secondary infection.
Resorption atelectasis — RUL
The outlined right upper lobe shows volume loss and increased density from airway obstruction (mucus, foreign body, or tumor) causing that lobe to collapse — with the mediastinum shifting toward it.
Linear (plate-like) atelectasis
Thin, band-like opacities (arrows) — a common finding in surgical patients who are splinting from pain and taking shallow breaths, causing small areas of basilar lung collapse. Incentive spirometry is used specifically to re-expand these collapsed areas.
Tension pneumothorax
Air trapped in the pleural space under increasing pressure compresses the ipsilateral lung and pushes the mediastinum toward the opposite side — a compression-type physiology and a genuine emergency (compresses the contralateral lung and impairs venous return).

USMLE-Style Question

A patient with a large, obstructing endobronchial tumor develops complete collapse of the right lower lobe. Which of the following would be the expected finding on chest X-ray?

A. Mediastinal shift away from the right side
B. No mediastinal shift, since obstruction doesn't affect mediastinal position
C. Mediastinal shift toward the right side
D. Irreversible mediastinal fixation
E. Contralateral diaphragm elevation only
Answer: C. Airway obstruction causes resorption atelectasis, and the mediastinum shifts toward the side of volume loss — in this case, toward the collapsed right lower lobe.

USMLE-Style Question

A patient develops a large pleural effusion compressing the adjacent lung. Which direction would the mediastinum be expected to shift, and why?

A. Toward the effusion, due to loss of lung volume
B. Away from the effusion, due to added extrinsic volume/pressure
C. No shift occurs with compression atelectasis
D. The shift is unpredictable and varies by patient
E. Toward the effusion, but only if bilateral
Answer: B. Compression atelectasis results from an external mass effect (fluid, air, elevated diaphragm) adding volume/pressure on one side, pushing the mediastinum away from the involved side — the opposite pattern from resorption atelectasis.

8. Pulmonary Edema

Two broad categories: hemodynamic edema and edema from alveolar wall injury (increased permeability).

Hemodynamic Edema

↑ Hydrostatic pressureLeft-sided heart failure (cardiogenic — most common cause overall), volume overload, pulmonary vein obstruction
↓ Oncotic pressureHypoalbuminemia, nephrotic syndrome, liver disease, protein-losing enteropathy
Lymphatic obstructionRare cause

Cardiogenic pulmonary edema preferentially affects the basal (dependent) lung regions, because hydrostatic pressure is greatest there. Chronic cases lead to "heart failure cells" — hemosiderin-laden alveolar macrophages, formed when chronically elevated capillary pressure forces red cells to leak into alveoli, where macrophages phagocytose and break them down (confirmed with a Prussian blue iron stain). Grossly, chronically congested lungs are dark, firm, and heavy — brown induration.

"Heart failure cells" — H&E
Numerous alveolar macrophages packed with golden-brown hemosiderin pigment, the histologic signature of chronic pulmonary venous congestion.
Same process — Prussian blue stain
The Prussian blue stain turns iron-containing hemosiderin a distinct blue, confirming these are hemosiderin-laden macrophages ("siderophages") rather than some other pigment.

Edema from Alveolar Wall (Microvascular) Injury

Direct injuryInfection (e.g., bacterial pneumonia), inhaled gases (high O2, smoke), aspirated liquid (gastric contents, near-drowning), radiation, lung trauma
Indirect injurySIRS, transfusion-related, drugs/chemicals
Undetermined originHigh altitude, neurogenic

If diffuse, alveolar wall injury progresses to ARDS/diffuse alveolar damage (Section 9).

Radiographic Signs

Kerley lines are thin, linear opacities from fluid/cellular infiltration of the pulmonary interstitium:

Kerley AConnective tissue near the bronchoarterial bundle distends
Kerley BDistended interlobular septa — classically peripheral
Kerley CReticular network of lines, not tied to a specific structure
Kerley lines on chest X-ray
Labeled example distinguishing the three Kerley line patterns — a classic radiographic sign of interstitial pulmonary edema.

Case Walkthrough

A 75-year-old male presents with several days of progressively worsening shortness of breath.

Cardiogenic pulmonary edema — case CXR
Findings: cardiomegaly (heart >50% of chest width), bilateral small pleural effusions, increased pulmonary vasculature, and increased interstitial infiltrates — together diagnostic of pulmonary edema. On subsequent decompensation, this patient's film showed worsening cardiomegaly, persistent effusions, increasing reticular/ground-glass change, and eventual intubation (endotracheal tube tip visible just above the carina). Follow-up CT showed posterior infiltrates, increased vasculature, and an old scar. At autopsy: capillaries engorged/prominent, with intra-alveolar granular pink transudate — confirming cardiogenic pulmonary edema.

USMLE-Style Question

An autopsy on a patient with long-standing congestive heart failure reveals lungs that are grossly dark, firm, and heavy. Microscopy shows numerous alveolar macrophages filled with golden-brown pigment. Which special stain would confirm the identity of this pigment?

A. Prussian blue
B. Congo red
C. Periodic acid-Schiff
D. Masson trichrome
E. Congo red with polarization
Answer: C. Prussian blue stains iron and would confirm that the golden-brown pigment within these "heart failure cells" is hemosiderin, a byproduct of chronic pulmonary venous congestion and red cell extravasation into alveolar spaces.

USMLE-Style Question

Which of the following is the most common overall cause of pulmonary edema?

A. Nephrotic syndrome
B. Bacterial pneumonia
C. High-altitude exposure
D. Left-sided heart failure
E. Lymphatic obstruction
Answer: D. Increased hydrostatic pressure from left-sided heart failure (cardiogenic pulmonary edema) is the most common cause of pulmonary edema overall.

9. ARDS, Diffuse Alveolar Damage & Acute Interstitial Pneumonia

Criteria for acute lung injury (non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema): acute onset of dyspnea, hypoxemia, bilateral infiltrates, and absence of primary left-sided heart failure. Mediators implicated: cytokines, oxidants, TNF, interleukins, TGF-β; susceptibility may be inheritable. This can progress to acute interstitial pneumonia or ARDS/diffuse alveolar damage (DAD).

Acute Interstitial Pneumonia (Hamman-Rich)ARDS (Diffuse Alveolar Damage)
CauseNo identifiable causative disorder (idiopathic)Occurs secondary to a severe underlying condition (sepsis, diffuse pulmonary infection, gastric aspiration, trauma, etc.)
DemographicsAverage age ~59, M=FAny age, driven by the underlying critical illness
CourseAcute respiratory failure after ~3 weeks of illnessRapid onset; profound dyspnea, tachypnea, cyanosis, respiratory failure
Mortality33–74%, usually within 2 monthsUp to 40%

ARDS Pathophysiology

Diffuse damage to alveolar capillary walls (mediated by numerous factors) → secondary loss of surfactant, plus development of a pro-thrombotic state (↑ tissue factor, ↓ protein C).

High-yield contrast — Neonatal hyaline membrane disease (neonatal RDS) results from a deficiency of surfactant production by immature Type II pneumocytes. Adult ARDS instead results from inflammatory damage to the alveolar epithelium and endothelium, which secondarily causes surfactant loss — same histologic endpoint (hyaline membranes), fundamentally different starting mechanism.

Conditions Associated with ARDS (Know Broad Categories)

  • Infection/sepsis: sepsis, diffuse pulmonary infections (viral, mycoplasma, Pneumocystis), miliary TB
  • Trauma: mechanical trauma/head injury, pulmonary contusion, near-drowning, fractures with fat embolism, burns
  • Inhalation/toxic injury: oxygen toxicity, smoke, irritant gases/chemicals, radiation
  • Drugs/overdose: heroin/methadone, aspirin, barbiturates, paraquat
  • Hematologic/systemic: transfusion-associated lung injury, DIC, pancreatitis, uremia, cardiopulmonary bypass, hypersensitivity reactions

Initial CXR may be normal in the first 24 hours — patients are typically already critically ill from the underlying process when ARDS becomes superimposed, and diffuse bilateral infiltrates develop as it progresses.

Diffuse alveolar damage — hyaline membranes
Eosinophilic (pink), fibrin-rich hyaline membranes (arrows) lining the alveolar walls — the histologic hallmark of the acute exudative phase of ARDS/DAD. These membranes are composed of fibrin-rich edema fluid mixed with necrotic epithelial cell debris.
Diffuse alveolar damage — healing stage
In the organizing/healing phase, thickened alveolar septa show Type II pneumocyte hyperplasia (enlarged, reactive nuclei) as these progenitor cells attempt to repopulate the denuded alveolar surface. This stage can either resolve completely or progress to interstitial fibrosis and chronic restrictive lung disease.

Case Walkthrough — Sepsis-Induced ARDS

A 30-year-old woman with diabetes develops a UTI, then left flank pain (pyelonephritis), then presents in septic shock (BP 70/40, HR 120) with labs showing DIC (schistocytes, thrombocytopenia, prolonged PT/PTT) and acute kidney injury; blood cultures grow gram-negative bacilli. CXR shows bilateral interstitial infiltrates. She dies on hospital day 2; at autopsy, lungs are heavy, firm, red, and boggy.

Sequence of eventsUTI → gram-negative septicemia → septic shock → DIC with hemolytic anemia → diffuse alveolar damage (ARDS) → renal failure → death
Cause of radiographic infiltratesProtein-rich edema fluid plus a pneumonic process
Composition of hyaline membranesFibrin-rich edema fluid with mixed necrotic and epithelial cell debris
If she had survivedType II pneumocyte hyperplasia, intra-alveolar fibrosis, thickened alveolar walls, interstitial fibrosis → possible chronic restrictive lung disease (though complete resolution is also possible)

USMLE-Style Question

Which of the following best distinguishes the pathogenesis of neonatal hyaline membrane disease from adult ARDS?

A. Neonatal disease is caused by inflammatory endothelial injury; adult ARDS by surfactant deficiency
B. Both conditions share an identical primary mechanism
C. Neonatal disease requires a pro-thrombotic state; adult ARDS does not
D. Neonatal disease is caused by surfactant deficiency from immature Type II pneumocytes; adult ARDS by inflammatory damage to the alveolar epithelium/endothelium
E. Neither condition produces hyaline membranes
Answer: D. Neonatal RDS results primarily from a developmental deficiency of surfactant production; adult ARDS results from inflammatory/toxic damage to the alveolar-capillary membrane, with surfactant loss occurring secondarily. Both can produce hyaline membranes histologically despite the different root cause.

USMLE-Style Question

Several weeks later, biopsy shows thickened alveolar septa with hyperplastic cells lining alveolar walls that have enlarged, reactive-appearing nuclei. What is the most likely identity of these proliferating cells?

A. Alveolar macrophages
B. Type I pneumocytes
C. Club cells

Answer: E. Type II pneumocyte hyperplasia is the expected finding during the healing/organizing phase of diffuse alveolar damage, as these progenitor cells proliferate to repopulate the denuded alveolar epithelium.

10. Cystic Fibrosis

The most common lethal genetic disease affecting Caucasians (~1 in 2,500 live births; ~5% heterozygous carrier rate). Autosomal recessive. Caused by mutation of the CFTR (cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator) gene on chromosome band 7q31.2, encoding an epithelial chloride channel protein. Results in widespread disorder of epithelial fluid transport across exocrine glands and the respiratory, GI, and reproductive epithelial linings.

CFTR Mutation Classes

Six mutation classes exist; the most commonly tested is Class II: abnormal protein folding, processing, and trafficking. The most common single mutation, ΔF508 (present in ~70% of CF alleles), falls into this class — it causes defective protein folding in the Golgi/endoplasmic reticulum, so the protein never reaches the cell surface.

Channel Physiology (High-Yield, Frequently Tested)

  • CFTR normally inhibits the epithelial sodium channel (ENaC)
  • In CF, loss of CFTR function → ENaC activity increases in most epithelia (e.g., lung, intestine) → excess sodium (and secondary water) reabsorption → dehydrated, thickened mucus
  • Exception — sweat ducts: ENaC activity decreases in CF sweat glands → sweat with abnormally high chloride and sodium content (the basis of the sweat chloride test)
  • CFTR also affects outwardly rectified chloride channels, inwardly rectified potassium channels, ATP transport, and mucus secretion; it mediates bicarbonate transport — its loss causes more acidic luminal fluid, which promotes mucin precipitation
Sweat gland is the exception to the rule — Everywhere else in the body, CF causes ENaC hyperactivity (→ dehydrated secretions). In the sweat duct specifically, ENaC activity is decreased, which is why sweat chloride (and sodium) go UP instead of down — the opposite direction from every other CF-affected epithelium.

Respiratory Findings

  • Chronic sinopulmonary disease with persistent colonization by classic CF pathogens — early: S. aureus and non-typeable H. influenzae; later (progressively more common with age): Pseudomonas aeruginosa (present in ~80% of patients by age 20)
  • Chronic cough/sputum production, chronic bronchitis with mucosal hyperplasia/hypertrophy
  • Persistent radiographic abnormalities: bronchiectasis, atelectasis, infiltrates, hyperinflation
  • Airway obstruction: wheezing, air trapping, viscous mucus, obstructive pneumonias/abscesses
  • Nasal polyps in ~25% — test any child with recurrent sinonasal polyps for CF
  • Digital clubbing; can progress to cor pulmonale
  • ~80% of CF patients ultimately die from cardiorespiratory disease

GI, Nutritional & Reproductive Findings

  • Meconium ileus (neonatal presentation)
  • Pancreatic insufficiency in 85–90% — exocrine gland atrophy and fibrosis, sometimes leaving only islet cells (→ CF-related diabetes from islet destruction)
  • Chronic hepatic disease; failure to thrive with fat-soluble vitamin deficiencies (A, D, E, K)
  • Salivary gland dysfunction (squamous metaplasia, glandular atrophy)
  • Salt-loss syndrome: acute salt depletion, chronic metabolic alkalosis
  • Males: obstructive azoospermia — ~95% infertile; can present as isolated congenital bilateral absence of the vas deferens in an otherwise mild/atypical CF phenotype
Atypical/non-classic CF — Mutations other than ΔF508, or modifier genes, can produce a mild phenotype (e.g., an adult presenting only with infertility from bilateral vas deferens absence plus recurrent pancreatitis) that goes undiagnosed for years — CF exists on a spectrum, not just the classic severe childhood presentation.

Diagnosis

Requires one or more characteristic phenotypic features (or a family history of CF in a sibling/parent/child, or a positive newborn screen) PLUS one of: elevated sweat chloride on two occasions; intermediate sweat chloride + two identified CFTR mutations; or intermediate sweat chloride + abnormal nasal epithelial ion transport.

Prognosis & Treatment

Current median survival is ~30.8 years; predicted survival for those born in 2018 is 44–50 years — a reflection of steadily improving management.

USMLE-Style Question

A researcher studying CFTR channel physiology in sweat gland epithelium notes a paradoxical finding compared to airway epithelium. What is the key difference in CF sweat ducts?

A. ENaC activity increases, just as it does in the airway
B. CFTR is not expressed in sweat glands at all
C. ENaC activity decreases, leading to elevated sweat sodium and chloride
D. Bicarbonate transport is unaffected in sweat glands
E. Sweat becomes hypotonic in cystic fibrosis
Answer: C. Unlike most CF-affected epithelia (where loss of CFTR increases ENaC activity, causing sodium/water hyperabsorption and dehydrated secretions), sweat ducts show decreased ENaC activity in CF — resulting in reduced sodium (and chloride) reabsorption back into the body, producing abnormally salty sweat, the basis of the sweat chloride diagnostic test.

USMLE-Style Question

A 19-year-old CF patient has been colonized with an increasing burden of a specific gram-negative organism over the past several years, now affecting about 80% of similarly-aged CF patients. Which organism is this?

A. Staphylococcus aureus
B. Non-typeable Haemophilus influenzae
C. Streptococcus pneumoniae
D. Pseudomonas aeruginosa
E. Mycobacterium tuberculosis
Answer: D. While S. aureus and non-typeable H. influenzae are common early colonizers in CF, Pseudomonas aeruginosa becomes progressively more prevalent with age, present in approximately 80% of CF patients by age 20, and antibiotic regimens in CF must specifically cover it.

USMLE-Style Question

A CF patient's most common CFTR mutation results in a protein that folds improperly and is degraded before ever reaching the cell membrane. Which class of CFTR mutation does this represent?

A. Class I — no protein synthesis
B. Class II — abnormal protein folding, processing, and trafficking
C. Class III — defective channel regulation
D. Class IV — defective chloride conductance
E. Class V — reduced protein synthesis
Answer: B. The ΔF508 mutation (present in ~70% of CF alleles) is a Class II mutation, causing defective protein folding in the endoplasmic reticulum/Golgi such that the CFTR protein never reaches the cell surface.

Club (Clara) cellsBronchioles; secrete protective/surfactant-like fluid; detoxify inhaled substances; replace goblet cells distally
Type I vs. II pneumocytesType I = 95% surface area, gas exchange, can't divide. Type II = 5%, surfactant (lamellar bodies), progenitor cell
Pores of Kohn / Canals of LambertCollateral ventilation: alveolus-to-alveolus / bronchiole-to-alveolus, respectively
Bronchiole vs. bronchusBronchiole: no cartilage, no submucosal glands, club cells not goblet cells
X-ray density ruleBone (white) > soft tissue/fluid > fat > air (black)
PA vs. AP filmAP (bedside) magnifies heart size; PA (radiology dept.) is accurate
Good inspiration10–11 posterior ribs visible
Cardiac silhouette<50% chest width on PA; <60% on AP
Resorption atelectasisAirway obstruction; mediastinum shifts TOWARD collapse
Compression atelectasisExternal pressure; mediastinum shifts AWAY from involved lung
Contraction atelectasisFibrosis; irreversible
Cardiogenic pulmonary edemaMost common cause = left heart failure; basal predominance; heart failure cells (hemosiderin, Prussian blue+)
Kerley B linesDistended interlobular septa, peripheral — sign of interstitial edema
Neonatal RDS vs. adult ARDSNeonatal = surfactant deficiency (immature Type II cells); Adult = inflammatory alveolar-capillary injury
ARDS hallmarkHyaline membranes (fibrin + necrotic epithelial debris); healing = Type II pneumocyte hyperplasia
Acute interstitial pneumoniaLike ARDS but idiopathic (no causative disorder); ~59y average age; high mortality
CF gene/chromosomeCFTR, chromosome 7q31.2, autosomal recessive
ΔF508~70% of CF alleles; Class II mutation — misfolded protein degraded in ER/Golgi
CFTR/ENaC relationshipCFTR inhibits ENaC; CF → ↑ENaC almost everywhere (dehydrated mucus) EXCEPT sweat ducts (↓ENaC → salty sweat)
CF early vs. late pathogenEarly: S. aureus, nontypeable H. influenzae; Late: Pseudomonas aeruginosa (~80% by age 20)
CF pancreas85–90% exocrine insufficiency; can progress to CF-related diabetes
CF male infertility~95%; obstructive azoospermia / congenital bilateral absence of vas deferens
CF diagnosisPhenotype/family history/newborn screen + abnormal sweat chloride (or CFTR mutations / abnormal nasal ion transport)
CF median survival~30.8 years currently; ~44–50 years predicted for those born 2018
