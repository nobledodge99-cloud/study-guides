Normal EKG Basics & 12-Lead Reference

Before getting into pathology, get comfortable with what a genuinely normal tracing is showing you — every abnormality in this guide is defined as a deviation from this baseline. An EKG (electrocardiogram) is simply a recording of the heart's electrical conduction pathway from the surface of the skin. That pathway always fires in the same order: the sinoatrial (SA) node fires first (it's the heart's natural pacemaker), the impulse spreads across both atria, pauses briefly at the atrioventricular (AV) node, then races down the bundle of His into the left and right bundle branches, and out through the Purkinje fibers to depolarize the ventricles. Each of these steps produces one specific wave or segment on the tracing:

Wave/segmentWhat it represents
P waveAtrial depolarization (both atria contracting)
PR intervalStart of atrial depolarization → start of ventricular depolarization; includes the AV node's built-in delay, which lets the atria finish contracting before the ventricles begin
QRS complexVentricular depolarization (the ventricles contracting) — by far the largest deflection, since ventricular muscle mass dwarfs atrial muscle mass
ST segmentEnd of ventricular depolarization → start of repolarization; normally flat/isoelectric, which is exactly why ischemia shows up here first as elevation or depression
T waveVentricular repolarization (the ventricles electrically "resetting" before the next beat)
QT intervalStart of the QRS → end of the T wave; represents the entire span of ventricular electrical activity, contraction and recovery combined

The paper itself

Standard EKG paper runs at 25 mm/sec and is printed on a grid of small and large (bold) boxes that measure both time (horizontal axis) and voltage (vertical axis):

Box sizeTime (horizontal)Voltage (vertical)
Small box (1×1 mm)0.04 sec0.1 mV
Large/bold box (5×5 mm)0.2 sec0.5 mV

Five large boxes = 1 full second. Always confirm you're looking at standard 25 mm/sec paper before calculating a rate — some tracings are deliberately run at 50 mm/sec to spread out arrhythmias for easier viewing, which will make the rate look artificially slow if you don't catch it.

Put it all together and a genuinely normal 12-lead tracing has: a sinus rate of 60–100 bpm, a P wave before every QRS complex, a normal axis, normal intervals, and orderly R wave progression across the chest (precordial) leads — all defined below.

What each lead group "should" show

The 12 leads are just 12 different camera angles on the same electrical event — six limb leads (I, II, III, aVR, aVL, aVF) looking at the heart from the frontal plane, and six precordial/chest leads (V1–V6) looking at it from the horizontal plane, sweeping from the right ventricle (V1) across to the left ventricle (V6).

Lead groupNormal appearance
I, II (limb)Upright P, upright QRS, upright T
aVREverything negative — it looks at the heart "backwards"; the one lead where negative = normal
III, aVLVariable/biphasic — near-perpendicular to the main depolarization vectors, so small deflections are expected here
aVFUpright P and QRS (inferior view)
V1–V2Predominantly negative QRS (rS pattern) — facing the right ventricle, small or no septal Q
V3–V4Transition zone — R and S waves roughly equal ("R = S" point)
V5–V6Predominantly positive QRS (qR pattern), tallest R waves, small normal septal q wave, upright T

The steady rise in R wave height from V1→V6 is called R wave progression — loss of this pattern (poor R wave progression) is itself a clue worth flagging (prior anterior MI, LVH [left ventricular hypertrophy], lead misplacement, COPD [chronic obstructive pulmonary disease]).

Fast normal-vs-abnormal gut check
Scan in this order: rate/rhythm obviously normal? → P wave present and upright in II, negative in aVR? → QRS narrow with orderly R wave progression V1→V6? → ST segments isoelectric, T waves upright where R waves are tall? If all four are "yes," you're looking at a genuinely normal tracing and the answer likely lives in the vignette, not the strip (or it's a trick "normal EKG" answer choice).

The Master Flowchart

Every EKG question follows the same skeleton. Work down this order every single time — don't jump to "it's probably X" before you've walked the sequence. The vignette almost always tells you which branch to lean toward before you even look at the strip.

Abbreviations used from here on
SVT = supraventricular tachycardia; PSVT = paroxysmal SVT; AVNRT = atrioventricular nodal reentrant tachycardia; AVRT = atrioventricular reentrant tachycardia; VT = ventricular tachycardia; VF = ventricular fibrillation; AFib = atrial fibrillation; MAT = multifocal atrial tachycardia; WPW = Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome; BBB = bundle branch block (RBBB = right BBB, LBBB = left BBB); RAD/LAD = right/left axis deviation; RVH/LVH = right/left ventricular hypertrophy; STEMI/NSTEMI = ST-elevation/non–ST-elevation myocardial infarction; S1Q3T3 = a classic (though nonspecific) pulmonary embolism pattern — an S wave in lead I, a Q wave in lead III, and an inverted T wave in lead III. Why this order matters
Rate and rhythm tell you whether you're even looking at a tachyarrhythmia, a block, or a "normal-rate" tracing where the real story is in the ST segment. Axis and intervals are quick checks that can immediately flag WPW, a fascicular block, or QT-prolonging drug exposure before you've even scrutinized the ST segment. Save ST/T interpretation for last — it's the most time-consuming and the most likely to be the actual answer the question is testing.

Step 1 — Rate

Confirm paper speed is standard 25 mm/sec before doing anything else — a doubled speed (50 mm/sec) will make everything look artificially slow if you don't catch it.

CategoryRate
Bradycardia<60 bpm
Normal sinus rate60–100 bpm
Tachycardia>100 bpm

Fastest method (lecturer-preferred): find an R wave sitting on/near a bold line, then count down 300 – 150 – 100 – 75 – 60 – 50 at each successive bold line until the next R wave lands.

Key exception
Not every abnormal rate is pathologic — trained athletes may have resting rates as low as 35–40 bpm. Let the vignette (symptoms, hemodynamic status) tell you whether a rate needs treatment.

Step 2 — Rhythm & the P-QRS Relationship

Ask the four rhythm questions every time: (1) Are normal P waves present? (2) Narrow or wide QRS? (3) What's the relationship between P and QRS? (4) Regular or irregular?

Worked example A 68-year-old with COPD is tachycardic with an irregularly irregular rhythm. You see clearly organized P waves in lead II, but they change shape from beat to beat — at least 3 distinct morphologies. → Multifocal atrial tachycardia — the COPD is your vignette anchor; confirm by scanning any lead with good P wave visibility for morphology changes, not just checking R-R regularity (which alone would be indistinguishable from AFib).

Step 5 — ST Segment, T Waves & Ischemia/Infarction Localization

This is usually where the actual diagnosis lives. Once you suspect ischemia, your job is to (1) confirm criteria for a ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) — as opposed to a non–ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), which shows normal or only subtle ST/T changes and is diagnosed mainly by troponin — (2) localize the territory, and (3) find the reciprocal changes that confirm it isn't an artifact.

Reciprocal changes = your confirmation step
Don't stop at seeing elevation in one territory — glance at the mirror-opposite leads. Finding reciprocal depression is what separates a real STEMI from an early-repolarization look-alike or pericarditis (which shows diffuse concave elevation with PR depression and no reciprocal changes).

STEMI evolution timeline (useful for "how many hours/days ago" questions)

Hyperacute T waves → ST elevation ("tombstone") → ST/T merge → (post-reperfusion) ST returns to baseline, Q wave develops, R wave amplitude lost → T wave inverts/normalizes, but the Q wave persists permanently as scar evidence.

Worked example Chest pain, new ST elevation in II, III, aVF with reciprocal depression in I and aVL. → Inferior STEMI, RCA territory. Next step in a real patient: check right-sided leads (V4R) for RV involvement and avoid nitrates if RV infarct is present (preload-dependent).

Putting it all together
Every real EKG vignette is really asking: "Does the clinical picture match a pattern I can verify on the strip?" Let the history pick your leading hypothesis, then use the flowchart above (rate → rhythm → axis → intervals → ST/T) to confirm or refute it in the specific leads that matter — you rarely need to interpret all 12 leads in equal depth to answer one question.
