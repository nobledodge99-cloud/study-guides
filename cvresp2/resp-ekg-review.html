Framework Refresher — Just the Pulmonary-Relevant Pieces

Still run the full sequence: RATE → RHYTHM → AXIS → INTERVALS/QRS → CONDUCTION → ST–T/QT → SYNTHESIS. The pieces below are the ones that actually change when the lungs (not the heart) are the primary problem.

Axis & Right-Heart Clues

Right axis deviation (I negative, aVF positive) is the single biggest tip-off that something on the right side of the heart is under pressure or volume overload — think chronic lung disease, acute PE, or pulmonary hypertension before assuming a primary cardiac cause.

Conduction: Where RBBB Fits

PatternECG clueWhy it matters here
RBBBQRS ≥0.12s; RSR′ ("rabbit ears") in V1–V2; wide S in I/V6A conduction abnormality (not a rhythm) that reflects delayed RV activation — can be chronic (cor pulmonale, congenital) or acute (PE causing sudden RV strain)

Hypertrophy & Enlargement — the Right Side

RVH: right axis deviation, tall R in V1 (R>S), dominant S in V6, possible RV strain (T inversion/ST depression V1–V3, II, III, aVF). RAE (P pulmonale): increased-amplitude, normal-duration P wave, axis often ≥+90° — caused by pulmonary HTN, cor pulmonale, or chronic lung disease. Contrast this with the left-sided findings (LVH, LAE/P mitrale) you already know from systemic hypertension and mitral valve disease — those are NOT primarily driven by lung pathology.
One-line discriminator: if the EKG is pushing you rightward (axis, P wave, ventricular voltage) and the vignette doesn't explain it with tricuspid or pulmonic valve disease, think lungs first — COPD/chronic hypoxia, pulmonary hypertension, cor pulmonale, or acute PE.

Case-Based EKGs

The vignettes from the Master Guide that are built around a pulmonary mechanism

Pentamidine & a Prolonged QT in PJP PneumoniaCIS I · Case 8Hypoxic lung infection + drug effect

48 y/o female admitted with hypoxia, diagnosed with PJP pneumonia, given two doses of pentamidine; a routine post-dose EKG is obtained.
Rate100 bpm
RhythmSinus tachycardia
Axis+30°
Key IdentifiersQT prolongation; no infarction
Leads to Key In OnAny lead for QT/QTc measurement
High-yield stem/vignette clues: Pentamidine (used for PJP pneumonia, classically in advanced HIV/immunosuppression) is a textbook drug cause of acquired QT prolongation — the downstream risk is Torsades de Pointes, not AV block or WPW.
Why it's here: PJP pneumonia is the pulmonary diagnosis driving the whole stem — hypoxia from the pneumonia plus a QT-prolonging treatment (pentamidine) is the pairing to recognize, even though the EKG finding itself (QT prolongation) is a drug effect rather than a direct lung finding.

Afib After a Cross-Country Drive — S1Q3T3 & Acute PECIS II · Case 1Acute PE / right heart strain

72 y/o male with a 15-year history of well-controlled COPD, sudden palpitations and dyspnea the day after a cross-country drive (up to 14 hrs/day). EKG one year ago was normal.
Rate80 bpm
RhythmAtrial fibrillation
Axis+30°
Key IdentifiersAbsent P waves, irregularly irregular R–R; no infarction, no hypertrophy
Leads to Key In OnLead I for a prominent S wave, lead III for a Q wave + T-wave inversion (S1Q3T3) if PE is suspected
High-yield stem/vignette clues: Long car ride (prolonged immobility → DVT risk) + new-onset AFib-like presentation + dyspnea is a classic acute pulmonary embolism setup — not a COPD flare, not an MI, not renal failure or pericarditis. Having well-controlled COPD in the history is a distractor, not the diagnosis.
The pattern to know: S1Q3T3 (prominent S in I, Q in III, inverted T in III) is a classic-but-insensitive sign of acute PE and acute right heart strain — most patients with PE do NOT show it, so treat it as a pattern-recognition pearl, not a rule-out test. New-onset AFib/atrial arrhythmia is itself a common EKG finding in acute PE from acute right atrial stretch.

RBBB with LAFB (Bifascicular Block)CIS II · Rapid Fire #4RBBB

Rate80 bpm
RhythmNormal sinus
Axis−60°
Key IdentifiersWidened QRS with RSR′ in V1–3 (RBBB); LAD with qR in I/aVL and rS in II/III/aVF (LAFB)
Leads to Key In OnV1–V3 for RBBB; I/aVL vs. II/III/aVF for the fascicular block axis
A bifascicular block (RBBB + LAFB) — watch for progression toward complete heart block. Recognize the RBBB pattern itself (rabbit ears, wide terminal S) independent of the fascicular block riding along with it.

RBBB with LPFB (Bifascicular Block)CIS II · Rapid Fire #12RBBB + right axis shift

Rate100 bpm
RhythmSinus tachycardia
Axis+110°
Key IdentifiersRight axis deviation + S1Q3 pattern, plus RSR′ in V2 = RBBB with LPFB
Leads to Key In OnV2 for RSR′; I and III for the S1Q3 axis shift
Another bifascicular block pattern; unopposed right-sided forces shift the axis rightward. Note the S1Q3 pattern here is from the fascicular block, not PE — context (acute dyspnea/immobility vs. an incidental conduction finding) is what separates the two.

Reference Atlas

Didactic patterns, by category — the ones with a direct pulmonary tie-in

Multifocal Atrial Tachycardia (MAT)

Rate>100 bpm
RhythmChaotic/irregular — "wandering atrial pacemaker" at a faster rate
Identifiers≥3 distinct P-wave morphologies
High-yield stem clues: Elderly patient with COPD or CHF and an irregular tachycardia with multiple P-wave shapes = MAT. If MAT shows up outside a COPD/CHF context, consider PE instead.
Rarely life-threatening. Consider theophylline toxicity as a cause. Treat the underlying lung disease; avoid beta-blockers if there's significant COPD.

Right Bundle Branch Block (RBBB)

RBBB vs. LBBB — Side-by-Side Comparison

IdentifiersCompares V1/V6 morphology for RBBB vs. LBBB against a normal QRS

RBBB — Criteria

IdentifiersQRS >0.12s; RSR′ ("rabbit ears") in V1–V2 with R′ larger than the initial R; wide reciprocal S waves in I, aVL, V5–V6; T wave opposite the terminal QRS deflection
Leads to Key In OnV1–V2 for rabbit ears; I, aVL, V5–V6 for reciprocal wide S waves
High-yield stem clues: "Rabbit ears" in V1 is the classic RBBB visual, though sometimes you'll only see a tall R wave rather than a true RSR′.

RBBB with Left Axis Deviation (Bifascicular Block)

IdentifiersRBBB pattern plus LAD from a concurrent left anterior fascicular block (LAFB): qR in I/aVL, rS in II/III/aVF
High-yield stem clues: RBBB + a fascicular block = bifascicular block; watch for progression toward complete heart block.
Why RBBB matters for the respiratory exam: RBBB is the conduction pattern most associated with right-heart pathology — it can be an incidental/chronic finding (congenital, idiopathic) or reflect real right ventricular strain from cor pulmonale, chronic lung disease, or acute PE. Pair it with the axis and P-wave findings below before assuming an isolated conduction quirk.

Right Atrial Enlargement (P Pulmonale)

IdentifiersIncreased amplitude of the early (right atrial) portion of the P wave; P-wave duration stays normal; P-wave axis may shift rightward toward/beyond +90°
Causes: tricuspid regurgitation, pulmonary valve stenosis, pulmonary hypertension, cor pulmonale (chronic lung disease) — pressure or volume overload of the right atrium. "P pulmonale" is named for exactly this association with chronic lung disease.

Right Ventricular Hypertrophy (RVH)

IdentifiersRight axis deviation; narrow QRS; tall R in V1 (R>S, >7mm or R/S>1); dominant S in V6 (>7mm or R/S<1); RV strain (T inversion/ST depression in V1–V3, II, III, aVF); RBBB or incomplete RBBB may coexist
Most common causes: pulmonary disease and congenital heart disease. This is the EKG signature of chronic cor pulmonale — the right ventricle hypertrophying against chronically elevated pulmonary vascular resistance.

Respiratory–EKG Cross-Reminders

Chronic right-heart picture (cor pulmonale / long-standing lung disease): right axis deviation + P pulmonale (RAE) + RVH criteria ± RBBB. Builds up slowly — think COPD, pulmonary fibrosis, chronic pulmonary hypertension, OSA with chronic hypoxemia.
Acute right-heart picture (PE): sudden dyspnea/pleuritic pain + new right axis shift, new RBBB, new S1Q3T3, sinus tachycardia, or new-onset atrial arrhythmia (AFib/flutter) from acute right atrial stretch. The EKG is frequently normal or shows only sinus tachycardia — a normal EKG never rules out PE.
COPD/CHF + irregular tachycardia with ≥3 P-wave morphologies → MAT, not AFib. Avoid beta-blockers if COPD is significant; treat the underlying pulmonary process.
Hypoxic/critically ill pulmonary patients are at higher risk for drug-induced QT prolongation (e.g., pentamidine for PJP pneumonia) — always scan the QT/QTc on these tracings, independent of the primary pulmonary diagnosis.
Don't over-call it: an isolated RBBB or a mild rightward axis in an otherwise asymptomatic patient is frequently incidental/congenital. Only build the "lung disease" story when the vignette (chronic dyspnea, hypoxia, known COPD/pulm HTN) and the exam findings (RAE + RVH + RBBB together) actually support it.
