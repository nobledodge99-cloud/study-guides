    1. What Is Shock?

    Shock occurs when blood flow to vital organs drops below the rate required to sustain their critical functions — hypoperfusion. This leads to widespread cellular and tissue hypoxia. Left uncorrected, this cascades to cellular death, multiorgan failure (MOF), and death. Crucially, the early stages of shock are reversible — a delay in diagnosis or treatment is what allows progression to irreversible damage.

    Shock is most easily flagged by hypotension — systolic blood pressure <90 mmHg or MAP <65 mmHg — but blood pressure and blood flow are not the same thing:

    • Patients with normally low resting blood pressure may tolerate lower pressures before true hypoperfusion sets in.
    • Chronically hypertensive patients may develop hypoperfusion at higher-than-expected pressures.

    So the assessment of shock always requires both an objective vital sign measurement and a subjective assessment of end-organ perfusion (mental status, urine output, skin findings, capillary refill).

    Mechanistic definition Shock is characterized by decreased oxygen delivery and/or increased oxygen consumption and/or inadequate oxygen utilization at the tissue level — the common final pathway is cellular and tissue hypoxia, regardless of the upstream cause.

    Four vs. Five Categories — a Terminology Note

    Sources classify shock slightly differently. The classic ScholarRx breakdown gives five categories, splitting neurogenic shock out on its own:

    Mnemonic "New Cells Down and Out (from) Hypoxia" = Neurogenic, Cardiogenic, Distributive, Obstructive, Hypovolemic

    StatPearls instead uses four broad categories — distributive, hypovolemic, cardiogenic, obstructive — folding neurogenic (and endocrine) shock in as subtypes of distributive shock, since both share the hallmark of decreased systemic vascular resistance. Know both framings: exam questions may test either the "5 distinct types" model (useful for hemodynamic reasoning, since neurogenic shock behaves very differently from vasodilatory septic shock) or the "distributive umbrella" model.

    Check Your Understanding

    1. Shock is most easily flagged clinically by which threshold?

    Correct: SBP <90 mmHg or MAP <65 mmHg. These are the standard hypotension thresholds used to flag possible shock, though they must be interpreted alongside perfusion signs, not in isolation.

    2. A chronically hypertensive patient is more likely to develop true tissue hypoperfusion:

    Correct. Chronically hypertensive patients have adapted to higher baseline perfusion pressures, so their organs can become underperfused at blood pressures that would look "normal" or only mildly low on a standard chart — this is why perfusion assessment (mental status, urine output, skin) matters alongside the number itself.

    3. Which classification scheme places neurogenic shock as a subtype of distributive shock rather than as its own separate category?

    Correct: the StatPearls 4-category model. It groups neurogenic (and endocrine) shock under the distributive umbrella since SVR falls in both. The 5-type model keeps neurogenic shock separate because its cardiac response (no compensatory tachycardia) is fundamentally different.

    2. Clinical Presentation

    Patients with shock appear acutely ill and may be obtunded or unconscious. Common findings include:

    • Hypotension
    • Tachycardia, tachypnea
    • Syncope or presyncope (especially orthostatic)
    • Altered mental status / obtundation
    • Shortness of breath, chest pain, abdominal pain
    • Cool, clammy, mottled extremities; cool/blue/painful fingers and toes
    • Oliguria
    • Metabolic acidosis, hyperlactatemia

    Sometimes the cause is obvious (massive GI bleed, high fever, burns, trauma); often it is not, and the patient presents as undifferentiated shock — the diagnosis of shock is clear, but the underlying etiology is not yet known.

    Check Your Understanding

    1. A trauma patient has unilateral pleuritic chest pain, absent breath sounds on that side, tracheal deviation away from it, and distended neck veins. This picture best fits:

    Correct: tension pneumothorax. Absent breath sounds plus tracheal deviation away from the affected side is the classic pneumothorax pattern; JVD reflects impaired venous return from the elevated intrathoracic pressure.

    2. The Beck triad (↑JVP, muffled heart sounds, hypotension) is a diagnostic clue for:

    Correct: cardiac tamponade. Pericardial fluid restricts cardiac filling and contraction, producing muffled heart sounds, elevated JVP, and hypotension — often with pulsus paradoxus as well.

    3. Flushing, urticaria, oral/facial edema, wheeze, and inspiratory stridor minutes after a bee sting point to:

    Correct: anaphylactic shock. This is the classic IgE-mediated hypersensitivity presentation, with rapid onset after antigen exposure (here, an insect sting).

    3. Stages of Shock

    Regardless of trigger, shock tends to progress through a recognizable pattern (these are markers of progression, not discrete stages every patient exhibits):

    Trigger Insult → Compensated Shock → Uncompensated Shock → End-Organ Failure / Irreversible Shock

    Compensated (Pre-Shock)

    The body maintains a normal blood pressure while responding to the trigger. Sympathetic activation increases heart rate and contractility (↑ cardiac output) and drives peripheral vasoconstriction (↑ SVR). Clinically: tachycardia, restlessness/rapid breathing, cool distal extremities with delayed capillary refill.

    Uncompensated Shock

    Compensatory mechanisms fail and blood pressure falls. End-organ dysfunction becomes apparent — syncope, confusion, declining urine output, weak/thready pulses. Anaerobic glycolysis increases as oxygen delivery fails, producing metabolic (lactic) acidosis; respiratory rate increases to compensate.

    End-Organ Failure / Irreversible Shock

    Vital organs fail sequentially. Heart rate, MAP, and respiration become unresponsive to therapy. Urine output stops, neurologic function fades, coma and death follow.

    SystemLate-stage signs
    NervousMental status change (confusion → loss of consciousness)
    CardiovascularDiaphoresis, ↑HR, ↓BP
    Respiratory↑Respiratory rate
    ExtremitiesCyanosis
    Renal↓Urine output (oliguria/anuria)

    Check Your Understanding

    1. Which finding best marks the transition from compensated to uncompensated shock?

    Correct. Tachycardia and cool extremities are compensatory (still "compensated" stage) findings. Confusion, syncope, and falling urine output signal that compensation is failing and end-organ dysfunction is beginning — the hallmark of uncompensated shock.

    2. Increasing reliance on anaerobic glycolysis during uncompensated shock produces which acid-base picture?

    Correct. Tissue hypoxia forces cells into anaerobic glycolysis, raising serum lactate and causing metabolic acidosis; respiratory rate increases as the body tries to compensate by blowing off CO2.

    3. In the end-organ failure/irreversible stage of shock, which parameters become unresponsive to therapy?

    Correct. By this final stage, heart rate, MAP, and respiratory rate all enter free fall and no longer respond to treatment, alongside falling urine output and fading neurologic activity — this stage is generally irreversible.

    4. Physiologic Parameters

    All types of shock ultimately alter one or more of: mean arterial pressure (MAP), cardiac output (CO), and systemic vascular resistance (SVR).

    Mean Arterial Pressure

    MAP = diastolic BP + ⅓(systolic BP − diastolic BP) = diastolic BP + ⅓(pulse pressure)

    Cardiac Output

    CO = Heart Rate × Stroke Volume

    Stroke volume is decreased by:

    • Decreased preload — decreased end-diastolic pressure/volume (EDV)
    • Increased afterload — increased resistance to ejection, mainly driven by SVR (also estimated by MAP)
    • Decreased contractility — reduced force of contraction, independent of preload/afterload

    Echocardiography can noninvasively estimate cardiac output.

    Systemic Vascular Resistance

    SVR = MAP / Cardiac Output

    SVR is mainly determined by arteriolar tone, regulated largely by the sympathetic nervous system.

    Arterial Oxygen Content & Delivery

    Since shock ultimately reflects failed tissue oxygenation, oxygen delivery is a key concept:

    Oxygen delivery = Cardiac Output × Arterial Oxygen Content
    Arterial O2 content (Ca) ≈ Hb × 1.36 × SaO2

    Arterial oxygen content can fall because of: decreased cardiac output, anemia (↓Hb), or hypoxemia (↓SaO2, usually from lung disease) — treating these three is the primary ICU focus.

    High-yield detail In septic shock, tissues often fail to extract oxygen normally, producing a small arteriovenous oxygen difference despite adequate delivery — a clue that distinguishes distributive shock's oxygen-utilization defect from the delivery-limited defect of the other shock types.

    Check Your Understanding

    1. A patient's blood pressure is 120/60 mmHg. What is their MAP?

    Correct: 80 mmHg. MAP = diastolic + ⅓(pulse pressure) = 60 + ⅓(60) = 60 + 20 = 80 mmHg.

    2. Systemic vascular resistance is calculated as:

    Correct: SVR = MAP / cardiac output. The other formulas listed are cardiac output (HR × SV) and oxygen delivery (CO × Ca), respectively — easy to mix up on a timed exam.

    3. A patient develops significant anemia. Which shock-relevant parameter is most directly reduced as a result?

    Correct: arterial oxygen content. Ca ≈ Hb × 1.36 × SaO2, so a falling hemoglobin directly lowers oxygen content (and therefore oxygen delivery) even if cardiac output and SaO2 are unchanged.

    5. The Five Types of Shock

    Each type differs in how it perturbs cardiac output, afterload (SVR), and preload (EDV). Figure logic to memorize: which variable is the primary derangement (P), and which variables move to compensate.

    5a. Distributive Shock — ↓ Systemic Vascular Resistance

    Primary problem: extensive systemic vasodilation → ↓SVR → ↓MAP. The body compensates by driving up cardiac output (↑HR and ↑SV via sympathetic activation), which rapidly empties the heart and decreases EDV (preload).

    Clinically: hypotensive, but skin may be warm even at the extremities (unlike the cold extremities of the other shock types) because of peripheral vasodilation.

    Causes

    Septic Shock HIGH YIELD

    Sepsis = life-threatening organ dysfunction from a dysregulated host response to infection. Septic shock = sepsis + hypotension requiring vasopressors + lactate >2 mmol/L despite adequate fluid resuscitation. Most common US pathogens: gram-positive bacteria (e.g., Streptococcus pneumoniae, Enterococcus). Mechanism: bacterial components (e.g., LPS) trigger cytokine release (TGF, IL-1, IFN-γ, IL-6, IL-12, IL-18) → endothelial damage → release of vasodilators (nitric oxide, prostaglandin I2).

    Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS)

    A whole-body inflammatory response to stress — infectious (sepsis) or noninfectious (trauma, pancreatitis, burns, fat/air/amniotic fluid embolism). Presentation varies: tachycardia, hyper- or hypothermia, very high or very low WBC. Can progress to multiorgan failure via complement activation, cytokines, and humoral/cellular immune activation.

    Anaphylactic Shock

    IgE-mediated hypersensitivity reaction on re-exposure to an antigen. Basophil/mast cell degranulation releases histamine, prostaglandins, and leukotrienes → vasodilation + bronchospasm. Onset within seconds to minutes. Common triggers: drugs (antibiotics, NSAIDs), food, insect stings, latex. Clinically also produces flushing, urticaria, oral/facial edema, wheeze, stridor.

    Adrenal Crisis / Endocrine Shock

    Major deficiency of cortisol and/or aldosterone — classically abrupt withdrawal of chronic corticosteroids (Addisonian crisis). These hormones are critical for maintaining SVR; their loss produces hypotension/shock. StatPearls also groups myxedema (severe hypothyroidism) under this "endocrine shock" umbrella.

    Check Your Understanding

    1. Septic shock is defined, in part, by a serum lactate level above:

    Correct: >2 mmol/L. Septic shock requires sepsis + hypotension needing vasopressors to maintain MAP + a lactate >2 mmol/L despite adequate fluid resuscitation.

    2. The most common gram-positive pathogens implicated in US sepsis/septic shock include:

    Correct. The other options list gram-negative bacteria or fungi, which are also important sepsis pathogens but are not the most common gram-positive culprits.

    3. Abrupt discontinuation of chronic corticosteroid therapy can precipitate which type of distributive shock?

    Correct: adrenal crisis. Sudden loss of exogenous corticosteroid support (on top of suppressed endogenous adrenal function) causes a profound cortisol/aldosterone deficiency, impairing the ability to maintain SVR.

    5b. Cardiogenic Shock — ↓ Cardiac Output

    Primary problem: the heart fails to pump effectively → ↓cardiac output → ↓MAP. The body compensates by raising SVR (arteriolar constriction) — this produces high afterload despite low blood pressure, which can be clinically confusing. Remember: BP depends on the combination of SVR, CO, and volume — here, profoundly low CO dominates the picture.

    Preload (EDV) rises because the failing heart cannot adequately eject the blood returning to it, leading to pulmonary edema.

    Clinically: tachycardia, dyspnea (pulmonary edema), S3 heart sound.

    Causes

    • Cardiomyopathies — acute MI affecting >40% of the LV, MI with multivessel CAD, RV MI, fulminant dilated cardiomyopathy, cardiac arrest (myocardial stunning), myocarditis
    • Arrhythmias — both tachy- and bradyarrhythmias
    • Mechanical — severe aortic or mitral insufficiency, papillary muscle/chordae tendineae rupture, ventricular free wall aneurysm rupture
    • Valvular disease
    Clinical correlation — treatment logic Inotropes (e.g., dobutamine) increase contractility but raise arrhythmia risk. Vasodilators lower SVR and cardiac workload but can drop the diastolic pressure needed to perfuse the coronary arteries (which fill only in diastole) — so vasodilators are not used alone in cardiogenic shock. Some agents (dobutamine, milrinone) act as combined inotrope/vasodilators but still risk further decompensating blood pressure.

    Check Your Understanding

    1. A 60-year-old male has an MI, is initially stabilized, then develops a ventricular arrhythmia hours later and loses consciousness. BP falls to 90/60 mmHg, pulse 130 bpm, respirations 20/min, lungs clear. A change in which parameter contributes most to this hypotension?

    Correct: contractility. This patient is in cardiogenic shock. The drop in cardiac output can result from low contractility, low preload, or high afterload — here, the MI has caused loss of myocytes, greatly lowering contractility. Arterial oxygen content would drop if there were pulmonary edema causing poor oxygen exchange, but there's no evidence of that here (lungs are clear). Heart rate is increased as a compensation for the low MAP — a high heart rate raises MAP, it doesn't cause the hypotension. Preload is reduced in hypovolemic shock, not cardiogenic shock — in cardiogenic shock preload is increased, from compensatory renal reabsorption of salt and water. Vascular radius is proportional to SVR; decreased SVR is the primary distributive-shock mechanism, but in cardiogenic shock SVR increases to compensate for the failing heart.

    2. Which class of drug should generally NOT be used alone to treat cardiogenic shock, given the risk of dropping coronary perfusion pressure?

    Correct: vasodilators. Coronary arteries fill only during diastole, so lowering SVR/diastolic pressure with a vasodilator alone can worsen coronary perfusion in an already-failing heart. Combined inotrope/vasodilator agents (dobutamine, milrinone) are used more cautiously for this reason.

    3. A patient has central, crushing chest pain radiating to the left arm/neck, hypotension, and tachycardia. Echo shows decreased stroke volume. Which pattern of shock is this?

    Correct: cardiogenic shock. Classic ACS presentation with decreased stroke volume on echo points to primary pump failure — falling cardiac output driving the hypotension, with reflex tachycardia and typically rising preload.

    5c. Hypovolemic Shock — ↓ Preload

    Primary problem: loss of intravascular volume → ↓venous return → ↓preload (EDV) → ↓stroke volume → ↓cardiac output. The body compensates by raising SVR (afterload) via arteriolar constriction and raising HR/contractility — but because preload is low, cardiac output stays low despite these compensations.

    Clinically: cold extremities (vasoconstriction), pallor; a bleeding source is often evident.

    Hemorrhagic Causes

    • GI bleed (upper or lower — variceal bleed, portal hypertensive gastropathy, peptic ulcer, diverticulosis), trauma
    • Vascular etiologies — aortoenteric fistula, ruptured AAA, tumor eroding into a major vessel
    • Spontaneous bleeding with supratherapeutic anticoagulation

    Non-Hemorrhagic Causes

    • GI losses — vomiting, diarrhea, NG suction, drains
    • Renal losses — diuretic-induced, hypoaldosteronism
    • Skin/insensible losses — burns, Stevens-Johnson syndrome/TEN, heatstroke, fever
    • Third-spacing — pancreatitis, cirrhosis, bowel obstruction, trauma

    Check Your Understanding

    1. Which of the following is a non-hemorrhagic cause of hypovolemic shock?

    Correct: severe diarrhea. The other three options are hemorrhagic causes. Non-hemorrhagic causes include GI losses (vomiting/diarrhea), renal losses, skin/insensible losses, and third-spacing.

    2. In hypovolemic shock, what happens to SVR, and why?

    Correct. As preload and cardiac output fall, the body compensates by raising SVR (afterload) through arteriolar constriction — this is why hypovolemic shock patients have cold, vasoconstricted extremities.

    3. A trauma patient has pale, cool extremities and an obvious actively bleeding wound. Which type of shock is most likely?

    Correct: hypovolemic shock. Cool, pale extremities reflect compensatory vasoconstriction from falling preload/volume, and an obvious bleeding source strongly supports a hemorrhagic hypovolemic picture.

    5d. Neurogenic Shock — Everything Down

    Caused by failure of the autonomic nervous system, classically from traumatic brain or spinal cord injury. Autonomic failure disrupts all compensatory responses simultaneously:

    • Arterial vasodilation → low SVR/afterload
    • Loss of sympathetic drive to the heart → low HR and contractility → low CO
    • Loss of venous tone → low EDV/preload
    Distinguishing feature Neurogenic shock is the only type of shock without compensatory tachycardia — heart rate is normal or even low — because the sympathetic nervous system itself cannot mount the response. This is the key differentiator from distributive (septic/anaphylactic) shock, which is otherwise hemodynamically similar (both have ↓SVR).

    Some sources classify neurogenic shock as a subtype of distributive shock (since SVR falls in both), but its cardiac response is fundamentally different — hence the "5 types" model keeps it separate.

    Clinically: neurologic signs/symptoms with a history of brain or spinal cord trauma; notably warm, dry skin below the level of injury (vasodilated) without reflex tachycardia.

    Check Your Understanding

    1. Which type of shock is unique in that patients typically do NOT develop compensatory tachycardia?

    Correct: neurogenic shock. Because the autonomic nervous system itself has failed, the sympathetic surge that produces tachycardia in every other type of shock cannot occur — heart rate stays normal or even falls despite hypotension.

    2. What is the classic trigger for neurogenic shock?

    Correct: traumatic brain or spinal cord injury. This disrupts the autonomic pathways controlling vascular tone, heart rate, and venous tone all at once.

    3. A patient with a cervical spinal cord injury has BP 78/40, HR 58, and warm, dry extremities below the injury level. This pattern is best explained by:

    Correct: neurogenic shock. Hypotension without reflex tachycardia, plus warm/dry (vasodilated) skin rather than the cold, clammy skin of most other shock types, is the classic neurogenic shock picture following spinal cord injury.

    5e. Obstructive Shock — Blockage → ↓ Cardiac Output

    Circulation through the heart is physically blocked. Two subtypes, each with a distinct mechanism:

    Pulmonary (Vascular) Obstructive Shock

    Increased resistance within the pulmonary circulation prevents blood from reaching the left heart. This raises RV preload but lowers LV preload (and stroke volume), so total cardiac output drops. Classic cause: massive pulmonary embolism; also severe pulmonary hypertension.

    Structural (Mechanical) Obstructive Shock

    Resistance arises within the heart itself, restricting ventricular filling. This decreases both right and left ventricular preload and cardiac output. Causes:

    • Tension pneumothorax — air in the pleural space collapses the lung; high intrathoracic pressure prevents venous return and cardiac filling
    • Cardiac tamponade — pericardial fluid prevents normal cardiac contraction/filling
    • Restrictive cardiomyopathy, constrictive pericarditis

    In both subtypes: HR rises to compensate for low CO, and SVR/afterload rises in response to low CO — superficially resembling cardiogenic shock. The key difference: in obstructive shock, LV preload is decreased because the obstruction itself is preventing venous return/filling, whereas in cardiogenic shock, preload rises because a healthy venous system keeps returning blood to a heart that can't eject it.

    Clinically: dominated by the underlying cause — leg swelling/dyspnea/pleuritic chest pain (PE); absent breath sounds, tracheal deviation (pneumothorax); distant heart sounds + JVD (tamponade).

    Check Your Understanding

    1. A truck driver develops a pulmonary embolism after a long-haul DVT. If he develops shock from the PE, which of the following is most likely?

    Correct: increased SVR. Cardiac output falls (impaired LV filling from the PE), so the body compensates by raising SVR/afterload, just as in cardiogenic shock. Heart rate rises to compensate, and LV preload actually falls — blood can't get past the obstruction to fill the left heart.

    2. A trauma patient has a tension pneumothorax. Which physical exam pattern is expected?

    Correct. Muffled heart sounds + pulsus paradoxus instead describe cardiac tamponade — a common distractor pairing on obstructive shock questions.

    3. How does LV preload in structural obstructive shock differ from cardiogenic shock, and why?

    Correct. This is the key hemodynamic distinguisher between the two: in obstructive shock the obstruction itself prevents venous return/filling (preload falls), while in cardiogenic shock a healthy venous system keeps returning blood to a heart that can't pump it out (preload rises).

    Check Your Understanding

    1. Which shock type is the only one where MAP, preload, SVR, and cardiac output are ALL listed as primary (P) derangements?

    Correct: neurogenic. Because autonomic failure knocks out every compensatory mechanism at once, all four variables fall together as primary derangements rather than one falling and the others compensating.

    2. A patient's workup shows high preload, low cardiac output, and high SVR. Which type of shock best fits this profile?

    Correct: cardiogenic shock. It's the only type on the comparison table with an increased preload (the failing heart can't eject the blood returning to it) alongside low CO and compensatory high SVR — hypovolemic and structural obstructive shock both have decreased preload instead.

    7. Complications

    Nearly all complications trace back to hypoperfusion/hypoxia of individual organ systems:

    • Cardiac: arrhythmias, cardiac arrest, myocardial infarction
    • GI: mesenteric ischemia and necrosis
    • Hematologic: disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)
    • Pulmonary: ARDS (noncardiogenic pulmonary edema/respiratory failure)
    • Renal: acute tubular necrosis → kidney failure → anuria
    • MSK: ischemic/necrotic digits, especially toes
    • Neurologic: stroke, ischemic injury, coma

    Check Your Understanding

    1. Widespread activation of the clotting cascade during severe shock can lead to which hematologic complication?

    Correct: DIC. Systemic hypoperfusion and inflammation can trigger widespread microvascular clotting that consumes clotting factors and platelets, paradoxically leading to bleeding as well.

    2. Noncardiogenic pulmonary edema and respiratory failure occurring as a complication of shock is termed:

    Correct: ARDS (acute/adult respiratory distress syndrome). This reflects diffuse alveolar-capillary injury from systemic hypoperfusion/inflammation rather than a cardiac cause of pulmonary edema.

    8. Evaluation & Diagnosis

    Resuscitation should never be delayed while searching for the etiology of undifferentiated shock. Evaluation happens in parallel with treatment.

    History & Physical

    Focused history (from patient/family if possible) plus review of outpatient records for baseline vitals, risk factors, and medications. Look for clues: chest pain, infectious symptoms, trauma, abdominal pain. Note febrile state or paradoxical bradycardia + hypotension in trauma as a clue pattern.

    Workup

    • Telemetry + 12-lead EKG — may show ACS, arrhythmia, or clues to tamponade/PE
    • Labs: CBC with differential, renal/liver function, serum lactate (key risk-stratification marker), cardiac biomarkers, D-dimer, coagulation panel, type & screen, blood/urine cultures, blood gas
    • Imaging: chest X-ray (source of infection, ARDS, pulmonary edema, widened mediastinum in aortic dissection); CT as indicated
    • Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS)/focused cardiac ultrasound — reduces diagnostic uncertainty and guides resuscitation at the bedside
    • Right heart (Swan-Ganz) catheter — measures right/left atrial pressure (preload), cardiac output, and allows precise SVR calculation in unclear cases. Used less often now (recognized risks, no mortality benefit) — best reserved for cases where volume status is genuinely uncertain and needs to guide fluid therapy.

    Check Your Understanding

    1. Which single lab value is emphasized as a key risk-stratification tool in undifferentiated shock?

    Correct: serum lactate. Elevated lactate reflects the anaerobic metabolism of tissue hypoperfusion and is used both to help diagnose shock and to track response to resuscitation.

    2. Right heart (Swan-Ganz) catheterization is used less often today primarily because:

    Correct. It remains useful when volume status is genuinely unclear and needs to guide fluid therapy, but routine use has declined given its risks and lack of proven mortality benefit.

    3. Which bedside diagnostic tool has been shown to reduce diagnostic uncertainty and guide resuscitation in undifferentiated hypotension?

    Correct: POCUS/focused cardiac ultrasound. It can be performed immediately at the bedside, unlike the other listed studies, which are either too slow or not appropriate for an acutely unstable patient.

    9. Management

    Overall goal: stabilize the patient and treat the underlying cause. Initial approach: secure airway/breathing (oxygen, intubation if needed), obtain IV or IO access (central access if peripheral access fails or prolonged pressors/large-volume resuscitation are anticipated).

    First Step (Undifferentiated Shock)

    Unless the patient has pulmonary edema, give an initial normal saline infusion to any hypotensive patient and observe the response — hypovolemic shock responds well. No response → ICU-level monitoring for further diagnosis and therapy.

    Type-Specific Management

    TypeManagement
    Septic shockAggressive fluids (30 mL/kg isotonic crystalloid within 3 hr, reassess and add more as needed); empiric antibiotics within 1 hr; if vasopressors needed, target MAP 65 mmHg; norepinephrine first-line, add vasopressin if refractory
    Anaphylactic shockAggressive IV crystalloids (4–6 L); stop offending agent; IM epinephrine, antihistamines, corticosteroids, nebulized albuterol
    Adrenal crisisJudicious fluids; IV dexamethasone
    Hypovolemic shockTwo large-bore IVs or central line; Trendelenburg position; aggressive isotonic crystalloids (2–4 L); PRBC transfusion if ongoing bleed; treat the underlying source; continue crystalloids ± vasopressors
    Obstructive shock (general)Judicious IV crystalloids; early norepinephrine (± vasopressin) if shock persists; continue fluids but monitor closely for worsening
    Massive PEThrombolysis. Caution: aggressive IV fluids can paradoxically worsen hypotension via RV dilation/septal bowing that compromises LV filling
    Tension pneumothoraxNeedle thoracostomy → tube thoracostomy
    Cardiac tamponadePericardiocentesis — even small-volume fluid removal can produce significant clinical improvement
    Cardiogenic shockACLS/cardioversion for unstable arrhythmia; judicious fluids only if no pulmonary edema; inotropes (dobutamine most common) or intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) if refractory; norepinephrine + inotropes if needed; thrombolysis/revascularization ± IABP for STEMI
    Neurogenic shockSaline; vasoconstrictors if needed
    Distributive vs. cardiogenic drug logic, at a glance Distributive → SVR is the problem → treat with pressors (norepinephrine, vasopressin). Cardiogenic → pump function is the problem → treat with inotropes (dobutamine). Hypovolemic/neurogenic → volume/preload is the problem → treat with fluids (± pressors in neurogenic once volume is addressed).

    Check Your Understanding

    1. A patient in septic shock requires vasopressor support. What is the target MAP, and what is the first-line agent?

    Correct: MAP 65 mmHg, norepinephrine first-line. Vasopressin is added if refractory to norepinephrine alone. Dobutamine is an inotrope for cardiogenic shock/concurrent cardiac dysfunction, not first-line pressor support.

    2. Why should IV fluids be given cautiously in a hypotensive patient with a suspected massive pulmonary embolism?

    Correct. An already-strained right ventricle can over-distend with aggressive fluids, bowing the septum into the LV cavity and further reducing LV filling and cardiac output — a paradoxical worsening of shock.

    3. What is the first-line treatment for anaphylactic shock?

    Correct: IM epinephrine. It reverses both the vasodilation and bronchospasm of anaphylaxis rapidly. Antihistamines, corticosteroids, and nebulized albuterol are adjuncts, not substitutes for epinephrine.

    10. Differential Diagnosis

    Undifferentiated shock's differential spans all four/five major categories above. Two additional considerations:

    • Patients can have a combination of shock syndromes simultaneously (e.g., septic + hypovolemic from poor oral intake).
    • Pharmacologic shock — vasodilation or myocardial depression from medications: benzodiazepines, beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, opioids, anticholinergics, sildenafil.

    Check Your Understanding

    1. Which of the following medication classes can cause "pharmacologic shock" via vasodilation or myocardial depression?

    Correct: beta-blockers. Along with calcium channel blockers, benzodiazepines, opioids, anticholinergics, and sildenafil, beta-blockers can produce shock-like hypotension through myocardial depression or vasodilation, independent of an infectious or hemorrhagic cause.

    2. Can a patient have more than one type of shock occurring simultaneously?

    Correct: yes. A patient can, for example, be septic (distributive) while also volume-depleted from poor oral intake (hypovolemic) — this is part of why undifferentiated shock can be diagnostically challenging.

    TypeApproximate mortality

    Septic shock survivors also carry significant long-term morbidity, often requiring long-term acute care or post-acute placement.

    Epidemiology Distributive shock is the most common overall type, followed by hypovolemic, then cardiogenic; obstructive shock is comparatively rare. Within distributive shock, septic shock is the most common subtype.

    12. Case Connection

    Case ST, a middle-aged female, arrives after a car crash. Blood pressure has been falling over 15 minutes despite IV fluids en route. Exam: BP 74/50 mmHg, HR 120 bpm, abdomen distended and firm, extremities cool.

    What's happening? ST is in hypovolemic shock from ongoing blood loss into her abdomen (hemoperitoneum) — a firm, distended abdomen after trauma is a red flag for internal hemorrhage. Her tachycardia and cool extremities reflect the expected compensatory response (↑SVR, ↑HR) to falling preload.

    Management: because preload must be restored, the rate of IV fluids is increased and blood transfusion is arranged; surgery is urgently consulted for the hemoperitoneum. She goes to the OR for hemorrhage control and recovers.
