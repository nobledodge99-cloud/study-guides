Part 1 — Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD)

COPD — overview & risk factors

COPD is a disease characterized by progressive, mostly irreversible airflow obstruction. Onset is typically middle-aged or elderly, 20-30 years after the causative exposure. COPD is the 3rd leading cause of mortality in the United States, with mortality increasing in both men and women.

Cigarette smoking — the dominant risk factor

By far the leading cause of COPD. Lung function decline relates to both the duration and intensity of smoking:

GroupFEV1 decline
Non-smokers (after age 30)~25 mL/year
Smokers~40 mL/year
"Rapid decliner" smokers (small %)~100 mL/year — can develop COPD in their 4th-5th decade

Reduced lung growth from maternal smoking in pregnancy, 2nd-hand childhood exposure, and adolescent smoking also increases later-life COPD risk. Other exposures: workplace dusts from mining, cotton mills, and grain-handling facilities.

Clinical pearlCOPD risk is a function of cumulative lifetime lung insult — it isn't only about the smoker themselves. In utero and childhood secondhand exposure lowers peak attained lung function, so the same later decline reaches the COPD threshold sooner.

Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency

A genetic risk factor for COPD, accounting for only 1-2% of COPD cases. Alpha-1 antitrypsin is a serine protease inhibitor secreted by the liver that protects lung tissue against neutrophil elastase and other serine proteases. Deficient patients are very susceptible to smoking-related lung damage.

  • Consider it in patients who are young at COPD onset
  • Also causes liver function test abnormalities and cirrhosis (the same protein, when misfolded, accumulates and damages hepatocytes)
Clinical pearlAlpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency is a two-organ disease: lung disease from a lack of antiprotease protection, and liver disease from accumulation of the misfolded protein itself. A young patient (30s-40s) with panacinar, lower-lobe-predominant emphysema and unexplained liver disease should make you think of this.

Emphysema

Definition: enlargement of the air spaces distal to the terminal bronchiole, with destruction of the alveolar walls. Caused by an imbalance of elastase-antielastase activity in the lung (too much elastase activity, too little antielastase protection).

Centriacinar vs. panacinar emphysema

TypeDistributionAssociation
Centriacinar (centrilobular)Affects respiratory bronchioles distal to the terminal bronchiole; remainder of the acinus is sparedOccurs with smoking
PanacinarAlveolar ducts and adjacent alveoli, with coalescence and bullae formationOccurs with smoking; classic/common in alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency

Most severe COPD patients have a combination of centriacinar and panacinar emphysema.

Clinical pearlCentriacinar = smoking, upper-lobe predominant. Panacinar = alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, lower-lobe predominant. Both patterns can also simply reflect heavy smoking, but panacinar in a young, lower-lobe-predominant pattern should trigger an alpha-1 antitrypsin workup.

Related airway changes

Chronic bronchitis and bronchiolitis: enlargement of bronchial mucous glands and increased epithelial goblet cell production, leading to cough and increased mucus production.

Pulmonary hypertension: hypoxemia causes vasoconstriction and increased pulmonary vascular resistance in small pulmonary arteries, leading to vascular remodeling — medial smooth muscle enlargement and intimal fibrosis.

Emphysema is defined by destruction of alveolar walls and enlargement of the airspaces distal to the terminal bronchiole. Most COPD patients have a mixture of both processes.

Lung mechanics, gas exchange & ventilatory drive in COPD

Elastic recoil & airway resistance

Elastic recoil is the lung's innate ability to deflate following inflation, produced by elastic fibers in the lung parenchyma plus surface tension at the alveolar air-liquid interface. Elastic recoil normally helps maintain the patency of small airways.

  • Elastic recoil is markedly decreased in COPD (destroyed alveolar walls = less tissue to recoil)
  • Airway resistance is increased in COPD
  • The sites of airflow obstruction are the distal airways <2mm diameter
Clinical pearlDecreased elastic recoil is why COPD patients dynamically collapse their small airways on exhalation (hence pursed-lip breathing and auto-PEEP) — it isn't only about mucus or bronchospasm the way it is in asthma.

Hypoxemia & hypercapnia

Mild hypoxemia can be detected in early COPD. Hypercapnia presents only in severe COPD. Ventilation-perfusion mismatching is common, secondary to uneven ventilation.

Altered ventilatory drive in COPD

Normally, the central chemoreceptors' response to rising PaCO2 is the primary drive to breathe. In patients with chronic, longstanding CO2 retention, the central chemoreceptors become relatively desensitized to CO2 over time, and hypoxemia (via peripheral chemoreceptors) becomes a more important driver of ventilation than it is in a normal person.

Clinical pearlThis is the mechanistic basis for the classic teaching about oxygen hazards in emphysema below — the patient's own drive to breathe has partly shifted from 'CO2 too high' to 'O2 too low.'

Hazards of high-flow oxygen in emphysema/COPD

In a patient whose ventilatory drive has shifted toward hypoxic drive, giving high-flow supplemental oxygen can blunt that hypoxic respiratory drive and worsen alveolar hypoventilation, raising PaCO2 further (CO2 narcosis risk). Additional contributing mechanisms taught alongside this: worsening of V/Q mismatch (releasing hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction in poorly ventilated units, diverting blood flow there) and the Haldane effect (oxygenated hemoglobin carries less CO2, releasing more dissolved CO2 into the blood).

Clinical pearlThe practical teaching point: titrate supplemental oxygen carefully in COPD/emphysema patients — the goal is adequate oxygenation (commonly SpO2 ~88-92% in stable COPD), not maximal oxygenation, to avoid precipitating hypoventilation and hypercapnic respiratory failure.

COPD — history & physical findings

History

Current or past cigarette use, dyspnea with slow progression, history of acute bronchitis, chronic cough, sputum production, and wheezing.

Physical findings

Barrel chest, prolonged expiratory phase, accessory muscle use, low/flattened diaphragm, distant heart sounds, diminished breath sounds, rhonchi, wheezing, cyanosis, pedal edema, distended jugular veins, hepatic congestion, and cachexia.

"Pink puffers" vs. "blue bloaters"

PhenotypeDominant processClassic picture
Pink pufferEmphysema-predominantThin, dyspneic, pursed-lip breathing, relatively well-oxygenated ("pink") at rest by working hard to breathe ("puffer")
Blue bloaterChronic bronchitis-predominantOverweight, cyanotic ("blue"), edematous/cor-pulmonale ("bloater"), more prone to hypoxemia/hypercapnia at rest
Clinical pearlThese are classic teaching phenotypes describing the two ends of a spectrum — most real COPD patients are a mixture. But it's a very testable pair of names, so know pink puffer = emphysema-type, blue bloater = chronic-bronchitis-type.

COPD — PFTs, staging & imaging

PFT staging (GOLD-style spirometric severity)

StageSeverityFEV1/FVCFEV1 (% predicted)

All stages share a reduced FEV1/FVC ratio <70% — this is the obstructive signature. Severity is then graded by how low the FEV1 itself is.

Chest x-ray

Hyperinflation, flattened diaphragms, increased retrosternal space, and bullae. Can be normal in mild-to-moderate COPD.

Differential diagnosis

Asthma, bronchiectasis, bronchiolitis obliterans.

COPD — treatment

Smoking cessation

Decreases the rate of FEV1 decline in mild-to-moderate COPD back down to the rate seen in nonsmokers. Reduces both respiratory and cardiovascular deaths.

Bronchodilators

Short-acting agents are used as rescue inhalers. An oral (metered-dose) inhaler with a spacer is just as effective as nebulizer treatment. Long-acting bronchodilators provide improvement in 10-15% of patients and reduce exacerbations by 15-20%. Tiotropium once daily is superior to salmeterol twice daily.

ClassShort-actingLong-acting
B2-adrenergic agonists (inhaled)Albuterol, levalbuterolSalmeterol, formoterol
Anticholinergics (inhaled)Ipratropium bromideTiotropium

Methylxanthines

Theophylline (oral): antagonizes adenosine receptors, increases cAMP; a weak bronchodilator that requires close monitoring — narrow therapeutic window, numerous side effects, numerous drug interactions.

Phosphodiesterase-4 inhibitor

Roflumilast: can increase FEV1 by ~50 mL and reduce exacerbations in moderate-to-severe COPD, even in patients already on tiotropium.

Corticosteroids

Inhaled: reduces COPD exacerbations by 15-20%; added benefit when combined with a long-acting beta agonist.

Systemic (chronic, low-dose prednisone): no proven benefit, and carries many adverse effects.

Oxygen

Chronic hypoxemia can lead to pulmonary hypertension and cor pulmonale. Long-term oxygen extends life in patients with chronic hypoxemia (mortality benefit).

Other measures

  • Immunizations: influenza, pneumococcus, and COVID-19
  • Pulmonary rehabilitation: exercise/endurance training improves walking distance

Surgical options

ProcedureUse
Lung volume reduction surgerySevere emphysema involving the upper lobes
Lung transplantationSevere incapacitation, no other major comorbidities; median survival ~5 years
Clinical pearlOnly two therapies are proven to reduce mortality in COPD: smoking cessation and long-term oxygen therapy in the chronically hypoxemic subgroup. Everything else (bronchodilators, inhaled steroids, roflumilast, pulmonary rehab) improves symptoms/exacerbations/quality of life without a proven mortality benefit — a favorite exam distinction.

Check yourself

Exam-style question

AInhaled corticosteroids and roflumilast

COPD exacerbation & prognosis

Definition: a combination of dyspnea, cough, and productive sputum that is worse than the patient's usual stable state.

Causes

Respiratory infections are thought to cause most exacerbations:

TypeOrganisms
Bacterial (most common)Haemophilus influenzae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Moraxella catarrhalis
Bacterial (less common, but seen in severe disease / recent hospitalization or intubation)Pseudomonas aeruginosa, enteric bacilli
ViralRhinoviruses, influenza, parainfluenza, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV)

Airborne pollution is another recognized trigger.

Assessing & managing a hospitalized exacerbation

Assess dyspnea, accessory muscle use, ABG, hemodynamic stability, and mental alertness. Management:

  • Antibiotics when cough and purulent sputum are present
  • Systemic corticosteroids
  • Increase short-acting bronchodilator frequency
  • Oxygen to maintain saturation >90%
  • Noninvasive positive-pressure ventilation
  • Intubation and mechanical ventilation, if needed

Prognosis

  • Only half of patients with an FEV1 of 40% (predicted) will survive 5 years
  • Smoking cessation reduces mortality in mild-to-moderate COPD
  • Oxygen reduces mortality in the subset with chronic hypoxemia

Check yourself

Exam-style question
A patient hospitalized for a COPD exacerbation has not been recently hospitalized or intubated. Which organism is the LEAST likely cause of their exacerbation?
AHaemophilus influenzae
BStreptococcus pneumoniae
CMoraxella catarrhalis
DRhinovirus
EPseudomonas aeruginosa
Explanation: Pseudomonas aeruginosa and enteric bacilli are less common causes of COPD exacerbation overall, and are seen especially in patients with more severe disease or a history of recent hospitalization/intubation — neither of which applies here. H. influenzae, S. pneumoniae, and M. catarrhalis are the classic bacterial causes, and rhinovirus is a classic viral cause.

Part 2 — Asthma

Asthma — definition & epidemiology

Asthma is a clinical syndrome of unknown etiology with 3 distinct components:

  1. Recurrent airway obstruction — resolves spontaneously or with treatment
  2. Airway hyper-responsiveness — exaggerated bronchoconstriction in response to stimuli that have little or no effect on non-asthmatic patients
  3. Airway inflammation
Clinical pearlThese 3 components are a favorite short-answer/definition question: obstruction, hyper-responsiveness, and inflammation. All three, not just bronchospasm, are part of the disease definition.

Population affected

  • A common disorder, affecting ~8% of adults
  • Boys > girls before puberty; after puberty, women > men
  • Most cases start before age 25, but asthma can occur at any age
  • One of the most common reasons for seeking medical care: ~15 million outpatient visits/year and ~2 million hospitalizations/year

Check yourself

Exam-style question
Which three components together define the clinical syndrome of asthma?
ABronchospasm, mucus plugging, and infection
BRecurrent airway obstruction, airway hyper-responsiveness, and airway inflammation
CFixed airflow obstruction, eosinophilia, and cor pulmonale
DAtopy, elevated IgE, and nasal polyps
ECough, wheeze, and dyspnea
Explanation: Asthma is defined by 3 distinct components: recurrent airway obstruction (reversible spontaneously or with treatment), airway hyper-responsiveness (exaggerated bronchoconstriction to stimuli that wouldn't affect a non-asthmatic), and airway inflammation.

Asthma — pathologic changes & airway obstruction

Pathologic changes by severity

SeverityPathology
MildEdema and hyperemia of the mucosa, plus mucosal infiltration with mast cells, eosinophils, and lymphocytes
ModerateChemokines — eotaxin, RANTES, macrophage inflammatory protein-1 alpha, and interleukin-8 — drive inflammation and smooth muscle constriction
SevereHypertrophy and hyperplasia of airway glands and smooth muscle, leading to severe airway wall thickening

What actually causes the airflow obstruction

A combination of three processes:

  1. Constriction of airway smooth muscle
  2. Thickening of the airway epithelium
  3. Liquids (secretions) in the airway
Clinical pearlIt's not bronchospasm alone — chronic, poorly controlled asthma causes structural airway remodeling (smooth muscle and gland hypertrophy/hyperplasia) that itself narrows the airway independent of any acute bronchospasm, which is part of why early anti-inflammatory control matters.

Asthma triggers & mediators

Triggers

Atopy, allergy, cold air, smoking or smoke in the environment, pollution, climate changes, emotion, medications, occupational exposures, and food.

Samter's triad (aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease)

A classic clinical triad linking three findings: asthma + aspirin/NSAID sensitivity + nasal polyps (chronic rhinosinusitis). Ingestion of aspirin or other NSAIDs in these patients can trigger a severe bronchospastic reaction, thought to relate to shunting of arachidonic acid metabolism toward the leukotriene pathway when cyclooxygenase is inhibited.

Clinical pearlSamter's triad is a very testable named pattern: NSAID-sensitive asthma + nasal polyps. Avoid aspirin/NSAIDs in these patients; leukotriene pathway drugs are a rational treatment choice given the underlying mechanism.

Asthma mediators

MediatorSource / mechanism
AcetylcholineReleased from intrapulmonary motor nerves; stimulates M3 muscarinic receptors, causing airway smooth muscle constriction
HistamineReleased from mast cells — plays only a minor role
Leukotrienes and lipoxinsDerived from lipoxygenation of arachidonic acid, released from target-cell membrane phospholipids during cellular activation
Nitric oxideProduced by airway epithelial cells and inflammatory cells in the asthmatic lung; high levels found during an asthma attack
Clinical pearlAcetylcholine acting on M3 receptors is the pharmacologic rationale for anticholinergic bronchodilators (e.g., ipratropium) in obstructive airway disease. Exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) testing, used clinically to track airway inflammation, follows directly from the 'high NO during an attack' fact above.

Asthma — history, categories & physical exam

History

Dyspnea, cough, wheezing, and anxiety. May be exercise-induced, aspirin-induced, extrinsic (allergen-induced), or intrinsic (unknown cause). Some patients present only with cough, hoarseness, or difficulty sleeping through the night. Rapid changes in temperature or humidity may precipitate an attack. Always consider occupational exposures.

Asthma categories (by symptom frequency)

CategorySymptom pattern
Mild intermittent≤2 days/week, or ≤2 nights/month
Mild persistent>2 days/week but <1x/day, or >2 nights/month
Moderate persistentDaily, or >1x/week
Severe persistentContinual during the day, and frequent at night
Clinical pearlCategory drives step therapy below — the more frequent/severe the baseline symptom pattern, the higher the starting treatment step.

Physical examination

Often normal between attacks. During an attack:

  • Vital signs: tachypnea (RR often 25-40/min), tachycardia, pulsus paradoxus
  • Accessory muscle use, hyperinflation, prolonged expiratory phase
  • Wheezing is loudest during expiration but can also be heard during inspiration; wheezing is polyphonic
  • Decreased breath sounds = severe obstruction (a worrying sign, not reassurance)
  • Ominous signs: inability to speak or drink, fatigue, drowsiness, confusion, cyanosis

Exam-style question

Explanation: Moderate persistent asthma is defined by daily symptoms, or symptoms more than once a week (without being continual, which would be severe persistent). Mild intermittent is ≤2 days/week or ≤2 nights/month; mild persistent is >2 days/week but <1x/day, or >2 nights/month; severe persistent is continual during the day and frequent at night.

Asthma — outpatient step therapy

Asthma management is personalized: assess, adjust, and review response on an ongoing basis (confirm the diagnosis if needed, control symptoms and modifiable risk factors including lung function/comorbidities/inhaler technique/adherence/patient goals). Treatment is adjusted up and down the step ladder based on symptom control.

StepPreferred controllerPreferred reliever
Step 1As-needed low-dose ICS-formoterolAs-needed low-dose ICS-formoterol (or as-needed SABA as the "other" option)
Step 2Daily low-dose inhaled corticosteroid (ICS), or as-needed low-dose ICS-formoterol
Step 3Low-dose ICS-LABAAs-needed low-dose ICS-formoterol for patients on maintenance-and-reliever therapy
Step 4Medium-dose ICS-LABA
Step 5High-dose ICS-LABA; refer for phenotypic assessment ± add-on therapy (e.g., tiotropium, anti-IgE, anti-IL5/5R, anti-IL4R)

ICS = inhaled corticosteroid; LABA = long-acting beta-agonist; SABA = short-acting beta-agonist; LTRA = leukotriene receptor antagonist; OCS = oral corticosteroid.

Clinical pearlThe big-picture shift in modern asthma guidelines: an as-needed SABA-only strategy is no longer the preferred reliever at any step — as-needed low-dose ICS-formoterol is now preferred, because it treats the underlying inflammation at the same time it relieves symptoms, rather than masking symptoms with bronchodilation alone.

Clinical pearlThe cleanest way to remember this: COPD is an umbrella term whose two classic component processes are emphysema (alveolar destruction) and chronic bronchitis (mucous gland/airway change) — asthma is a separate disease defined by reversibility and hyper-responsiveness, even though its symptoms (dyspnea, wheeze, cough) can look identical to a COPD exacerbation.

DOnset before age 25

Explanation: COPD's obstruction is, by definition, mostly irreversible/progressive, whereas asthma's hallmark is reversibility (spontaneous or with treatment). Fixed, largely irreversible obstruction points away from asthma and toward COPD/chronic bronchitis.
Exam-style question
Compared to asthma, which feature is more characteristic of chronic bronchitis?
AAirway hyper-responsiveness to stimuli that don't affect non-asthmatics
BPredominantly eosinophilic airway inflammation
CElevated exhaled nitric oxide during attacks
DChronic productive cough most days for at least 3 months/year over 2+ consecutive years
EOnset most often before age 25
Explanation: Chronic bronchitis is a clinically defined entity: productive cough on most days for at least 3 months of the year, for at least 2 consecutive years, reflecting mucous gland/goblet cell hyperplasia. Hyper-responsiveness, eosinophilic inflammation, elevated FeNO during attacks, and typical onset before age 25 are asthma features.
