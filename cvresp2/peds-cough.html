What is a cough — and when is it "chronic"?

A cough is a natural protective mechanism of the body. A strong and efficient cough is needed to mobilize secretions and eliminate foreign entities (viruses, bacteria, chemicals, pollutants) from the respiratory tract. Cough is one of the most common chief complaints in a pediatric office — especially in the fall/winter "sixth season."

An ordinary cold-associated cough typically lasts 7-10 days, sometimes up to 2 weeks. A chronic cough in the pediatric population is defined as a cough lasting longer than 4 weeks.

The four phases of a cough

The cough reflex: (1) rest, (2) inspiratory phase — inspiration with the glottis open, (3) compression phase — glottic closure with increasing intrathoracic pressure, (4) cough/expulsion — glottis opens with increased diaphragmatic pressure and expulsion of air. (Image: PIR, Chronic Cough, 2022)

Common causes overview & cause by age

The four most common causes of chronic cough in children: asthma, postinfectious cough (viral or bacterial), gastroesophageal reflux (GER), and protracted bacterial bronchitis (PBB). Asthma is the single most common cause overall — but day-to-day, postinfectious cough (back-to-back viral illnesses, especially in daycare-age kids) is what's seen most often in clinic.

Age groupLeading causes
Infants & <3 years oldPBB and postinfectious cough account for 68% of cases
Preschool, 3-4 years oldPBB and postinfectious cough account for only ~9% of cases — other etiologies become relatively more important
Older children/adolescents >11 years oldShift toward infectious agents (e.g., Mycoplasma) and habit cough
Clinical pearlAlways ask: was there a period where the child actually got better for a few days before a new fever/runny nose/sore throat started? A 'yes' points toward back-to-back viral illnesses rather than PBB or a single unresolving process.

Common causes — pulmonary & infectious

Common causes of chronic cough — pulmonary causes (asthma) and infectious causes (viral, Bordetella pertussis, Mycoplasma pneumoniae), plus postinfectious hypersensitivity and protracted bacterial bronchitis, with presentation/risk factors/exam/cough character/evaluation/sequelae for each. (Masson, Kier, Chandran; Pediatr Rev 2022;43(12):691-703)

Asthma

The most common cause of chronic cough in the pediatric patient. Presents with coughing, wheezing, sometimes exercise intolerance, and chest pain/tightness. A key diagnostic highlight: the cough must be responsive to a SABA (albuterol) to support the diagnosis. Risk factors: early viral infections in genetically predisposed (atopic) individuals — eczema, allergic rhinitis, or a parent with asthma — plus environmental exposures (cockroaches, mold, wildfire smoke). Exam: wheeze, cough, diminished air entry if severe. Cough is spastic/dry-sounding, responds to bronchodilator, and resolves with an empirical trial of inhaled corticosteroids. Evaluation may include allergy testing or spirometry. Long-term risk if poorly controlled: airway remodeling.

Viral URI / viral bronchiolitis

Cough ± fever plus upper respiratory symptoms (nasal congestion, pharyngitis, sometimes rash). Biggest risk factor is exposure — sick contacts at home, daycare, or school, or recent travel. Exam is variable: wet or dry cough, rhonchi, rales, sometimes wheeze if bronchiolitis. A nasopharyngeal/sputum PCR panel can identify influenza, COVID-19, or RSV if needed.

Bordetella pertussis

Three classic stages: catarrhal → paroxysmal → convalescent. Upper respiratory symptoms and cough, with apnea episodes in babies. Nicknamed the "100-day cough" because it can seem never-ending. Requires antibiotic treatment. Unvaccinated status is a key risk factor. Exam: paroxysmal cough that classically terminates with an inspiratory "whoop."

Mycoplasma pneumoniae ("walking pneumonia")

Also called tracheobronchitis — a very spastic-sounding cough, worse with coughing itself. Most often seen in teenagers, with associated fatigue, fever, headache, and sick contacts at school. Productive cough with wheeze and rales — though sometimes no phlegm at all. Treated with antibiotics (classically azithromycin, "the Z-pack").

Postinfectious hypersensitivity cough

A patient coughing more than 3 weeks after resolution of an acute respiratory tract infection may have this — occurring in roughly 1 in 10 children after a viral illness (RSV, rhinovirus/common cold). Chest x-rays are typically normal, and the cough resolves on its own. Thought to be due to airway epithelial inflammation/disruption that lowers the threshold for cough to non-noxious stimuli.

Protracted bacterial bronchitis (PBB)

A wet cough lasting >4 weeks, mainly a clinical diagnosis of exclusion (no other cause identified). Cause is not fully known; thought to relate to frequent viral illnesses causing airway injury/inflammation that makes bacterial growth easier. Chest x-ray may show nothing specific. Typically resolves with a 2-week course of antibiotics (amoxicillin or augmentin). Risk factors: daycare attendance, underlying tracheomalacia/bronchomalacia (airway abnormalities that trap mucus). Sequela: bronchiectasis, with up to a 40% recurrence rate.

Clinical pearlA pediatric patient with 4 weeks of consistent wet cough that never had a well interval, with no clear new-illness onset, is reasonable to trial on a course of antibiotics with close follow-up for PBB — if it resolves, that confirms the diagnosis retrospectively.

Common causes — upper airway & GI

Common causes of chronic cough — upper airway (chronic rhinosinusitis/upper airway cough syndrome) and GI (gastroesophageal reflux), with presentation/risk factors/exam/cough character/evaluation/sequelae. (Masson, Kier, Chandran; Pediatr Rev 2022;43(12):691-703)

Chronic rhinosinusitis / upper airway cough syndrome (UACS)

Think of this as chronic post-nasal drip — every time the child lays down, drainage stimulates the cough reflex, so cough is classically worse at night. Associated with headaches and nasal congestion ± discharge. Risk factors: chronic allergies, narrow sinuses, high-arch palate, deviated septum. Exam: sinus/facial tenderness (push on the maxillary/frontal sinuses; have the child bend forward and ask about pressure), thick nasal congestion, cobblestoning of the posterior oropharynx, thick mucus down the back of the throat. Cough can be dry or productive. Rather than jumping straight to sinus CT, a trial of antibiotics (augmentin) is often reasonable first, reserving ENT referral/CT for non-responders. Sequelae: nasal polyps, diminished quality of life.

Gastroesophageal reflux (GER/GERD)

A classic cause in supine infants <6 months, but also seen in toddlers/older children with chronic reflux. Infants: choking/gagging, spitting up around feeds, back-arching or arm-splaying posturing, frequent regurgitation. Older children can describe epigastric or substernal pain. Risk factors: prematurity, developmental delay, poor truncal tone, obesity; symptoms correlate with feeds. Exam varies by age — young infants may just be "happy spitters" or fussy with laying flat; older children may have voice changes (chronic vocal cord irritation), upper airway sounds, occasional wheeze/tachypnea. Cough can be wet or dry and is worse in the supine position. Management is often empiric (reflux precautions/modifications) in younger children; older children with a cough specifically tied to reflux may be referred to pediatric GI for 24-hour pH probe monitoring. Sequelae of uncontrolled GERD: failure to thrive, recurrent wheezing, and pneumonia.

Uncommon but important causes

Retained foreign body aspiration (FBA)

Not all wheezing is asthma. A history of sudden-onset coughing in an otherwise healthy child, with no evidence of a preceding URI, is highly suspicious for aspiration — though this classic history is often absent. The classic triad is new-onset cough, wheeze, and asymmetric breath sounds, pointing to a retained foreign body. Foreign bodies are often radiolucent, not radiopaque — especially organic material (nuts, seeds) as opposed to a coin or button battery — so a chest x-ray may show nothing specific. Look for a history of coughing with/after eating, or a choking episode, and a focal wheeze or absent breath sounds on exam.

Cystic fibrosis

Presents with failure to thrive, chronic cough, recurrent bacterial pneumonia, chronic sinusitis, and nasal polyps. Exam: poor growth, wheeze, rales/rhonchi, sinusitis, nasal polyps. Diagnosed via the newborn screen (checked in the first weeks of life in every US state) — but a normal newborn screen does not rule out CF. The gold-standard confirmatory test is the sweat chloride test, which should also be considered in a new patient with a concerning history/exam whose newborn screen result is unknown/unavailable.

Tracheoesophageal fistula (TEF)

Cough with a strict temporal relationship to feeds — every time the child eats, not positional like reflux (worse lying down/at night). Diagnosed with chest CT, upper endoscopy, or bronchoscopy.

Habit cough

A honking-sounding cough with no identifiable trigger — the child isn't currently sick and has no history of asthma. Most often seen in tweens (10-12) and teenagers. The key clue: the cough is absent during sleep or when the child is distracted (e.g., absorbed in a video game). This is a diagnosis of exclusion; exam is otherwise benign with clear lungs.

Mediastinal mass

High risk for severe illness/death — a slowly worsening cough (dry or wet), often with few other symptoms early on, more common in teenagers. This is a classic presentation for lymphoma. Diagnosis: chest x-ray showing mediastinal widening or a subtle mass → chest CT and specialist referral.

Clinical pearlA slowly progressive cough in a teenager with an otherwise unremarkable workup should prompt a chest x-ray looking for mediastinal widening — lymphoma is the classic diagnosis not to miss.

Emerging causes

Long COVID / COVID-19-related chronic cough

A dry cough associated with shortness of breath, typically following a COVID-19 infection. Still an emerging entity — long-term sequelae are not yet fully understood.

EVALI (e-cigarette/vaping-associated lung injury)

Risk factor: vaping, especially with certain brands (e.g., JUUL). Exam is typically normal; the cough is dry. Diagnosis is made with a chest CT without contrast.

Practice questions

Exam-style question
A 9-month-old attending daycare has had a wet cough for 5 weeks. There was never a symptom-free interval — no clear new fever/runny nose to suggest a fresh illness — just a persistent cough that hasn't improved. Chest x-ray shows no focal findings. What is the most likely diagnosis, and what is the reasonable next step?
AHabit cough; reassurance only
BCystic fibrosis; proceed directly to sweat chloride testing
CProtracted bacterial bronchitis; a 2-week trial of amoxicillin/augmentin with close follow-up
DTracheoesophageal fistula; chest CT
EForeign body aspiration; bronchoscopy
Explanation: A wet cough >4 weeks without other identified cause, and without a clear interval of wellness suggesting back-to-back viral illnesses, is classic protracted bacterial bronchitis — a clinical diagnosis of exclusion typically trialed on a 2-week antibiotic course with follow-up to confirm response.
Exam-style question
A 15-year-old presents with 10 days of fatigue, low-grade fever, headache, and a productive cough with wheeze and rales. Several classmates have been sick with similar symptoms. Which organism is most likely, and what is the classic treatment?
ABordetella pertussis; supportive care only
BRespiratory syncytial virus; nebulized racemic epinephrine
CStreptococcus pneumoniae; no antibiotics needed
DAspergillus; itraconazole
EMycoplasma pneumoniae; azithromycin
Explanation: Mycoplasma pneumoniae ("walking pneumonia"/tracheobronchitis) classically affects teenagers with sick contacts, fatigue, fever, headache, and a productive cough with wheeze/rales, and is treated with a macrolide such as azithromycin.
Exam-style question
A 3-year-old has a chronic dry cough that is present at daycare and at home during the day but never wakes him at night, and completely disappears when he's absorbed in a favorite show. He has no history of asthma and is not currently ill. Exam is normal with clear lungs. What is the most likely diagnosis?
AProtracted bacterial bronchitis
BHabit cough
CVocal cord dysfunction
DPostinfectious hypersensitivity cough
EAsthma
Explanation: A honking cough with no identifiable trigger, absent during sleep or distraction, and a benign exam, is the classic picture of habit cough — a diagnosis of exclusion typically seen in older children/tweens and teens.
Exam-style question
A 2-year-old has a chronic cough that occurs every time she eats or drinks, regardless of position, without a clear relationship to lying down or nighttime. Which diagnosis should be considered, and how is it confirmed?
AGERD; 24-hour pH probe monitoring
BProtracted bacterial bronchitis; 2-week antibiotic trial
CVocal cord dysfunction; flexible laryngoscopy
DTracheoesophageal fistula; chest CT, upper endoscopy, or bronchoscopy
EHabit cough; observation
Explanation: A cough with a strict temporal relationship to feeding — not positional like reflux — should raise concern for a tracheoesophageal fistula, confirmed with chest CT, upper endoscopy, or bronchoscopy.
Exam-style question
A 6-year-old has a slowly progressive, several-week dry cough with no fever, no wheeze, and an otherwise unremarkable exam. A chest x-ray is obtained and shows mediastinal widening. What diagnosis is this most classically concerning for, and what is the next step?
ALymphoma; chest CT and specialist referral
BCystic fibrosis; sweat chloride test
CForeign body aspiration; rigid bronchoscopy
DBordetella pertussis; azithromycin
EGERD; empiric reflux precautions
Explanation: A slowly worsening cough with mediastinal widening on chest x-ray is a classic presentation for lymphoma in this age group, warranting chest CT and referral to further specialists.
Exam-style question
A previously healthy 2-year-old has a sudden onset of coughing and wheeze while playing with small toys, with no preceding cold symptoms. Exam reveals a focal wheeze on the right side. Chest x-ray is unremarkable. What is the most likely explanation for the normal chest x-ray?
AThe child does not actually have a foreign body
BChest x-rays cannot detect any airway pathology in children
CMost aspirated foreign bodies, especially organic material like nuts or seeds, are radiolucent rather than radiopaque
DThis rules out foreign body aspiration entirely
EThe foreign body has already passed
Explanation: Foreign bodies — especially organic material such as nuts and seeds — are often radiolucent, so a normal chest x-ray does not rule out foreign body aspiration; the classic triad is new-onset cough, wheeze, and asymmetric breath sounds.
Exam-style question
A newborn's state newborn screen for cystic fibrosis comes back normal. At 18 months, the child is now failing to thrive with recurrent bacterial pneumonias and chronic sinusitis. What is the most appropriate next step?
AReassure the family that CF has already been ruled out
BRepeat the newborn screen only
CStart empiric antibiotics with no further workup
DRefer directly for lung transplant evaluation
EObtain a sweat chloride test despite the normal newborn screen
Explanation: A normal newborn screen does not rule out cystic fibrosis. When the history and exam remain concerning, the gold-standard confirmatory test is a sweat chloride test.
Exam-style question
A 4-year-old has a chronic cough that is notably worse every night after being put to bed, associated with headaches and thick nasal congestion. Exam shows cobblestoning of the posterior oropharynx and tenderness over the maxillary sinuses. What is the most likely diagnosis, and what is a reasonable initial management step?
AHabit cough; reassurance
BChronic rhinosinusitis/upper airway cough syndrome; a trial of antibiotics such as augmentin before jumping to sinus CT
CTracheoesophageal fistula; upper endoscopy
DMediastinal mass; chest CT
ECystic fibrosis; sweat chloride test
Explanation: Chronic post-nasal drip from rhinosinusitis/upper airway cough syndrome classically worsens at night (drainage while supine) and presents with headache, congestion, sinus tenderness, and posterior oropharyngeal cobblestoning; an antibiotic trial is often reasonable before proceeding to CT imaging.
