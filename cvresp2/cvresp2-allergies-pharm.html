1. Histamine Physiology

Histamine is a biologically active amine, nearly ubiquitously distributed, formed by decarboxylation of L-histidine (via histidine decarboxylase). Once formed, it is either stored or rapidly inactivated — very little is excreted unchanged.

Storage

  • Most tissue histamine is sequestered in granules within mast cells and basophils
  • Tissue histamine content tracks mast cell content — richest at sites of potential injury (nose, mouth, feet), internal body surfaces, and blood vessels
  • Non-mast cell histamine: in the CNS, acts as a neurotransmitter (sleep/wake, appetite, learning/memory, thermoregulation, cardiovascular regulation) — no current drug targets exploit this. In the GI tract, stored/released from enterochromaffin-like (ECL) cells of the gastric fundus as a gastric acid secretagogue.

Release

ImmunologicIgE-mediated (type I immediate hypersensitivity); also IgG-/IgM-mediated reactions that activate complement
Drug/chemical-inducedMorphine, tubocurarine, organic bases (e.g., antibiotics), radiocontrast dye, vancomycin — displace histamine from storage without requiring energy and without mast cell injury/degranulation
PhysicalMechanical or thermal injury (e.g., burns)
Clinical translation — A patient on morphine who develops hives/itching/flushing, or a patient having a vancomycin infusion reaction ("red man syndrome"), is experiencing direct histamine release/displacement — not necessarily a true IgE allergy.

Receptor Subtypes (all G protein-coupled)

ReceptorG-Protein / 2nd MessengerDistribution
H1Gq → ↑ IP3, DAGSmooth muscle, endothelium, brain
H2Gs → ↑ cAMPGastric mucosa, cardiac muscle, mast cells, brain
H3Gi → ↓ cAMPPresynaptic auto-/heteroreceptors: brain, myenteric plexus
H4Gi → ↓ cAMPEosinophils, neutrophils, CD4 T cells (chemoattractant role)
Structural quirk worth remembering — H1 and H2 receptors are structurally more closely related to the muscarinic and 5-HT1 (serotonin) receptors, respectively, than they are to each other.

Tissue & Organ System Effects

  • Allergy/inflammation: urticaria, pruritus, allergic rhinitis, anaphylactic shock; local vasodilation and increased blood flow at sites of inflammation
  • Cardiovascular: ↓ blood pressure (systolic and diastolic) with reflex ↑ heart rate; low-dose histamine → H1-mediated nitric oxide release; high-dose → H2-mediated cAMP-driven vasodilation. Capillary dilation, local edema (endothelial cells separate, allowing transudation of fluid/small proteins), flushing, warmth, headache.
  • "Triple response" (intradermal histamine injection): (1) red spot — local vasodilation, seconds; (2) flare — axon reflex; (3) wheal — local edema, 1–2 minutes
  • GI smooth muscle: contraction → diarrhea
  • Bronchioles: constriction — not clinically striking in most people, but asthmatics are exquisitely sensitive (possibly hyperactive neural response)
  • Exocrine: gastric acid secretion via H2 receptors
  • Peripheral nerves: stimulates sensory endings → pain, itching, contributes to the flare/triple response

Therapeutic Use & Toxicity of Histamine Itself

Essentially none — rarely used as an aerosol for bronchial hyperreactivity testing (methacholine is now preferred). Toxicity/overdose effects are a direct extension of its physiology: flushing, hypotension, tachycardia, headache, urticaria, bronchoconstriction, GI upset.

USMLE-Style Question

A patient receiving IV morphine for postoperative pain develops localized itching, flushing, and hives at the infusion site, without hypotension or airway compromise. Which mechanism best explains this reaction?

A. Direct, non-immunologic displacement of histamine from mast cell stores
B. IgE cross-linking on mast cell surface receptors
C. Complement activation via an IgG-mediated reaction
D. Direct stimulation of H2 receptors on gastric parietal cells
E. Activation of presynaptic H3 autoreceptors
Answer: A. Morphine (along with tubocurarine, certain antibiotics, and radiocontrast dye) can directly displace histamine from its bound intracellular form — a non-immunologic, energy-independent process that does not require mast cell injury or IgE involvement.

USMLE-Style Question

A researcher injects histamine intradermally and observes a sequence of skin changes over several minutes. Which of the following correctly matches the wheal component of the "triple response" with its underlying mechanism?

A. Immediate red spot from direct capillary vasodilation
B. Local edema from endothelial cell separation and fluid transudation, appearing over 1–2 minutes
C. Flare formation mediated by a spinal reflex arc
D. Bronchoconstriction from direct airway smooth muscle H1 stimulation
E. Gastric acid hypersecretion from H2 receptor activation
Answer: B. The wheal is the local edema component of the triple response, forming over 1–2 minutes as histamine causes endothelial cells to separate, allowing transudation of fluid and small proteins into the perivascular space. (The red spot is immediate/seconds, and the flare is mediated by an axon reflex, not a spinal reflex.)

2. H1 Receptor Antagonists — First-Generation

MOA (applies to all H1 blockers, both generations): reversible, competitive binding at the H1 receptor. Because histamine receptors show constitutive activity, these agents may actually function as inverse agonists rather than classic neutral antagonists. H1 blockers do not block gastric acid secretion or histamine release — that is H2 receptor territory.

First-generation agents cause significant sedation because they readily cross into the CNS and have prominent antimuscarinic (atropine-like) activity — this sedation is independent of H1 blockade itself.

Diphenhydramine (Benadryl) Core

The prototypical sedating first-generation H1 blocker. Notable antimuscarinic effects (dry mouth, urinary retention, confusion in the elderly). Also has meaningful antinausea/antiemetic activity (motion sickness) and local anesthetic action (Na+ channel blockade) at higher-than-allergy doses. Used for insomnia (found in "PM" combination products), and given parenterally for acute dystonic reactions / antiparkinsonism effects related to antipsychotic use.

Chlorpheniramine Core

Classic first-generation agent, sedating, prototype "name recognition" drug found in many OTC cold/allergy combination products.

Promethazine (Phenergan) Core

Notable for additional α-adrenergic receptor blockade → added hypotension, dizziness, reflex tachycardia (a receptor activity that diphenhydramine/chlorpheniramine don't share to the same degree). Also has antiemetic and local anesthetic properties, similar to diphenhydramine.

Other first-generation agents (name recognition only): Brompheniramine, cyclizine, cyproheptadine, dimenhydrinate (Dramamine — motion sickness), hydroxyzine (active metabolite → cetirizine), meclizine (long-acting, motion sickness).

Receptor Activity Beyond H1 Blockade

Antimuscarinic (anticholinergic)Dry mouth, urinary retention, sinus tachycardia — worsened in elderly patients, especially with BPH
α-adrenergic blockade (promethazine)Hypotension, dizziness, reflex tachycardia
Serotonin receptor inhibition↑ appetite (some agents, e.g., cyproheptadine)
None of these block H2 receptors — that's why H1 blockers have no effect on gastric acid secretion, regardless of generation.

USMLE-Style Question

A 78-year-old man with benign prostatic hyperplasia takes diphenhydramine nightly for insomnia. He presents with new confusion, dry mouth, and difficulty urinating. Which property of diphenhydramine most directly explains this presentation?

A. H2 receptor antagonism
B. α1-adrenergic receptor blockade
C. Serotonin receptor antagonism
D. Antimuscarinic (anticholinergic) activity
E. Sodium channel blockade
Answer: D. Diphenhydramine's antimuscarinic activity produces dry mouth, urinary retention, and confusion — effects that are particularly hazardous in elderly patients and are worsened in men with BPH, where anticholinergic-induced urinary retention can become severe.

USMLE-Style Question

A patient started on promethazine for nausea reports new dizziness and lightheadedness when standing. Which additional receptor activity of promethazine, beyond H1 blockade, best explains this symptom?

A. Muscarinic receptor blockade
B. α-adrenergic receptor blockade
C. H2 receptor blockade
D. 5-HT receptor blockade
E. β-adrenergic receptor blockade
Answer: B. Promethazine has notable α-adrenergic receptor blocking activity in addition to H1 blockade, producing orthostatic hypotension, dizziness, and reflex tachycardia — a distinguishing feature from other first-generation agents.

3. H1 Receptor Antagonists — Second-Generation

Second-generation agents are much less sedating because they are more hydrophilic and are substrates of P-glycoprotein (an efflux transporter at the blood-brain barrier) — they simply don't accumulate in the CNS the way first-generation agents do.

Cetirizine (Zyrtec) Core

Active metabolite of hydroxyzine. Of the second-generation agents, it carries a relatively higher (though still low) risk of drowsiness compared to loratadine/fexofenadine.

Fexofenadine (Allegra) Core

Active metabolite of terfenadine — the drug that replaced terfenadine after its 1998 withdrawal (see ADR/DDI section). One of the least sedating agents in the class, useful when daytime alertness matters.

Loratadine (Claritin) Core

Metabolized to desloratadine. Along with fexofenadine, has the lowest risk of drowsiness among second-generation agents.

Desloratadine (Clarinex) Core

The active metabolite of loratadine, also marketed as its own therapeutic entity.

Other second-generation agents: Azelastine (nasal spray formulation — see Allergic Rhinitis section), levocetirizine (Xyzal).

Prodrug → Active Metabolite Relationships (High-Yield)

Hydroxyzine →Cetirizine
Terfenadine →Fexofenadine
Loratadine →Desloratadine

Pharmacokinetics (Both Generations)

  • Rapidly absorbed orally
  • Metabolized in the liver; second-generation agents specifically via CYP3A4
  • Duration of action typically 4–6 hours (meclizine and several second-generation agents are long-acting, 12–24 hours)
Relative sedation ranking — Among second-generation agents, cetirizine causes somewhat more drowsiness than loratadine or fexofenadine, which have the lowest sedation risk in the class — a relevant consideration for patients who need to stay alert (e.g., driving, operating machinery, certain jobs).

USMLE-Style Question

A pharmacology exam asks students to match a prodrug to its clinically available active metabolite. Which pairing is correct?

A. Cetirizine → hydroxyzine
B. Desloratadine → fexofenadine
C. Fexofenadine → loratadine
D. Loratadine → cetirizine
E. Terfenadine → fexofenadine
Answer: E. Terfenadine's active metabolite is fexofenadine, which replaced terfenadine on the market after terfenadine's 1998 withdrawal for causing torsades de pointes. (The other correct pairings run the opposite direction: hydroxyzine → cetirizine, loratadine → desloratadine.)

USMLE-Style Question

A commercial airline pilot with seasonal allergies wants an antihistamine with the lowest possible risk of drowsiness so she can safely fly. Which of the following would be the most appropriate choice?

A. Fexofenadine
B. Diphenhydramine
C. Chlorpheniramine
D. Promethazine
E. Hydroxyzine
Answer: A. Fexofenadine (along with loratadine) has the lowest risk of sedation among the antihistamines, making it preferable when wakefulness and psychomotor performance are essential. All the other options are sedating first-generation agents.

5. ADRs, Toxicity, Overdose & Drug Interactions

Common ADRs

  • Sedation and antimuscarinic effects — most common, much more prominent with first-generation agents (dry mouth, dry nasal passages, urinary retention)
  • CNS: dizziness, tinnitus, nervousness, insomnia, fatigue, blurred vision
  • Hypersensitivity reactions — relatively common with topical antihistamine formulations

Poisoning / Overdose

  • Central effects: excitation (especially in children), hallucinations, ataxia, incoordination, convulsions
  • Anticholinergic toxidrome: fixed and dilated pupils, flushed face, fever
  • Terminal: deepening coma with cardiorespiratory collapse and death
Historical DDI case — know this story — Terfenadine (Seldane) and astemizole (Hismanal) were voluntarily withdrawn in 1998 after being linked to fatal torsades de pointes (QT prolongation). This occurred specifically when co-administered with drugs that inhibit their metabolism via CYP3A4, causing the parent drugs to accumulate. CYP3A4 inhibitors implicated: macrolide antibiotics, azole antifungals (e.g., fluconazole), HIV protease inhibitors (indinavir, ritonavir, saquinavir, nelfinavir), certain SSRIs (fluvoxamine, sertraline), zileuton, and grapefruit juice.

USMLE-Style Question

A patient with severe atopic dermatitis and significant sleep disruption from itching is being considered for antihistamine therapy. Which agent would most appropriately address both the itching and the sleep disturbance, despite limited efficacy data for this indication overall?

A. Loratadine
B. Fexofenadine
C. Diphenhydramine
D. Famotidine
E. Desloratadine
Answer: C. For atopic dermatitis, sedating first-generation agents like diphenhydramine are preferred specifically because the sedative property itself helps reduce nighttime awareness of itching and aids sleep — even though large randomized efficacy data for this specific use are limited.

USMLE-Style Question

A patient taking an antihistamine for years is prescribed a macrolide antibiotic for a respiratory infection and subsequently develops syncope; ECG shows a markedly prolonged QT interval. This clinical scenario is most historically associated with which of the following now-withdrawn agents?

A. Diphenhydramine
B. Loratadine
C. Terfenadine
D. Cetirizine
E. Azelastine
Answer: C. Terfenadine (along with astemizole) was withdrawn from the market in 1998 after being linked to fatal torsades de pointes when its CYP3A4 metabolism was inhibited by drugs such as macrolide antibiotics, causing toxic accumulation of the parent drug.

6. H2 Receptor Antagonists

Covered in depth in the GI System course — here, focus on name recognition and classification. Agents: cimetidine, famotidine, ranitidine.

  • MOA: competitive inhibition of H2 receptors on gastric parietal cells → inhibits gastric acid secretion. Highly selective for H2 — do not meaningfully block H1 receptors at normal doses.
  • Therapeutic use: peptic ulcer disease, GERD
  • PK: cleared via hepatic metabolism, glomerular filtration, and renal tubular secretion; short half-life (1–4 hours); dose-reduce in renal (and possibly severe hepatic) impairment
Cimetidine

Unique ADRs: gynecomastia or impotence in men, galactorrhea in women. Most likely of the H2 blockers to cause mental status changes (confusion, hallucinations, agitation) — especially in elderly/ICU patients with renal or hepatic dysfunction. Also the most significant CYP inhibitor of the group (CYP1A2, 2C9, 2D6, 3A4) — cimetidine >>> ranitidine; famotidine has negligible interactions. Watch for interactions with narrow-therapeutic-index drugs: theophylline, warfarin, phenytoin, lidocaine.

The key distinguishing point for this course — H2 blockers are not effective H1 blockers, and vice versa: the two receptor subtypes are pharmacologically distinct despite both responding to the same endogenous ligand.

USMLE-Style Question

A middle-aged man on long-term therapy for GERD develops bilateral breast tenderness and enlargement. Which drug is most likely responsible?

A. Famotidine
B. Cimetidine
C. Loratadine
D. Diphenhydramine
E. Ranitidine
Answer: B. Cimetidine is uniquely associated with antiandrogenic effects, including gynecomastia and impotence in men and galactorrhea in women — a distinguishing ADR not shared by the other H2 blockers.

7. Allergic Rhinitis Management

Seasonal allergic rhinitis (hay fever) results from allergen deposition on the nasal mucosa, triggering immediate hypersensitivity. Symptoms: sneezing, rhinorrhea, nasal obstruction, itching of eyes/nose/palate (also postnasal drip, cough, irritability, fatigue).

Treatment Hierarchy

  1. Intranasal glucocorticoids (beclomethasone, budesonide, ciclesonide, flunisolide, fluticasone propionate/furoate, mometasone furoate) — first-line and most effective treatment overall
  2. Second-generation oral H1 antagonists (or intranasal azelastine) — preferred for mild-to-moderate symptoms; produce considerable, though incomplete, symptom relief (unlike in asthma, where antihistamines don't help)
  3. Intranasal cromolyn — mast cell stabilizer/prophylaxis; effective but must be dosed 3–4 times daily, which limits adherence
  4. Intranasal ipratropium (muscarinic antagonist) — can help reduce secretions/rhinorrhea specifically
  5. Nasal decongestants — for acute symptom relief only, never long-term (see Section 8)
Why glucocorticoids win — Like inhaled therapy for asthma, intranasal delivery targets the drug directly to the site of inflammation, making topical glucocorticoids extremely effective with a favorable safety profile compared to systemic options.

USMLE-Style Question

A patient with moderate seasonal allergic rhinitis asks for the single most effective treatment option available. What should be recommended first-line?

A. Oral pseudoephedrine
B. Oral loratadine
C. Intranasal cromolyn
D. Intranasal ipratropium
E. Intranasal fluticasone
Answer: E. Intranasal glucocorticoids are the most effective treatment available for allergic rhinitis and are recommended first-line, ahead of oral or intranasal antihistamines, cromolyn, or ipratropium.

8. Decongestants

MOA: α1-adrenergic receptor agonism → vasoconstriction → reduced mucosal edema and nasal congestion. Effective only for congestion — do not help sneezing, itching, or discharge.

Pseudoephedrine (Sudafed) Core

Oral agent with α1, α2, and β agonist activity (broader than most other decongestants, which tend to be pure α1 agonists). Sale is legally restricted (behind-the-counter, quantity limits per day/month) due to diversion for methamphetamine synthesis.

Oxymetazoline (Afrin) Core

Topical/intranasal spray. Also has α2-agonist activity — can cause hypotension similar to other centrally-acting α2 agonists (e.g., clonidine). Must be limited to <48–72 hours (no more than 3 consecutive days) — longer use causes rebound congestion (rhinitis medicamentosa).

Phenylephrine pearl — Restrictions on pseudoephedrine sales drove a surge in oral phenylephrine use, but FDA-mandated studies have shown oral phenylephrine is not more effective than placebo for nasal congestion — a genuinely high-yield, testable fact.

ADRs & Cautions

Insomnia, excitability, headache, nervousness, palpitations, tachycardia, arrhythmia, hypertension, nausea, vomiting. Avoid/use caution in cardiovascular disease, hypertension, diabetes, hyperthyroidism given the sympathomimetic mechanism.

USMLE-Style Question

A patient has been using an over-the-counter nasal spray for congestion every day for the past two weeks. He reports that his congestion is now worse than before he started using it, and it seems to return almost immediately after each dose wears off. What is the most likely explanation?

A. Rhinitis medicamentosa from prolonged topical decongestant use
B. Development of allergic rhinitis
C. New viral upper respiratory infection
D. Tachyphylaxis to intranasal corticosteroids
E. Anticholinergic rebound
Answer: A. Topical decongestants like oxymetazoline should not be used for more than 3 consecutive days; prolonged use leads to rebound congestion (rhinitis medicamentosa), where congestion worsens between doses.

USMLE-Style Question

Following new restrictions on pseudoephedrine sales, many patients switched to an over-the-counter oral alternative marketed for nasal congestion relief. Which statement about this alternative agent is most accurate?

A. It is significantly more effective than pseudoephedrine
B. It works via H1 receptor blockade
C. Oral phenylephrine is not more effective than placebo for nasal congestion
D. It is contraindicated in all patients regardless of cardiovascular status
E. It has no restrictions on sale due to abuse potential
Answer: C. FDA-mandated studies found that oral phenylephrine — the OTC alternative that surged in popularity after pseudoephedrine restrictions — is not more effective than placebo for relieving nasal congestion.

9. Antitussives, Expectorants & Mucolytics

Cough is a nonspecific sign of airway irritation/inflammation mediated through vagal reflex pathways. Treating the underlying cause is preferred over symptomatic cough suppression whenever possible — many causes exist (airway hyperreactivity, smoking, infection, neoplasm, foreign body, postnasal drip, esophageal reflux). Overall, this drug category carries relatively weak evidence for efficacy.

Antitussives

Codeine Core

Opioid antitussive — acts on opioid receptors in the medullary cough center (± peripheral action on cough receptors in proximal airways). Codeine is a prodrug, converted to morphine for its effects; pharmacogenomics of this conversion matters — poor metabolizers may get no benefit, ultra-rapid metabolizers are at higher risk of opioid toxicity. ADRs: sedation, constipation (typical opioid effects), plus CNS/GI/GU effects.

Dextromethorphan Core

Structurally related to codeine but has no analgesic or addictive properties. Acts via sigma receptor stimulation (may also have some NMDA receptor antagonism and possible opioid receptor antagonism) — decreases cough receptor sensitivity and interrupts cough impulse transmission. Nearly as potent as codeine for cough suppression with fewer subjective/GI adverse effects; widely available OTC.

Benzonatate (Tessalon): non-opioid; local anesthetic/demulcent acting on pharyngeal nerve endings; for minor, transient cough.

Expectorants & Mucolytics

Guaifenesin — theoretical MOA is reducing mucus adhesiveness/surface tension to make a nonproductive cough more productive and less frequent; lacks convincing efficacy data.

Acetylcysteine (Mucomyst) — effective mucolytic (opens disulfide bonds in mucoproteins) but causes airway irritation; not currently recommended in practice.

Recombinant DNAse (dornase alfa, Pulmozyme) — nebulized for cystic fibrosis. A substantial portion of purulent CF secretions is extracellular DNA released from lysed inflammatory cells; this enzyme selectively cleaves that DNA, reducing mucus viscosity and improving airflow (also under investigation for COPD exacerbations).

USMLE-Style Question

A patient with cystic fibrosis has thick, purulent airway secretions that are difficult to clear. A nebulized enzyme is prescribed that selectively cleaves extracellular DNA released from lysed neutrophils in the sputum. Which drug is being described?

A. Guaifenesin
B. Acetylcysteine
C. Benzonatate
D. Dornase alfa (recombinant DNAse)
E. Dextromethorphan
Answer: D. Recombinant DNAse (dornase alfa) selectively cleaves the extracellular DNA that makes up a substantial portion of purulent CF secretions (released from lysed inflammatory cells), reducing mucus viscosity and improving airflow.

USMLE-Style Question

Which of the following best describes a key pharmacologic advantage of dextromethorphan over codeine as an antitussive?

A. It is a more potent cough suppressant overall
B. It requires hepatic bioactivation to an active metabolite
C. It lacks analgesic and addictive properties
D. It has superior efficacy proven in large randomized trials
E. It works by directly relaxing bronchial smooth muscle
Answer: C. Dextromethorphan is structurally related to codeine but lacks analgesic and addictive properties, making it available OTC and generally better tolerated, while remaining nearly as potent as codeine for cough suppression.

10. Lecture Knowledge Checks

These are the three cases Dr. Waller worked through directly in the lecture — good self-check before moving to the summary table.

Lecture Case 1

A 64-year-old man with benign prostatic hyperplasia presents with generalized itching that began after an afternoon of prolonged sun exposure, worse at night. Physical exam shows an enlarged prostate and no other evidence of disease. Which drug would be most appropriate?

A. Diphenhydramine
B. Famotidine
C. Loratadine
D. Theophylline
E. Acetaminophen
Answer: C. This looks like a case for a sedating first-generation agent given the nighttime itching, but the BPH is the deciding factor: diphenhydramine's antimuscarinic activity would worsen urinary retention in a man with an enlarged prostate. A second-generation agent like loratadine treats the itching without that risk. Famotidine (an H2 blocker) doesn't address histamine-mediated itching, and acetaminophen/theophylline don't treat the underlying cause at all.

Lecture Case 2

A 48-year-old woman with allergic urticaria starts treatment with cetirizine. Decreased synthesis of which substance most likely mediates the drug's therapeutic effect?

A. Cyclic adenosine monophosphate
B. Cyclic guanosine monophosphate
C. Cyclooxygenase-1
D. Cyclooxygenase-2
E. Inositol triphosphate
Answer: E. Cetirizine is an H1 blocker, and H1 receptors are Gq-coupled, signaling through increased IP3 and DAG. Blocking this receptor decreases IP3 synthesis. (Gs-coupled receptors like H2 would work through cAMP instead; phosphodiesterase inhibitors or nitrates affect cGMP — a different pathway entirely.)

Lecture Case 3

A 2-year-old boy is brought to the ED with a temperature of 104°F, flushed and dry skin, and widely dilated pupils unresponsive to light. He is agitated and has a brief tonic-clonic convulsion. His mother reports he may have swallowed several tablets of her allergy medication. Which drug most likely caused this presentation?

A. Azelastine
B. Diphenhydramine
C. Ibuprofen
D. Loratadine
E. Pseudoephedrine
Answer: B. This is a classic anticholinergic toxidrome (hyperthermia, flushed/dry skin, fixed dilated pupils, agitation, seizure) — consistent with diphenhydramine overdose given the stated "allergy medicine" context. Pseudoephedrine toxicity could also plausibly cause some overlapping sympathomimetic features (and was accepted as a reasonable alternative answer by the instructor), but the anticholinergic picture plus the specific mention of "allergy medicine" points most directly to diphenhydramine. Loratadine requires very high doses to produce significant toxicity, and ibuprofen (a COX inhibitor) doesn't fit this toxidrome at all.
