1. The Case & Framing the Problem

Case Stem — TJ 55-year-old commercial (DOT) truck driver, BMI 34.3, HTN, heart failure, prior hypertensive stroke. Pulls over to rest during his route; 20-lb weight gain over one year; loud snoring (bothers bed partner, not him); morning headache and dry mouth; father had OSA and died of MI. Labs: HCO₃ 33, Hgb 19/Hct 57%, BNP 68, TSH normal.

The task is never just "diagnose OSA." Excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS) in a patient with cardiovascular disease must be worked up broadly enough to catch central sleep apnea (CSA), obesity hypoventilation syndrome (OHS), and sustained hypoventilation hiding underneath an apparent OSA picture — because these change the test you order and the therapy you can safely use.

Safety First A patient who cannot reliably stay awake at the wheel needs immediate counseling to stop safety-sensitive driving before any test result comes back — this is a "first, do no harm" action item independent of the final diagnosis.
USMLE-Style Check
A 55-year-old commercial truck driver reports pulling over during routes because he cannot stay awake. He has obesity, heart failure, and a prior stroke. His workup for sleep apnea has not yet been completed. What is the most appropriate immediate action?
Answer: Counsel the patient to stop commercial driving until his sleepiness is evaluated and effectively treated. Safety takes priority over diagnostic completeness. A patient who cannot reliably remain awake while driving poses an immediate risk to himself and the public and should be counseled to stop safety-sensitive activity now, in parallel with (not instead of) starting the diagnostic workup. Applicable occupational/DOT reporting requirements should also be followed.

Reasoning Caution A normal TSH makes hypothyroidism less likely but does not, by itself, exclude or explain the rest of the differential — don't let one negative test end the workup.
USMLE-Style Check
A 55-year-old man with obesity and heart failure has daytime sleepiness, loud snoring, and morning headache. TSH is normal and there is no history of cataplexy or sleep paralysis. Which diagnosis remains best supported by this picture?
Answer: Obstructive sleep apnea, though central or mixed apnea must still be excluded given his cardiac history. The snoring, obesity, and morning headache point toward OSA, but his heart failure and stroke history keep central and mixed apnea on the differential until polysomnography characterizes the events. Narcolepsy is excluded by the absence of cataplexy/sleep paralysis, and a normal TSH argues against hypothyroidism as the driver.

USMLE-Style Check
On exam, a patient's tongue obscures visualization of the uvula and his neck is described as "thick" on inspection. Which additional history finding would most specifically raise concern for treatment-resistant OSA rather than simple snoring?
Answer: A neck circumference of 17.5 inches with a 10% weight gain over the past year. A neck circumference above 17 inches in a man combined with recent weight gain (a 10% weight gain predicts roughly a 6-fold increase in OSA risk) is the option most specific for significant OSA risk. A normal Mallampati grade and reassuring sleep history argue against it.

4. Central Sleep Apnea: Risk Factors & Mechanism

  • Rare compared to OSA; incidence rises with age. Occurs more often during NREM sleep. Women are less susceptible and require a larger magnitude of hypocapnia to trigger an event.
  • Most commonly driven by hyperventilation lowering PaCO₂ below the individual's apneic threshold → central apnea. Enhanced CO₂ chemosensitivity during sleep raises "loop gain" → ventilatory instability → CSA (classic in Cheyne-Stokes breathing of heart failure).
  • Can also result from persistent hypoventilation not driven by low PaCO₂ — e.g., encephalitis, polio, or respiratory-suppressant drugs (opioids).
  • Associated conditions: atrial fibrillation, heart failure, ischemic stroke, spinal cord injury, renal failure, chronic opioid use — almost always encountered in hospitalized patients rather than outpatients.
USMLE-Style Check
A hospitalized patient with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction develops a periodic breathing pattern with cycles of hyperpnea and apnea overnight. What is the primary physiologic driver of his central apnea?
Answer: Heightened chemosensitivity to CO2 causing hyperventilation that drives PaCO2 below the apneic threshold. Cheyne-Stokes breathing in heart failure results from high "loop gain": an oversensitive ventilatory response to CO2 causes hyperventilation that drives PaCO2 below the apneic threshold, triggering a central apnea, followed by CO2 accumulation that restarts hyperventilation — a self-perpetuating cycle. Options describing airway obstruction belong to OSA, not CSA.

5. Screening Tools (Do Not Diagnose OSA)

ToolWhat It MeasuresHigh-Yield Detail
STOP-BANGComposite OSA probabilityDeveloped for the preoperative setting; ~84% sensitivity at score ≥3; high sensitivity at low thresholds but limited specificity
Berlin QuestionnaireOSA probability~86% sensitivity for predicting presence of OSA
Epworth Sleepiness ScaleSubjective sleepiness onlyRequired by most insurers as part of the sleep history before a sleep study is covered; does not distinguish OSA from other causes of sleepiness
Symptom Sensitivity/Specificity Snoring has the highest sensitivity for OSA; choking/gasping is more specific for moderate-to-severe disease.
Who to screen Ask every adult about sleep satisfaction; screen further if dissatisfied/daytime-sleepy, in high-risk occupations (commercial drivers, transit operators), or after a sleepiness-related crash/near-miss.
USMLE-Style Check
A 48-year-old man is being evaluated the morning of an elective surgery. The anesthesia team wants a quick tool, validated for the preoperative setting, to estimate his risk of undiagnosed OSA before intubation. Which tool is most appropriate?
Answer: STOP-BANG questionnaire. STOP-BANG was specifically developed and validated to assess OSA risk in the preoperative setting (score ≥3 carries ~84% sensitivity). The Berlin Questionnaire and Epworth Scale are validated screening tools but not preoperative-specific, Mallampati grade is an airway exam finding rather than a full screening instrument, and AHI is a polysomnographic diagnostic metric, not a screening tool.

6. Choosing & Interpreting the Sleep Study

Testing OptionBest FitLimitations
Home sleep apnea test (HSAT)Uncomplicated adult, high pretest probability of moderate-severe OSALimited evaluation of sleep stages, arousals, CSA, or hypoventilation; a negative/inadequate study may still need PSG
Attended polysomnography (PSG)Significant cardiorespiratory disease, prior stroke, suspected hypoventilation, opioid use, severe insomnia, possible CSA, or nondiagnostic HSAT — gold standard overallMore resource-intensive; measures EEG, EOG, ECG, chin/leg EMG, respiratory effort, airflow, SpO₂, end-tidal CO₂

Definitions

  • Apnea: cessation of airflow ≥ 10 seconds.
  • Hypopnea: reduced airflow ≥ 10 seconds plus a 3% (or, per some payers, 4%) oxyhemoglobin desaturation or an arousal.
  • AHI (apnea-hypopnea index): mild 5–14.9/hr, moderate 15–29.9/hr, severe ≥30/hr — interpret alongside symptoms, desaturation burden, sleep stage, and body position, not in isolation.
Case Application TJ's heart failure, prior stroke, and possible sustained hypoventilation (bicarbonate 33) make him a poor candidate for a routine HSAT — he needs attended PSG.
USMLE-Style Check
A patient with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction and a prior ischemic stroke is being evaluated for suspected sleep apnea. Which diagnostic test is most appropriate as the initial study?
Answer: Attended polysomnography. Significant cardiorespiratory disease and a prior stroke raise concern for central or mixed apnea and possible hypoventilation — situations an HSAT cannot adequately characterize. Attended PSG measures respiratory effort, airflow, oxygenation, cardiac rhythm, and sleep architecture, making it the appropriate initial study in a complicated patient like this.

7. Reading the Tracing: Obstructive vs. Central vs. Mixed

PatternAirflowRespiratory EffortInterpretation
Obstructive apneaAbsentContinues, often paradoxicalUpper airway obstruction against a closed airway
Central apneaAbsentAbsentTransient loss of central ventilatory output
Mixed apneaAbsentAbsent initially, then resumesCentral onset followed by obstructive physiology
HypopneaReduced ≥10sVariableRequires associated desaturation or arousal to score
Sensor Pearl A thermistor detects airflow by temperature change (cool = inhale, warm = exhale); a nasal pressure transducer (NPT) is more sensitive to the subtle airflow drops that define hypopneas.
USMLE-Style Check
On a polysomnogram, an event shows complete absence of airflow for 22 seconds. Respiratory effort belts show no effort for the first several seconds of the event, but effort resumes before the event ends. How should this event be classified?
Answer: Mixed apnea. A mixed apnea begins with absent respiratory effort (the central component) and ends with effort resuming while airflow is still absent (the obstructive component). Pure obstructive apnea shows continuous effort throughout; pure central apnea shows absent effort throughout; a hypopnea shows reduced, not absent, airflow.

8. Pathophysiology & Systemic Complications

Repeated EventImmediate ResponseLong-Term Consequence
Hypoxemia + rising PaCO₂Chemoreceptor activation → arousalSleep fragmentation, morning headache, impaired concentration
Hypoxemia + arousalSympathetic surge → vasoconstrictionSystemic hypertension, ↑cardiovascular stress
Alveolar hypoxiaHypoxic pulmonary vasoconstrictionPulmonary vascular remodeling → pulmonary hypertension → right heart strain/cor pulmonale
Chronic hypoxemia↑Renal erythropoietinSecondary erythrocytosis/polycythemia → ↑viscosity (marked erythrocytosis still needs broader workup, not automatic attribution to OSA)
  • Morning headache: nocturnal hypercapnia causes cerebral vasodilation (carotid bodies are far more sensitive to PaCO₂ than PaO₂ near-normal range) → headache on waking.
  • Nocturia: ↑RV afterload from pulmonary HTN/polycythemia → ↑atrial volume → ↑ANP secretion (natriuresis) + suppressed ADH → ↑urine volume overnight.
  • EKG/exam findings of chronic OSA: right axis deviation, RVH strain pattern, cor pulmonale; metabolic alkalosis is a renal compensation for chronic respiratory acidosis (↑HCO₃ reabsorption in response to chronically elevated PaCO₂).
  • Acute apneic event acid-base: PaCO₂ rises/pH falls transiently during each apnea; persistent daytime hypercapnia with renal bicarbonate retention suggests chronic hypoventilation (e.g., OHS), not just uncomplicated nocturnal OSA.
USMLE-Style Check
A patient with long-standing severe OSA is found to have an elevated hematocrit, right axis deviation and RVH on ECG, and an elevated serum bicarbonate. Which mechanism best explains the elevated bicarbonate?
Answer: Renal compensation for chronic respiratory acidosis from nocturnal hypercapnia. Repeated nocturnal hypercapnic events raise PaCO2, and the kidney compensates over time by increasing bicarbonate reabsorption, producing a compensatory metabolic alkalosis (elevated serum bicarbonate) that reflects chronic respiratory acidosis. A loop diuretic can independently raise bicarbonate but is a distractor here, not the core physiologic mechanism being tested.

USMLE-Style Check
Which single feature, if present on polysomnography, most reliably differentiates central sleep apnea from obstructive sleep apnea?
Answer: Absence of respiratory effort during the apneic event. Both OSA and CSA can produce a high AHI and daytime somnolence, and bicarbonate elevation reflects chronic hypoventilation rather than distinguishing event type. The one feature that cleanly separates the two is respiratory effort: it persists in OSA (obstruction against a closed airway) and is absent in CSA (no central drive to breathe).

10. Treatment of OSA

CPAP — First-Line

Continuous positive airway pressure splints the airway open, pneumatically. Benefits: ↓AHI, ↓daytime sleepiness, improved oxygen desaturation, ↓diurnal/nocturnal BP, ↓pulmonary artery pressure, improved sleep efficiency/quality of life/executive function.

Adherence BarrierFix
Mask discomfort/skin irritation/claustrophobiaTry different mask types; good mask/skin hygiene
Nasal/throat drynessAdd heated humidification
Pressure intoleranceAdjust pressure or taper start; consider APAP (auto-titrating)
Machine noiseQuieter machine; ear plugs
CostWork with insurance; CBT can support both patient and partner adjustment

CMS compliance definition: ≥4 hours/night on ≥70% of nights over a 30-day period within the first 90 days of therapy — assess compliance before declaring CPAP a failure or switching therapy.

Other Options

  • Bilevel PAP (BiPAP) if CPAP pressure alone is insufficient; APAP auto-adjusts across a pressure range.
  • Supplemental O₂ is adjunctive only (comorbid low O₂), never primary OSA therapy.
  • Weight loss: helpful in essentially any overweight/obese OSA patient; not helpful for CSA in a normal/underweight patient. 10% weight loss → ~26% ↓severity; 9/10 mild-OSA patients who lose 15% of body weight achieve remission.
  • GLP-1 receptor agonists / tirzepatide: tirzepatide has an FDA indication specifically for moderate-severe OSA with obesity, alongside diet/exercise.
  • Oral mandibular advancement device: reasonable for mild-moderate OSA or CPAP intolerance; easier adherence for some patients.
  • Surgery (last resort): uvulopalatopharyngoplasty (fewer than half have durable long-term symptom reduction), tracheostomy, hypoglossal nerve stimulator — reserved for anatomic obstruction or PAP failure, not simply for CPAP-intolerance symptoms that can otherwise be managed.
USMLE-Style Check
A patient with moderate OSA has been on CPAP for 6 weeks but reports using it only 2-3 nights per week for about 2 hours each night due to nasal dryness and mask discomfort. What is the most appropriate next step?
Answer: Address the modifiable adherence barriers (try heated humidification, refit the mask) before concluding CPAP has failed. His usage (2-3 nights/week, ~2 hours/night) falls short of the CMS adherence threshold (≥4 hours/night on ≥70% of nights), but the barriers described — nasal dryness and mask discomfort — are classic modifiable adherence problems. These should be addressed (humidification, mask refitting, behavioral support) before CPAP is labeled a failure or the patient is escalated to surgery.

11. Treatment of CSA

CSA management is dictated by the underlying cause — there is no single default therapy the way CPAP is default for OSA.

CSA SyndromeMechanismTherapy
Sleep transition apneaCO₂ fluctuation during sleep-wake transitionsReassurance; occasionally hypnotics or O₂
Chronic narcotic therapyLoss of central driveReduce narcotic dose; consider PAP device
Cheyne-Stokes breathingHigh loop gain from robust chemosensitivity/ventilatory drive (heart failure)Optimize HF medical therapy; consider PAP device
Idiopathic central apneaUnknownSupportive; bilevel PAP; consider ventilatory stimulants
Treatment-emergent ("complex") apneaLower airway resistance at CPAP initiation improves CO₂ excretion efficiencyReassurance — generally resolves spontaneously
Sleep hypoventilation syndromes↓Drive with loss of wakefulness stimulus + ↓accessory muscle activity in REMNoninvasive ventilation
Heart Failure Caution Do not select advanced PAP therapy (e.g., adaptive servo-ventilation) for CSA without first knowing the heart failure phenotype and ejection fraction — therapy choice requires individualized specialist assessment (ASV has shown harm in some HFrEF populations).
USMLE-Style Check
A patient with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction is found to have predominant Cheyne-Stokes breathing with central apneas on polysomnography. Which statement about management is most accurate?
Answer: Management should focus on optimizing heart failure therapy, with advanced PAP selection individualized to his ejection fraction and specialist input. CSA related to heart failure is treated first by optimizing the underlying cardiac disease. Advanced PAP options (including adaptive servo-ventilation) can help but must be individualized to the heart failure phenotype and ejection fraction, since ASV has been associated with harm in certain HFrEF populations — it is not a one-size-fits-all first step. Weight loss is not an effective CSA therapy in a non-obese patient with heart failure-driven CSA.
