1. Overview & Approach to Arrhythmias

Arrhythmias can manifest as early beats, bigeminal (grouped) beats, pauses, bradycardia, tachycardia, or chaotic rhythms.

Case 1

A 59-year-old male with obesity and hypertension presents with palpitations for 3 hours, with similar short episodes over the past couple weeks, commonly early in the morning. No chest pain or dyspnea.

Vitals: P 132, RR 20, BP 148/90, T 98.8°F, SaO₂ 97% on RA.
Exam: Alert, anxious, no diaphoresis; heart irregularly irregular rate/rhythm, +S3, 3/6 systolic murmur best at apex; no JVD; small nontender thyroid; no peripheral edema; pulses 2+ and irregularly irregular in carotids, radials, DP; lungs clear.

What test would you order next? — A 12-lead EKG, which shows an undulating baseline with no distinct P waves: atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular rate.

Practice Questions

A patient presents with an irregularly irregular pulse, no discernible P waves, and an undulating baseline on EKG. What is the most likely diagnosis?

An irregularly irregular rhythm with an undulating baseline and no distinct P waves is the classic EKG description of atrial fibrillation. Atrial flutter instead shows a regular "sawtooth" pattern.

Which physical exam finding in Case 1 is most specific for identifying the underlying rhythm before the EKG is obtained?

An irregularly irregular pulse on palpation is the classic bedside clue for atrial fibrillation, prompting EKG confirmation.

2. Palpitations

A subjective symptom of a rapid or irregular heartbeat: fluttering, flip-flopping, pounding, sensation of skipped beats, lightheadedness, dizziness, chest/neck discomfort, dyspnea, exertional intolerance, near-syncope, or syncope.

Causes

CategoryExamples
CardiacArrhythmias, valvular disease, high-output states
Metabolic/EndocrineHyperthyroidism, hypoglycemia, pheochromocytoma
Substance useCaffeine, cocaine, amphetamines, alcohol, nicotine
MedicationsSympathomimetics, anticholinergics, vasodilators
PsychiatricAnxiety, panic disorder, somatization disorder

Practice Questions

A patient reports palpitations and is found to have a low TSH. Which category of cause is most likely?

Hyperthyroidism is a classic metabolic/endocrine cause of palpitations (along with hypoglycemia and pheochromocytoma) and should be screened for with a TSH.

Which of the following medication classes is a recognized cause of palpitations?

Sympathomimetics (e.g., decongestants, beta agonists), anticholinergics, and vasodilators are recognized medication causes of palpitations.

3. Paroxysmal Tachycardia — Localizing the Focus

Paroxysmal = sudden onset, comes and goes. An automaticity focus suddenly paces the heart at a rapid rate, usually 150–250 bpm. Determine which of 3 levels holds the increased automaticity focus:

Focus LevelP WavesQRS
AtrialPresentNarrow
JunctionalAbsent or invertedNarrow
VentricularAbsentWide

Practice Questions

A rhythm strip shows a rate of 180 bpm with absent P waves and a wide QRS complex. Where is the automaticity focus?

Absent P waves with a wide QRS localizes the automaticity focus to the ventricle. Junctional foci also lack normal P waves (or show inverted P waves) but produce a narrow QRS.

A paroxysmal tachycardia shows a narrow QRS with inverted P waves. Where is the focus most likely located?

Junctional foci produce absent or inverted P waves with a narrow QRS, since conduction still proceeds normally through the His-Purkinje system.

4. Atrial Fibrillation — Signs, Symptoms, EKG, Epidemiology & Risk Factors

Signs & Symptoms

  • Fatigue, irregular pulse.
  • May have: rapid heart rate, hypotension, MI, tachycardia-induced myocardial dysfunction.
  • ~90% of patients have asymptomatic recurrent episodes lasting up to 48 hours.

EKG

  • Irregularly irregular rhythm; fine or coarse atrial activity (no discrete P waves).

Epidemiology

  • Affects ~3% of adults; slightly more common in men; higher in older adults (~10% of adults >85).

Risk Factors

  • Subclinical atherosclerosis, increased left atrial size, mitral regurgitation, premature atrial contractions, pericarditis, subclinical hyperthyroidism.
  • Alcohol (heavy use or withdrawal), NSAID use, corticosteroids.
  • Obesity (10% weight loss significantly reduces A-fib recurrence), obstructive sleep apnea, pulmonary embolism, systolic hypertension, rheumatoid arthritis, parental history of A-fib.

Practice Questions

What percentage of adults are affected by atrial fibrillation, and how does prevalence change with age?

A-fib affects about 3% of adults overall, is slightly more common in men, and rises substantially with age, reaching roughly 10% in adults older than 85.

Which lifestyle intervention has been shown to significantly reduce the recurrence of atrial fibrillation?

A roughly 10% reduction in body weight has been shown (Pathak et al., LEGACY study) to significantly reduce recurrence of atrial fibrillation, making weight loss a first-line lifestyle intervention.

Which of the following is NOT a recognized risk factor for atrial fibrillation?

Subclinical hyperthyroidism (not hypothyroidism) is a recognized risk factor for A-fib. OSA, PE, and rheumatoid arthritis are all also on the risk-factor list.

Practice Questions

A patient reports palpitations occurring roughly once every 3 weeks, with a normal resting EKG in clinic. Which monitoring strategy is most appropriate?

A Holter monitor only captures 24–48 hours and would likely miss symptoms occurring every few weeks. An event monitor, worn for 2+ weeks and triggered by the patient during symptoms, is better suited to infrequent episodes.

Which lab test is ordered specifically to evaluate for possible heart failure in a patient with new atrial fibrillation?

BNP (or NT-proBNP) is used to evaluate for heart failure, which is both a cause and a consequence of atrial fibrillation.

6. A-fib Classification, Consequences & CHA₂DS₂-VASc Score

Classification

Consequences

  • Heart failure: ↓ cardiac output from decreased filling time and loss of atrial kick.
  • Myocardial ischemia.
  • Thrombus formation → stroke (left atrial appendage is the common site). Risk of stroke ~5%/year in untreated A-fib, higher with additional risk factors.

Practice Questions

A patient has had atrial fibrillation continuously for 14 months, and the patient and clinician have not yet decided to abandon a rhythm-control strategy. How is this classified?

A-fib lasting more than 12 months is classified as long-standing (chronic) persistent. "Permanent" specifically requires a joint decision to stop pursuing rhythm control, which hasn't occurred here.

A patient with atrial fibrillation has a CHA₂DS₂-VASc score of 2. What is the most appropriate anticoagulation recommendation?

A CHA₂DS₂-VASc score of 2 or higher warrants anticoagulation, corresponding to an adjusted stroke rate of 2.2%/year in this scoring system.

Where does thrombus formation most commonly occur in atrial fibrillation, leading to stroke?

The left atrial appendage is the classic site of stasis and thrombus formation in atrial fibrillation because of loss of coordinated atrial contraction.

7. A-fib Treatment — Unstable vs. Stable, Cardioversion vs. Defibrillation

Hemodynamically Unstable Patient

  • Hospitalize.
  • IV beta-blockers or calcium channel blockers for rate control.
  • Urgent synchronous electrical cardioversion if: severe hypotension/shock, pulmonary edema, or myocardial ischemia/infarction.
  • High stroke risk with cardioversion if in A-fib >48 hours without anticoagulation — but if unstable, there may be no choice.

Hemodynamically Stable Patient

  • Address underlying cause and lifestyle factors (weight loss, exercise); ~2/3 spontaneously convert to sinus rhythm within 24 hours of a first episode.
  • Rate control mainstay: beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, or digoxin.
  • Start anticoagulation per CHA₂DS₂-VASc score.
  • Cardioversion (electrical or chemical) considered for: recent-onset (1st episode) A-fib, identifiable precipitating factor, or symptomatic patients despite rate control.
  • If symptoms >48 hours or unknown duration: anticoagulate for 3 weeks before and a minimum of 4 weeks after cardioversion to prevent thromboembolic stroke. A transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) can rule out atrial thrombus and allow earlier cardioversion.

Cardioversion vs. Defibrillation

CardioversionDefibrillation
Timing of shockSynchronized to QRS complexAsynchronous, delivered randomly during cardiac cycle
Used forMost organized abnormal rhythms (e.g., A-fib, A-flutter)Ventricular fibrillation
Outcome noteDoesn't clearly improve exercise tolerance/QOL vs. rate control + anticoagulation alone in A-fibLife-saving in V-fib (pulseless)

Practice Questions

A patient with new-onset atrial fibrillation of unknown duration is hemodynamically stable and desires cardioversion. What is the appropriate anticoagulation strategy?

For A-fib of unknown duration or >48 hours, standard practice is 3 weeks of anticoagulation before and a minimum of 4 weeks after cardioversion, unless a TEE excludes atrial thrombus, allowing earlier cardioversion.

A patient in atrial fibrillation develops pulmonary edema and hypotension. What is the most appropriate immediate management?

A hemodynamically unstable patient (pulmonary edema, hypotension) with A-fib requires hospitalization and urgent synchronous electrical cardioversion — waiting for anticoagulation is not appropriate when the patient is unstable.

What key feature distinguishes defibrillation from cardioversion?

Defibrillation delivers an unsynchronized shock at a random point in the cardiac cycle and is used for ventricular fibrillation, whereas cardioversion is synchronized to the QRS and used for organized rhythms like A-fib.

8. Anticoagulants for Atrial Fibrillation

Drug(s)MechanismKey Points
Rivaroxaban, Apixaban, EdoxabanFactor Xa inhibitorsNo routine monitoring needed; expensive; reversal agents now available
DabigatranDirect thrombin inhibitorNo routine monitoring; expensive; reversal agent available
WarfarinVitamin K antagonist"Gold standard"; requires routine INR monitoring (target 2–3); preferred for mechanical heart valves, moderate-severe mitral stenosis, advanced kidney disease; inexpensive; reversed with vitamin K
Pearl: DOACs (dabigatran, rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban) are preferred over warfarin in most patients with non-valvular A-fib due to no need for routine INR monitoring and fewer dietary restrictions — but they are more expensive, and warfarin remains preferred for mechanical valves, significant mitral stenosis, and advanced kidney disease.

Practice Questions

A patient with a mechanical mitral valve is diagnosed with atrial fibrillation. Which anticoagulant is preferred?

Warfarin remains the preferred anticoagulant for patients with mechanical heart valves; DOACs are not indicated in this setting.

What is a key advantage of DOACs (e.g., apixaban, dabigatran) over warfarin?

DOACs do not require routine coagulation monitoring and have fewer dietary interactions than warfarin, though they are more expensive and not preferred in mechanical valves or significant mitral stenosis.

9. Antiarrhythmic Drugs & Refractory A-fib

Rate Control

  • Beta-blockers; calcium channel blockers (non-dihydropyridine, e.g., verapamil/diltiazem); digoxin.

Rhythm Control

  • Class IC (flecainide, propafenone): most commonly used class I agents; primarily treat atrial arrhythmias, usually with AV nodal blockers to prevent 1:1 conduction of atrial flutter. Toxicity → QRS widening. Contraindicated in ischemic or structural heart disease (risk of ventricular arrhythmias/death).
  • Class II (beta-blockers) and Class IV (non-dihydropyridine CCBs): inhibit arrhythmia induction and AV conduction, decreasing rate in SVT/atrial arrhythmias.
  • Class III (sotalol, dofetilide): treat atrial and ventricular arrhythmias; typically initiated inpatient with regular QTc monitoring; caution in kidney disease. Dofetilide is notorious for dangerous drug-drug interactions.
  • Amiodarone (Class III, multi-channel blocker): used for recurrent VT or A-fib; low proarrhythmic risk, but associated with thyroid, liver, lung, and eye toxicity plus neurologic side effects. Monitor thyroid/liver function every 6 months; annual pulmonary function tests and ophthalmic exams. Interacts with warfarin, statins, and digoxin.
  • Dofetilide (Class III): used in paroxysmal A-fib without overt heart failure.
  • Digoxin (excluded from Vaughan-Williams): positive inotrope that also ↑ vagal activity → lower resting HR; used for rate control in A-fib.
  • Adenosine (excluded from Vaughan-Williams): used acutely to interrupt AV conduction and terminate SVT; also helps diagnose the arrhythmia mechanism.
Caution: Flecainide/ibutilide are most effective for chemical cardioversion but only 30–50% effective overall, and some antiarrhythmics are pro-arrhythmic by prolonging the QT interval, risking Torsades de Pointes.

Refractory Atrial Fibrillation

Defined as persistent symptoms despite rate control that limit activity.

  • Catheter ablation of irritable foci (cryotherapy or radiofrequency) — successful ~70% of the time, minimally invasive.
  • Surgical ablation (Maze procedure).
  • Radiofrequency ablation of the AV node + permanent pacemaker — last resort.

Practice Questions

A patient with a history of ischemic heart disease is being considered for rhythm control of atrial fibrillation. Which class of antiarrhythmic is contraindicated?

Class IC agents are contraindicated in patients with ischemic or structural heart disease because of the increased risk of promoting ventricular arrhythmias and death.

A patient on amiodarone for recurrent atrial fibrillation should be monitored regularly for which of the following?

Amiodarone carries thyroid, liver, lung, and eye toxicity risks, requiring thyroid/liver function tests every 6 months and annual pulmonary function testing and ophthalmic exams.

A patient with refractory atrial fibrillation despite rate control undergoes catheter ablation. What is the approximate success rate?

Catheter ablation of irritable foci is successful approximately 70% of the time and is minimally invasive, making it a preferred option before considering AV node ablation with pacemaker placement.

10. Escape Rhythms & Premature Beats — General Concepts

Escape Rhythms

Occur in response to a pause in sinus rhythm — a backup pacemaker rescues the heart.

Escape RhythmRate (bpm)EKG Appearance
Atrial escape60+Pause, then change in P-wave morphology
Junctional escape40–60Pause, then narrow-complex junctional beats
Ventricular escape20–40Pause, then ventricular escape beat

Premature Beats

  • An automaticity focus fires spontaneously, creating a beat earlier than expected (premature beat/contraction) — often looks different than other beats.
  • A short pause typically follows a premature beat (due to prolonged repolarization) before the previous rhythm resumes.
  • Can occur at any level below the SA node: atrial (PAC), junctional, or ventricular (PVC).
Escape vs. premature — the key distinction: In an escape beat, the pause comes before a different-looking beat (a backup pacemaker rescuing a pause). In a premature beat, the different-looking beat comes early, and the pause follows after it.

Practice Questions

Which escape rhythm has the slowest intrinsic rate?

Ventricular escape rhythms are the slowest backup pacemakers (20–40 bpm), reflecting their location furthest down the conduction system from the SA node.

What is the key distinguishing feature between an escape beat and a premature beat?

Escape beats rescue a pause (pause first, then the rescuing beat), while premature beats occur early and are followed by a compensatory pause.

11. Premature Ventricular Contractions (PVCs)

Also called ventricular premature beats (VPBs) — isolated beats originating from ventricular tissue.

Symptoms

  • Irregular heartbeat, "skipped" heartbeat.

EKG

  • Wide QRS complexes (≥120 msec) with morphology differing from the patient's normal beats.
  • Not preceded by a P wave; retrograde ventriculoatrial conduction may occur.

Consequences

  • Usually none — 80% of healthy people have occasional PVCs.
  • Sudden death may occur when PVCs are associated with heart disease or if they increase during exercise. PVC burden is the most important predictor.

Causes

CategoryExamples
Associated conditionsHeart failure, MI, myocarditis, hypertension, LVH, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, congenital heart disease, idiopathic VT, hypokalemia/hypomagnesemia, hypoxia (COPD, sleep apnea), endocrine disorders
MedicationsDigoxin, aminophylline, tricyclic antidepressants, sympathomimetics (decongestants, antihistamines, beta agonists)
Substance useNicotine, alcohol, caffeine, cocaine, amphetamines

Workup (High-Risk Patients)

  • CMP + magnesium, CBC, BNP, TSH, drug screen if stimulant use suspected, Holter monitor (24–48 hr), exercise stress testing, echocardiogram or other testing for suspected associated conditions.

Treatment

  • Avoid stimulants and alcohol.
  • Reassurance if no structural/conduction abnormality and PVC burden <10%.
  • Manage comorbid conditions (including heart disease).
  • Antiarrhythmics usually not required — beta-blockers if significantly symptomatic.
  • Catheter ablation for refractory patients.

Practice Questions

A healthy 35-year-old with no cardiac history is found to have occasional PVCs on routine EKG and is asymptomatic. What is the most appropriate management?

Occasional PVCs are common in healthy individuals (80% prevalence) and are generally benign in the absence of structural heart disease or high PVC burden — reassurance is appropriate.

Which single factor is the most important predictor of adverse outcomes in patients with PVCs and underlying heart disease?

PVC burden (the proportion of total beats that are PVCs) is the most important predictor of risk, particularly when PVCs occur in the setting of underlying heart disease or increase during exercise.

Which EKG feature is characteristic of a PVC?

PVCs show a wide QRS complex (≥120 msec) with a different morphology from the patient's normal beats and are not preceded by a P wave.

12. Premature Atrial Contractions (PACs)

  • An automaticity atrial focus (not the SA node) fires a heartbeat earlier than expected.
  • P′ wave contour, PR interval, and timing differ from a normal SA-node-generated beat.
  • Conducted normally through the AV node and ventricles → narrow QRS.
  • Generally benign.
FeatureFinding
RateUsually normal before the premature beat
RegularityOccasional irregular beat
P wavesPremature P′ wave of altered shape
PR interval0.12–0.20 sec
QRS duration0.04–0.12 sec (narrow)

Practice Questions

A PAC has a narrow QRS despite arising from an ectopic atrial focus. Why?

Even though the P′ wave originates from an ectopic atrial focus rather than the SA node, conduction proceeds normally through the AV node and His-Purkinje system, producing a narrow QRS.

What differentiates the P wave of a PAC from a normal sinus P wave?

Because the PAC arises from an ectopic atrial focus rather than the SA node, its P′ wave morphology, PR interval, and timing differ from the normal sinus P wave.

13. Bradycardia, Tachy-Brady Syndrome & AV Blocks

Case 2

An 80-year-old male with prior MI and heart failure is found on the floor after repeated syncope ("passing out"). EKG in the ED shows atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response alternating with severe bradycardia — each bradycardic episode triggers syncope. This is tachy-brady syndrome (sick sinus syndrome). He is treated with a temporary transvenous pacemaker with resolution of symptoms until a permanent pacemaker can be placed.

Bradycardia

  • Defined as HR <60; can be normal in healthy athletic persons or at rest/sleep.
  • Concerning if symptomatic or HR <40.
  • Causes: sinus node dysfunction, right coronary artery sclerosis, intracranial hypertension; medications (beta-blockers, CCBs, antiarrhythmics, digoxin); infections (Lyme disease); cardiac trauma (blunt force, catheters, surgery).
  • Workup: TSH, CMP, exercise treadmill test (assess chronotropic competence), Holter monitor.

AV Blocks

TypeEKG FindingPrognosis
First degreePR interval >0.20 sec↑ risk of A-fib and all-cause mortality
Second degree, Mobitz I (Wenckebach)Progressively lengthening PR interval until a P wave is not conducted (dropped QRS), then the cycle resetsBenign; improves with exercise/↑ sympathetic tone
Second degree, Mobitz IIFixed PR interval with intermittently dropped QRS (e.g., 3:1 conduction — 3 P waves per QRS)High risk of progression to complete (3rd degree) block; block usually below the AV node
Third degree (complete)P waves march out completely independent of QRS complexes (AV dissociation)Complete block at the AV node; causes severe bradycardia

Practice Questions

An EKG shows P waves marching out completely independently of QRS complexes, with no relationship between the two. What is this rhythm?

Complete AV dissociation — P waves and QRS complexes marching out independently — is diagnostic of third degree (complete) heart block.

An EKG shows progressively lengthening PR intervals until a QRS is dropped, then the pattern resets. Which AV block is this, and what is its typical prognosis?

Progressive PR prolongation followed by a dropped beat is the classic pattern of Mobitz type I (Wenckebach) second-degree AV block, which generally has a benign prognosis and improves with increased sympathetic tone.

An elderly patient with alternating episodes of rapid atrial fibrillation and severe symptomatic bradycardia, with syncope during the bradycardic episodes, most likely has which condition?

Alternating tachyarrhythmia (often A-fib) with severe bradycardia, causing syncope during the slow phases, is the hallmark of tachy-brady (sick sinus) syndrome, often ultimately requiring pacemaker placement.

14. Pacemakers & Paced Rhythms

Indications

  • Symptomatic bradycardia without a reversible cause.
  • Asymptomatic bradycardia with significant pauses (>3 seconds) or persistent HR <40 bpm.
  • Alternating bundle branch block.
  • Atrial fibrillation with 5-second pauses.
  • Second-degree Mobitz type II or third degree AV block.

Paced Rhythms

  • Sharp depolarization "spikes" on EKG — can be atrial, ventricular, or AV sequential pacers.
  • Atrial paced rhythm: spike before each P wave.
  • Ventricular paced rhythm: spike before each ventricular beat.
  • AV sequential pacemaker: both an atrial spike and a ventricular spike.
  • Paced ventricular beats are no longer diagnostic for findings like bundle branch block or myocardial infarction.

Practice Questions

Which of the following is an indication for permanent pacemaker placement?

Third degree (complete) AV block is a clear indication for pacemaker placement, along with symptomatic bradycardia, significant pauses (>3 sec), persistent HR <40, alternating bundle branch block, and Mobitz type II block.

Why can't a paced ventricular rhythm be used to diagnose a bundle branch block or myocardial infarction on EKG?

Ventricular pacing produces a wide, paced QRS morphology (originating from the pacing lead rather than native conduction), which masks the EKG changes normally used to diagnose bundle branch block or MI.

15. Ventricular Arrhythmias & ICDs

Ventricular Tachycardia (VT)

  • Coordinated contractions of ventricular origin; rapid rate reduces chamber filling time → ↓ cardiac output → can be fatal.

Ventricular Fibrillation (VF)

  • Ventricles vibrate without coordinated contraction — incompatible with life; patient is pulseless and requires immediate defibrillation.

Torsades de Pointes

  • Possibly caused by 2 competing automaticity foci in different ventricular areas; occurs in patients with a prolonged QT interval (congenital, electrolyte disturbance, or medication-related).
FeatureFinding
Rate>100 bpm (usually 250–350)
RegularityIrregular — "twisted streamer" look
P wavesNone
PR intervalNone
QRS duration>0.12 sec

Implantable Cardioverter-Defibrillator (ICD) Indications

  • Long QT syndrome.
  • Ventricular arrhythmias (VT, VF, Torsades de Pointes).
  • Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.
  • Brugada syndrome.
  • Certain heart failure patients with reduced ejection fraction.

Practice Questions

A pulseless patient is found to be in ventricular fibrillation. What is the immediate treatment?

Ventricular fibrillation is incompatible with life and a pulseless patient requires immediate asynchronous defibrillation, not synchronized cardioversion (which requires an organized QRS to sync to).

A patient develops Torsades de Pointes. Which underlying abnormality is most classically associated with this rhythm?

Torsades de Pointes occurs in the setting of a prolonged QT interval, which may be congenital, due to electrolyte disturbances, or medication-induced (including some antiarrhythmics).

Which of the following is NOT a standard indication for ICD placement?

Occasional PVCs in an otherwise healthy patient are benign and do not warrant ICD placement. ICDs are reserved for long QT syndrome, malignant ventricular arrhythmias, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Brugada syndrome, and certain HFrEF patients.

16. Other Supraventricular Rhythms — Wandering Pacemaker, MAT, Atrial Flutter, SVT, AVNRT, AVRT, WPW

Wandering Atrial Pacemaker

Pacemaker activity produced by an atrial focus that changes from beat to beat, producing varying P′ wave morphology (needs ≥3 distinct P-wave morphologies).

FeatureFinding
Rate60–100 bpm
RegularityIrregular
P wavesP′ wave shape varies
PR interval0.12–0.20 sec
QRS duration0.04–0.12 sec

Multifocal Atrial Tachycardia (MAT)

Wandering atrial pacemaker that accelerates into the tachycardic range; associated with pulmonary disease (e.g., COPD). Requires ≥3 distinct P′ wave morphologies.

FeatureFinding
Rate>100 bpm
RegularityIrregular
P wavesP′ wave shape varies
PR interval0.12–0.20 sec (can be shortened)
QRS duration0.04–0.12 sec

Atrial Flutter

An atrial focus produces rapid, identical depolarization "flutter" waves in a sawtooth pattern. Only 1 in a series of flutter waves is conducted through the AV node, usually in a predictable ratio (2:1, 3:1, 4:1). May be due to a reentry circuit.

FeatureFinding
RateVariable (normal or fast)
RegularityRegular
P wavesFlutter waves ("sawtooth" baseline)
PR intervalNone
QRS duration0.04–0.12 sec

Supraventricular Tachycardia (SVT)

"Wastebasket" term for tachycardia originating from an automaticity focus above the ventricles (SA node, atria, AV node, bundle of His). Narrow QRS; P waves may be lost in preceding T waves. Commonly paroxysmal; may be due to congenital accessory conduction pathways.

  • Vagal maneuvers (Valsalva, carotid sinus massage) are first-line therapy to acutely restore sinus rhythm.
  • Adenosine can terminate SVT and simultaneously help diagnose the mechanism: AV-node-dependent tachycardias terminate with adenosine, while continued P waves during adenosine suggest atrial flutter/tachycardia (AV block is unmasked but the atrial rhythm continues).

AV Nodal Reentrant Tachycardia (AVNRT)

  • Two-thirds of SVT (excluding atrial flutter/fibrillation).
  • Reentrant circuit within the AV node using both fast and slow pathways.
  • Short RP interval with a retrograde P wave very close to the QRS.
  • Terminated with vagal maneuvers or adenosine; prevented with beta-blockers or CCBs.
  • Catheter ablation is highly successful but carries a 1% risk of AV node injury requiring pacemaker placement.

Atrioventricular Reciprocating Tachycardia (AVRT)

  • Accessory-pathway-mediated tachycardia, often with pre-excitation (delta wave) on EKG.
  • Shortened PR interval; initial QRS is slurred (premature ventricular depolarization near the accessory pathway).
  • Anterograde conduction over the AV node in 90–95% of cases → narrow complex QRS.

Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW) Syndrome

Accessory pathway (bundle of Kent) bypasses the AV node → part of the ventricle depolarizes prematurely ("pre-excitation") → delta wave (upsloping of PR into the QRS). Can create dangerous paroxysmal reentrant tachycardia.

FeatureFinding
RateVariable
RegularityRegular
P wavesNormal
PR interval<0.12 sec (shortened)
QRS duration>0.1 sec (widened, delta wave)

Practice Questions

A patient with COPD develops an irregular tachycardia with at least 3 distinct P-wave morphologies. What is the most likely diagnosis?

MAT is a wandering atrial pacemaker accelerated into a tachycardic rate, requiring ≥3 distinct P′ wave morphologies, and is classically associated with pulmonary disease such as COPD.

An EKG shows a regular sawtooth baseline with a 3:1 atrial-to-ventricular conduction ratio. What is this rhythm?

A sawtooth baseline with a predictable conduction ratio (e.g., 2:1, 3:1, 4:1) is characteristic of atrial flutter, distinguishing it from the irregular, no-discrete-P-wave pattern of atrial fibrillation.

During an SVT, adenosine is administered and the tachycardia terminates abruptly. What does this suggest about the mechanism?

Tachycardias that terminate with adenosine are typically AV-node-dependent (AVNRT, AVRT). If continued P waves/atrial activity persist despite AV block from adenosine, this points to atrial flutter or atrial tachycardia instead.

A patient's EKG shows a shortened PR interval and a delta wave. Which diagnosis is most consistent with these findings?

A shortened PR interval with a delta wave (slurred upstroke of the QRS) is the classic EKG signature of WPW syndrome, caused by pre-excitation through the accessory bundle of Kent.
