How to Read Every EKG

The non-negotiable sequence: RATE → RHYTHM → AXIS → INTERVALS/QRS → CONDUCTION → ST–T/QT → SYNTHESIS. Run this sequence even when the diagnosis looks obvious — it prevents anchoring and makes hard tracings manageable. First confirm calibration: paper speed 25 mm/sec, voltage 10 mm/mV.

Rate & Rhythm

QuestionWhat to look forClinical reasoning
Rate300 ÷ large boxes between R waves, or 1500 ÷ small boxesNarrows the differential but does not name the rhythm
RegularityRegular, regularly irregular, or irregularly irregular?Irregularly irregular strongly favors AFib; patterned irregularity suggests another mechanism
P wavesPresent? Normal morphology? One before every QRS? Multiple morphologies?Tells you whether the atria are driving the ventricles
QRSNarrow or wide?Narrow = activation via the His-Purkinje system; wide raises BBB, ventricular rhythm, pacing, or pre-excitation
P:QRS relationshipFixed PR? Progressive PR change? Dropped QRS? AV dissociation?The key to distinguishing sinus rhythms from AV block and junctional/ventricular rhythms
High-yield rhythm discrimination: Afib = no organized P waves + irregularly irregular response. Atrial flutter = organized atrial activity, classic sawtooth. MAT = irregular rhythm, ≥3 distinct P-wave morphologies, atrial rate >100 (COPD/CHF context). SVT/AVNRT/AVRT = regular narrow-complex tachycardia, P waves hidden or retrograde (WPW = accessory pathway). VT = regular wide-complex tachycardia; AV dissociation, capture beats, or fusion beats strongly support it. Junctional rhythm = absent/inverted/retrograde P waves with a narrow QRS.

Axis & Intervals

AxisUse leads I and aVF first. I+/aVF+ = normal. I+/aVF− = leftward. I−/aVF+ = rightward. Both negative = extreme axis.
PRProlonged → AV nodal delay. Short PR + delta wave + widened QRS → WPW.
QRS≥0.12s = complete bundle-branch block or another cause of delayed ventricular activation.
QT/QTcProlonged repolarization creates torsades risk — always connect a long QT to medications, electrolytes, or congenital causes.
Interpretation habit: don't stop at "left axis deviation" or "wide QRS." Ask what diagnosis explains it in this patient.

Conduction: AV Block vs. Bundle-Branch Block

PatternECG clueWhere/why it mattersAction-level meaning
1° AV blockPR >0.20s; every P conductsDelayed AV conductionRecognize; significance depends on context
Mobitz I (Wenckebach)Progressive PR lengthening → dropped QRSUsually AV nodalOften transient/benign
Mobitz IIConstant PR on conducted beats + sudden dropped QRSUsually His-Purkinje; may have wide QRSMore dangerous; may progress to complete block
3° AV blockP waves and QRS complexes march independentlyComplete AV dissociationUnstable bradycardia risk; pacing often required
RBBBQRS ≥0.12s; RSR′ in V1–V2; wide S in I/V6Delayed RV activationA conduction abnormality, not a rhythm
LBBBQRS ≥0.12s; broad/notched R in I, aVL, V5–V6; negative V1Delayed LV activationSecondary ST-T changes can complicate ischemia interpretation
Clinical discriminator: a dropped beat with progressive PR lengthening is Mobitz I. A dropped beat with unchanged PR intervals is Mobitz II. P waves marching independently through QRS complexes is complete heart block.

Ischemia & Injury: Localize Before Naming the Artery

STEMI evolution: hyperacute T waves → J-point/ST elevation → more pronounced convex ST elevation → Q-wave/R-wave changes → T-wave inversion or normalization. ST elevation in ≥2 contiguous leads = acute injury; ST depression/T inversion may support ischemia/NSTEMI in the right context. Inferior territory = II, III, aVF — inferior STEMI + AV block → think RCA (supplies the AV node). Posterior injury may appear as reciprocal anterior ST depression — don't call anterior ST depression "just ischemia" without considering posterior MI.
Clinical reasoning rule: symptoms + territory + conduction finding can be more diagnostic than any single ECG feature. Example: chest pressure + ST elevation II/III/aVF + complete AV block → inferior STEMI with RCA involvement.

Hypertrophy & Chamber Enlargement

RVH: right axis deviation, tall R in V1 (R>S), dominant S in V6, possible RV strain. LVH: Sokolow-Lyon voltage (S in V1 + R in V5/V6 >35mm) plus supportive strain (ST depression/T inversion in left-sided leads). RAE (P pulmonale): pressure/volume overload — pulmonary HTN, chronic lung disease. LAE (P mitrale): mitral stenosis, LV dysfunction, mitral regurgitation, or other chronic left-sided loading.

The 30-Second Oral Interpretation

"Rate is ___. Rhythm is ___. Axis is ___. PR is ___; QRS is ___; QT/QTc is ___. There is/is not evidence of ___ conduction abnormality. ST-T changes are ___ in leads ___. Overall, this ECG is most consistent with ___, which in this patient suggests ___."
Worked example: "Rate is 72 beats/min. Rhythm is normal sinus. Axis is normal. PR is normal; QRS is narrow; QTc is not prolonged. There is no conduction abnormality. There is ST-segment elevation in II, III, and aVF with reciprocal depression in I and aVL. Overall, this ECG is most consistent with an acute inferior STEMI, which in this patient suggests an RCA culprit lesion."

Five Board-Style Reasoning Checkpoints

1. Older COPD patient, irregular narrow-complex tachycardia — before calling it AFib, inspect P-wave morphology (≥3 distinct morphologies + rate >100 favors MAT).

2. Dropped QRS complexes — Wenckebach vs. Mobitz II is separated by whether the PR interval progressively lengthens before the dropped beat (Mobitz I) or stays constant (Mobitz II).

3. Regular wide-complex tachycardia — AV dissociation, capture beats, or fusion beats push you toward VT.

4. Chest pressure + ST elevation II/III/aVF + complete AV block → inferior STEMI, most consistent with RCA involvement.

5. Short PR + delta wave + widened QRS → pre-excitation via an accessory pathway (WPW).
Mastery target: take an unfamiliar tracing through the same sequence every time, recognize the high-yield patterns, and use the vignette to decide what the ECG means clinically. Pattern recognition gets you the diagnosis; clinical reasoning gets you the point.

Part 1 · Case-Based EKGs

Real patient vignettes paired with their EKGs — CIS I, CIS II & Comprehensive CV Cases

Case 1 — The Missed DialysisCIS I

29 y/o female with diabetic ESRD on hemodialysis presents with nausea, lightheadedness, and weakness after missing 3 days of dialysis (skipped because of a head cold).
Rate84 bpm
RhythmSinus rhythm with 1° AV block
Axis−60°
Key IdentifiersPeaked T waves + prolonged QT interval; PR interval prolonged
Leads to Key In OnPrecordial leads for peaked/tented T waves; any lead for the PR interval
High-yield stem/vignette clues: "Missed dialysis" + ESRD + peaked T waves is a classic hyperkalemia setup — the 1° AV block is an early conduction sign of rising K⁺.
Clinical manifestations & management: Hyperkalemia from missed dialysis. Treat emergently: calcium gluconate (membrane stabilization), insulin/glucose and beta-2 agonists (shift K⁺ intracellularly), potassium binders, and urgent dialysis to remove the potassium load.

Case 2 — Chest Pain Radiating to the ArmCIS I

41 y/o male with chest pain radiating to the left arm, dyspnea, and diaphoresis. Pulse 110, BP 90/50, S3 present, bibasilar crackles 50% up.
Rate114 bpm
RhythmSinus tachycardia
Axis+30°
Key IdentifiersST-segment elevation in I, aVL, V1–V6 with reciprocal changes in III and aVF
Leads to Key In OnI, aVL, V1–V6 for the injury pattern; III/aVF for reciprocal depression
High-yield stem/vignette clues: S3 + crackles + hypotension in the setting of an anterolateral STEMI = early cardiogenic pump failure.
Clinical manifestations & management: Acute anterolateral MI (LAD territory). Reciprocal changes confirm it's a true injury pattern, not just diffuse ST change. Needs emergent revascularization.

Case 3 — The Racing Heart That ResolvedCIS I

48 y/o female felt dizzy and took her own pulse at ~200; by the time she's evaluated she feels fine with a pulse of 68 and a normal exam.
Rate72 bpm
RhythmNormal sinus
Axis−60°
Key IdentifiersDelta wave with short PR interval; Q waves in II, III, aVF represent WPW conduction, not infarction
Leads to Key In OnAny lead for the slurred delta-wave upstroke; inferior leads for the pseudo-infarct Q waves
High-yield stem/vignette clues: A resting EKG can look completely normal-rate but still carry the diagnosis if you catch the delta wave — "felt her pulse at 200, now resolved" = paroxysmal tachycardia via an accessory pathway.
Clinical manifestations & management: WPW syndrome. The paroxysmal tachycardia she felt was likely AVRT using the Bundle of Kent. Definitive treatment is catheter ablation of the accessory pathway.

Case 4 — Heaving Sensation When Lying DownCIS I

73 y/o male with an intermittent "heaving sensation" in his chest for months, worse lying down. No syncope, chest pain, dyspnea, or diaphoresis.
Rate114 bpm
RhythmAtrial fibrillation
Axis+60°
Key IdentifiersAbsent P waves; nonspecific T-wave flattening; no infarction
Leads to Key In OnLead II or V1 rhythm strip for the irregularly irregular baseline and absent organized P waves
High-yield stem/vignette clues: Chronic, intermittent "heaving/fluttering" sensation with no other red flags is a classic benign-sounding presentation of AFib.
Clinical manifestations & management: Atrial fibrillation. Assess rate control and thromboembolic risk (CHA₂DS₂-VASc) even in an asymptomatic-appearing patient.

Case 5 — Uncontrolled Hypertension for a DecadeCIS I

64 y/o male with long-standing HTN (BP consistently 170/98) who hasn't taken his medication regularly because he can't afford it.
Rate150 bpm
RhythmSinus tachycardia
Axis+30°
Key IdentifiersLeft ventricular hypertrophy with strain pattern (ST depression/T inversion in left-sided leads); no infarction
Leads to Key In OnV5–V6, I, aVL for the strain pattern; Sokolow-Lyon voltage (S in V1 + R in V5/V6)
High-yield stem/vignette clues: A decade of uncontrolled, undertreated hypertension is the setup for chronic pressure-overload LVH with a strain pattern.
Clinical manifestations & management: LVH with strain from chronic uncontrolled hypertension. Reinforces the importance of affordable, adherent antihypertensive therapy to prevent this remodeling.

Case 6 — Fatigue After a Medication SwitchCIS I

63 y/o male with 3 weeks of generalized fatigue and weakness after his BP medication was recently changed from chlorthalidone to atenolol.
Rate50 bpm
RhythmSinus bradycardia
Axis0°
Key Identifiers1° AV block (prolonged PR interval); no infarction
Leads to Key In OnAny lead for the PR interval measurement (>0.20 sec)
High-yield stem/vignette clues: Fatigue + a recent switch to a beta-blocker (atenolol) explains both the sinus bradycardia and the AV nodal delay — beta-blockade slows both the SA and AV nodes.
Clinical manifestations & management: Beta-blocker effect on the sinus and AV nodes. Isolated 1° AV block does not require treatment by itself, but the symptomatic bradycardia here warrants reassessing the dose/necessity of the beta-blocker.

Case 7 — The Old "Heart Attack"CIS I

65 y/o male, new patient, reports a "heart attack" five years ago but has no records. You obtain a baseline EKG.
Rate90 bpm
RhythmNormal sinus
Axis+60°
Key IdentifiersQ waves in II, III, aVF consistent with an old inferior wall MI
Leads to Key In OnII, III, aVF for pathologic Q waves (no acute ST changes)
High-yield stem/vignette clues: "History of heart attack, no records" + Q waves without acute ST changes = confirm and risk-stratify, don't dismiss and don't treat as acute.
Clinical manifestations & management: Old inferior MI. The correct next step is to risk-stratify this patient's coronary artery disease for stability (not admit for acute treatment, not dismiss the history, not assume he needs a pacemaker or antiarrhythmic).

Case 8 — Pentamidine and a Prolonged QTCIS I

48 y/o female admitted with hypoxia, diagnosed with PJP pneumonia, given two doses of pentamidine; a routine post-dose EKG is obtained.
Rate100 bpm
RhythmSinus tachycardia
Axis+30°
Key IdentifiersQT prolongation; no infarction
Leads to Key In OnAny lead for QT/QTc measurement
High-yield stem/vignette clues: Pentamidine is a classic drug cause of acquired QT prolongation — the major downstream risk being Torsades de Pointes, not the other listed arrhythmias (3° block, Wenckebach, WPW, AFib).
Clinical manifestations & management: Drug-induced (pentamidine) QT prolongation. Monitor closely and correct any coexisting electrolyte abnormalities (K⁺, Mg²⁺) to reduce Torsades risk.

Case 9 — Gardening in the HeatCIS I

54 y/o woman with chest pain, nausea, vomiting, diaphoresis, and hypotension after pulling weeds in the sun 30 minutes earlier. History of poorly-controlled diabetes and hyperlipidemia.
Rate/RhythmSee inferior MI + right-sided/RV infarct pattern
Key IdentifiersInferior MI pattern with right ventricular infarct involvement
Leads to Key In OnII, III, aVF for the inferior injury; right-sided leads (V4R) to confirm RV infarct
High-yield stem/vignette clues: Inferior MI + hypotension (80/40) is the classic "nitrate-sensitive, preload-dependent" RV infarct combination.
Clinical manifestations & management: For the hypotension of an RV infarct: give IV fluid resuscitation, not nitrates/morphine/diuretics (these drop preload further and can cause severe hypotension in a preload-dependent right ventricle). For the associated arrhythmia risk in acute MI, beta-blockers are part of standard management once hemodynamically appropriate.

Case 10 — Chest Pressure with a DeviceCIS I

78 y/o male with retrosternal chest pressure, diaphoresis, and dyspnea.
Rate75 bpm
RhythmAV sequential (dual-chamber) paced rhythm
Axis+150°
Key IdentifiersPacer spikes before both P wave and QRS; underlying ST/infarction status cannot be assessed
Leads to Key In OnAny lead for the paced spikes preceding both the P wave and QRS complex
High-yield stem/vignette clues: A paced rhythm cannot be used to rule out ischemia by ST-segment criteria — the correct move for concerning chest pain is to pursue cardiac enzymes/troponin, not to reassure or blame musculoskeletal pain.
Clinical manifestations & management: Ischemia cannot be excluded from this EKG because pacing obscures ST-segment interpretation. Admit for urgent evaluation of ischemic heart disease via cardiac biomarker analysis.

Case 11 — Chest Pressure with a Paradoxical Split S2CIS I

72 y/o male with 60 minutes of retrosternal chest pressure and diaphoresis. BP 120/68, pulse 100, and a paradoxically split S2 on exam.
Rate100 bpm
RhythmSinus tachycardia
Axis−60°
Key IdentifiersLBBB (infarction cannot be determined in its presence); biphasic P wave in V1 with a deep negative deflection = left atrial enlargement
Leads to Key In OnV1 for the biphasic P wave (LAE) and QS/rS morphology (LBBB); V5–V6, I, aVL for the broad/notched R wave of LBBB
High-yield stem/vignette clues: A paradoxically split S2 (P2 before A2 during expiration) points to delayed aortic valve closure — classic causes are LBBB, aortic stenosis, HOCM, MI, or an RV pacemaker.
Clinical manifestations & management: New LBBB in the setting of acute chest pain should be treated as a possible MI equivalent; because LBBB obscures standard ST-criteria, use serial troponins and any available prior EKG for comparison to diagnose infarction.

Part 1B · CIS II Cases

Dr. Kinder · 5 vignettes + 16 rapid-fire tracings

Case 1 — Palpitations After a Cross-Country DriveCIS II

72 y/o male with 15-year history of well-controlled COPD, sudden palpitations and dyspnea the day after a cross-country drive (up to 14 hrs/day). EKG one year ago was normal.
Rate80 bpm
RhythmAtrial fibrillation
Axis+30°
Key IdentifiersAbsent P waves, irregularly irregular R-R; no infarction, no hypertrophy
Leads to Key In OnLead I for a prominent S wave, lead III for a Q wave + T-wave inversion (S1Q3T3) if PE is suspected
High-yield stem/vignette clues: Long car ride (prolonged immobility) + new-onset AFib-like presentation is a classic acute pulmonary embolism setup, not a COPD flare, MI, renal failure, or pericarditis.
Clinical manifestations & management: The S1Q3T3 pattern (prominent S in I, Q in III, inverted T in III) is a classic-but-insensitive sign of acute PE and right heart strain — know it as a pattern-recognition pearl, not a rule-out test.

Case 2 — 48 Hours of Chest PainCIS II

46 y/o male, 2 pack/day smoker x 25 years, morbidly obese (350 lb) and diabetic, with 48 hours of chest pain. BP 96/62, pulse 80, crackles throughout.
Rate80 bpm
RhythmNormal sinus
Axis−30°
Key IdentifiersST changes in I, aVL, V1–4 (anterolateral MI) with reciprocal changes in II, III, aVF
Leads to Key In OnI, aVL, V1–V4 for the injury pattern; II/III/aVF for reciprocal change
High-yield stem/vignette clues: A 48-hour delay in presentation plus crackles/borderline hypotension signals a large anterolateral MI already producing pump failure.
Clinical manifestations & management: Anterolateral MI (LAD territory) with evidence of early heart failure (crackles) — high-risk presentation given the delayed presentation.

Case 3 — Left Alone for the WeekendCIS II

83 y/o female with mild dementia, left home alone for the weekend, presents with nausea and lightheadedness. Takes lisinopril, furosemide, and a potassium supplement for CHF; can't recall her dosing.
Rate100 bpm
RhythmJunctional tachycardia
Axis+120°
Key IdentifiersAbsent P waves, extremely peaked T waves, prolonged QT — hyperkalemia
Leads to Key In OnPrecordial leads for tented/peaked T waves; absence of visible P waves
High-yield stem/vignette clues: Dementia + unsupervised access to an ACE inhibitor, a potassium supplement, and a diuretic is a recipe for dangerous hyperkalemia.
Clinical manifestations & management: Immediately stop any potassium source first, then proceed with calcium (membrane stabilization), insulin/glucose and beta-2 agonists (intracellular shift), potassium binders, and diuretics as appropriate.

Case 4 — Found Down, ResuscitatedCIS II

56 y/o male brought in by EMS after being found unresponsive in the field and successfully resuscitated by paramedics. He remains unresponsive though vitally stable now. This is his field EKG.
Rate/RhythmVentricular fibrillation — chaotic, no organized QRS complexes
Key IdentifiersNo discernible P waves, QRS complexes, or T waves; fine/coarse chaotic baseline
High-yield stem/vignette clues: "Found down" + successful resuscitation + a chaotic EKG with no organized complexes = VFib, not VT, Torsades, AFib, or SVT (all of which retain some organized complex).
Clinical manifestations & management: VFib produces no cardiac output and requires immediate CPR and defibrillation; this patient's arrest was almost certainly this rhythm in the field.

Case 5 — Machine vs. Physician InterpretationCIS II

44 y/o male with dizziness and nausea. The machine reads the EKG as "LVH and old inferior wall MI" and you're asked to confirm or override.
Rate110 bpm
RhythmSinus tachycardia
Axis−60°
Key IdentifiersDelta wave with shortened PR interval = WPW; the machine's "LVH" and "inferior MI" reads are pseudo-findings from pre-excitation
Leads to Key In OnAny lead for the slurred delta-wave upstroke and short PR
High-yield stem/vignette clues: This is the classic teaching point that automated EKG machine readings can be fooled by WPW's pseudo-infarction and voltage changes — always verify the computer read yourself.
Clinical manifestations & management: WPW produces a pseudo-infarct pattern in up to 70% of patients (negative delta waves mimicking Q waves, or prominent R waves in V1–3 mimicking posterior MI) and can inflate voltage criteria, mimicking LVH.

Part 1C · Comprehensive CV Cases

Dr. Kinder · 16 EKGs across 13 numbered cases + 3 unnumbered tracings

Case 1 — Chest Pain That's Better Leaning ForwardComprehensive CV Cases

33 y/o male with non-radiating substernal chest pain, improved leaning forward and worse lying supine, 2 weeks after a flu-like illness. Pericardial friction rub along the LSB, some JVD elevation. VS: BP 114/70, P 100, afebrile, SpO2 97%.
Rate~100 bpm
RhythmSinus
Key IdentifiersDiffuse ST-segment elevation throughout the EKG; aVR is inverted (PR depression pattern)
Leads to Key In OnWidespread leads for diffuse (non-territorial) STE; aVR for reciprocal PR elevation/ST depression
High-yield stem/vignette clues: Positional pleuritic chest pain + friction rub + recent viral illness + diffuse (not localized) ST elevation = pericarditis, not MI.
Clinical manifestations & management: Treat with NSAIDs (not tPA, IV fluids, beta-blockers, or PCI/stenting). Order an echocardiogram to evaluate for pericardial effusion/tamponade, which is the feared complication. Confirmatory exam finding: the pericardial friction rub.

Case 2 — Sports Physical with a MurmurComprehensive CV Cases

17 y/o male, asymptomatic with exercise, sports physical. Grade III/VI systolic murmur at the aortic listening post radiating up the neck that softens with squatting.
Key IdentifiersLVH criteria: QRS >35mm, left ventricular strain, left axis deviation
Leads to Key In OnPrecordial leads for voltage criteria and strain pattern
High-yield stem/vignette clues: A murmur that gets SOFTER with squatting (increased venous return/preload, increased afterload) is dynamic outflow obstruction — hypertrophic (obstructive) cardiomyopathy, not fixed aortic stenosis (which would get louder or unchanged with reduced preload maneuvers... actually AS softens with squatting too, but the LVH-pattern EKG plus the murmur physiology here points to HOCM).
Clinical manifestations & management: Asymptomatic HOCM: no treatment restriction mandated outright, but needs echocardiogram and cardiology referral before clearing for competitive sports; risk-stratify for sudden cardiac death. Newer option: cardiac myosin inhibitors (e.g., mavacamten) for symptomatic obstructive HCM.

Case 3 — Appendicitis on the Wrong SideComprehensive CV Cases

23 y/o female with left lower quadrant rebound tenderness and a toxic appearance; pain began peri-umbilical and localized over "McBurney's point on the left." Surgeon is concerned about her EKG before taking her to the OR.
Key IdentifiersEKG complexes inverted relative to normal: lead I QRS negative, aVR QRS positive, precordial leads fail to show normal R-wave progression, P waves inverted
Leads to Key In OnLead I and aVR for the mirror-image inversion; V1–V6 for absent R-wave progression
High-yield stem/vignette clues: Left-sided "McBurney's point" appendicitis + a globally inverted-looking EKG = situs inversus (mirror-image organ arrangement), not cardiac pathology.
Clinical manifestations & management: This is a normal variant for this patient (dextrocardia with situs inversus) — clear her for surgery; the surgical diagnosis is appendicitis on the anatomically mirrored (left) side.

Case 4 — Chest Pain After CocaineComprehensive CV Cases

20 y/o college student with retrosternal chest pain starting 20 minutes after snorting cocaine at a party. T 99.1, BP 184/120, P 122, R 21.
Key IdentifiersST-segment elevation consistent with myocardial infarction
Leads to Key In OnTerritorial leads matching the vessel involved by coronary vasospasm
High-yield stem/vignette clues: Young patient, severe hypertension/tachycardia, and STEMI-pattern changes shortly after cocaine use = cocaine-induced coronary vasospasm, not atherosclerotic MI.
Clinical manifestations & management: Send to the cath lab; avoid pure beta-blockade (unopposed alpha stimulation can worsen vasospasm/hypertension). Treat with benzodiazepines, nitrates, and calcium channel blockers as first-line for cocaine-associated chest pain, alongside standard ACS workup.

Case 5 — Santa in the ColdComprehensive CV Cases

55 y/o male, dressed as Santa, found unresponsive after volunteering outside for 5 hours in December. BP 80/40, P 54, R 10, temp 86.2°F.
Rate54 bpm (bradycardic)
RhythmSinus bradycardia
Key IdentifiersOsborn (J) waves — an extra deflection at the end of the QRS complex; QT prolongation
Leads to Key In OnAny lead, but Osborn waves are most prominent at the QRS-ST junction
High-yield stem/vignette clues: Prolonged cold exposure + hypotension/bradycardia + Osborn waves = hypothermia (core temp <95°F; ~80% of patients show Osborn waves as core temp approaches 86°F).
Clinical manifestations & management: Treatment is core re-warming — not ACS therapy (morphine/O2/nitrates/aspirin), not an ICD, not an antiarrhythmic, and not tPA. Osborn waves resolve with rewarming.

Case 6 — Irritability, Kidney Stone, and a Neck LumpComprehensive CV Cases

54 y/o female with irritability, poor concentration, a recent kidney stone, a solid neck mass, and a 30 pack-year smoking history.
Key IdentifiersShortened QT interval
Leads to Key In OnAny lead for QT measurement (shortened, not prolonged)
High-yield stem/vignette clues: Neck mass + kidney stone + neuropsychiatric symptoms + a short QT = hypercalcemia from a PTH-secreting parathyroid tumor.
Clinical manifestations & management: High ionized calcium shortens the ST segment (and thus the QT interval) — the opposite EKG direction from hypocalcemia. The kidney stone and neck mass are downstream effects of the hypercalcemia/hyperparathyroidism.

Case 7 — Epigastric Pain in a WomanComprehensive CV Cases

58 y/o female with acute mid-upper gastric pain, nausea, and mild dyspnea for about an hour, unrelieved by Tums. No prior CV history; on estrogen/progesterone replacement. New III/VI holosystolic murmur over the mitral valve radiating to the axilla.
Key IdentifiersST-segment and T-wave changes in II, III, and aVF
Leads to Key In OnII, III, aVF for the inferior injury pattern
High-yield stem/vignette clues: "Epigastric pain" in a woman is a well-known atypical MI presentation — always keep ischemia on the differential for atypical GI-sounding complaints, especially with a new murmur.
Clinical manifestations & management: Acute inferior wall MI with a new murmur of mitral regurgitation from papillary muscle dysfunction (a mechanical complication of inferior MI). Pearl: always be suspicious of atypical MI presentations in women.

Case 8 — Diltiazem Given for PalpitationsComprehensive CV Cases

30 y/o female with palpitations, given diltiazem in the ED which resolved her symptoms; she was discharged on oral diltiazem. She died days later.
Key IdentifiersDelta wave (pre-excitation) of WPW syndrome; no P waves (atrial fibrillation)
Leads to Key In OnAny lead for the delta wave; absence of organized P waves for concurrent AFib
High-yield stem/vignette clues: This is the single highest-stakes WPW teaching point in the whole deck: WPW + atrial fibrillation treated with an AV nodal blocker (diltiazem, verapamil, beta-blockers, digoxin, or adenosine) can be fatal.
Clinical manifestations & management: AV nodal blockers block the heart's normal (protective, decremental) pathway and favor 1:1 conduction down the accessory pathway, which can degenerate AFib into ventricular fibrillation. Definitive treatment for WPW is catheter ablation of the accessory pathway.

Posterior Wall MIComprehensive CV Cases

Key IdentifiersPosterior MI classically shows tall R waves and ST depression in the anterior precordial leads (V1–V3) as a mirror-image reciprocal change
Leads to Key In OnV1–V3 for reciprocal ST depression/tall R waves; consider posterior leads V7–V9 to confirm
High-yield stem/vignette clues: Anterior ST depression should always raise the question "could this be a posterior MI?" rather than being called routine anterior ischemia.

Case 9 — Dizziness in a Lifelong SmokerComprehensive CV Cases

50 y/o male with dizziness and a syncopal episode; smokes 2 packs/day for 30 years ("that's his medication").
Key IdentifiersFlat baseline, no P waves, bradycardia — pacing from the AV node
Leads to Key In OnAny lead — absence of P waves with a narrow, bradycardic QRS
High-yield stem/vignette clues: Symptomatic bradycardia (dizziness, syncope) from a junctional escape rhythm is a pacemaker indication, not a job for epinephrine, norepinephrine, amiodarone, or cardioversion.
Clinical manifestations & management: Junctional rhythm with symptomatic bradycardia → pacemaker.

Case 10 — Palpitations and LightheadednessComprehensive CV Cases

62 y/o male with palpitations and lightheadedness.
Key IdentifiersRuns or "salvos" of ventricular tachycardia
Leads to Key In OnAny lead — look for a run of ≥3 wide, bizarre QRS complexes self-terminating back to sinus rhythm
High-yield stem/vignette clues: Nonsustained VT (NSVT) presenting with palpitations/lightheadedness warrants antiarrhythmic risk-stratification.
Clinical manifestations & management: Treatment options include amiodarone, sotalol, procainamide, or an AICD depending on the underlying substrate and risk of sudden cardiac death.

Case 11 — The Skipped Beat on a Routine PhysicalComprehensive CV Cases

72 y/o female for a routine physical, on atorvastatin only, normal vitals, with an occasional "skipped beat" heard on exam.
Key IdentifiersA PVC every third heartbeat (ventricular trigeminy)
Leads to Key In OnRhythm strip pattern: two normal sinus beats, then one PVC, repeating
High-yield stem/vignette clues: An asymptomatic, incidentally found PVC pattern (even trigeminy) in a stable, asymptomatic patient does not automatically require antiarrhythmic therapy.
Clinical manifestations & management: No treatment needed — the patient is stable and asymptomatic. This contrasts with PVCs found in the setting of acute MI or structural heart disease, which carry more concern.

Case 12 — Collapse on the Football FieldComprehensive CV Cases

17 y/o high school football player collapses on the field, found in ventricular tachycardia, defibrillated back to normal sinus rhythm.
Key IdentifiersST elevation in V1–V3 with a right-bundle-branch-block-like appearance
Leads to Key In OnV1–V3 for the coved/saddleback ST elevation pattern
High-yield stem/vignette clues: A young, otherwise healthy athlete who collapses in VT with this specific V1–V3 pattern is Brugada syndrome until proven otherwise (not hypokalemia, WPW, Torsades, or hypercalcemia).
Clinical manifestations & management: Brugada syndrome is a genetic (sodium channelopathy) condition causing dangerous arrhythmias. Genetic testing can help identify affected family members. Definitive management often involves ICD placement for those at risk of sudden cardiac death.

Case 13 — Repeated Doses from a Forgetful PatientComprehensive CV Cases

82 y/o female with early dementia, lives alone, repeatedly re-takes her lisinopril because she forgets she already took it. Presents with dizziness. BP 84/50, P 72, R 16.
Key IdentifiersPeaked T waves from hyperkalemia (K⁺ = 9.6 in this case) due to excess ACE-inhibitor dosing
Leads to Key In OnPrecordial leads for peaked/tented T waves
High-yield stem/vignette clues: Cognitive impairment + inadvertent repeated ACE-inhibitor dosing + hypotension + peaked T waves = severe iatrogenic hyperkalemia.
Clinical manifestations & management: Same hyperkalemia algorithm: stabilize the myocardium (calcium), shift potassium intracellularly (insulin/glucose, beta-2 agonists), remove potassium (binders, dialysis if severe), and address the medication-safety issue at home.

AV Sequential PacingComprehensive CV Cases

Key IdentifiersTwo small pacer spikes per cycle — one atrial spike, one ventricular spike
Leads to Key In OnAny lead — look for the paired spikes immediately preceding the P wave and QRS
Clinical manifestations & management: A ventricular-paced beat mimics an LBBB pattern because the pacing lead sits in the right ventricle, so the right ventricle depolarizes first (just as it does with a native left bundle branch block).

3° AV Block, UnstableComprehensive CV Cases

72 y/o male who collapsed in a store, brought in confused and lethargic by paramedics. BP 70/50.
Key IdentifiersP waves are entirely independent of the QRS complexes (complete AV dissociation)
Leads to Key In OnCompare the P-wave rate to the QRS rate — they march to their own, unrelated beats
High-yield stem/vignette clues: Hemodynamically unstable bradycardia (BP 70/50, confusion) with AV dissociation is complete heart block until proven otherwise.
Clinical manifestations & management: Treatment is pacing (transcutaneous/transvenous as a bridge to permanent pacemaker).

Part 2 · EKG Reference Atlas

One core example per named rhythm/condition, from the didactic CV/Resp II Clinical lecture (Dr. Jois)

Sinus Rhythms

Normal Sinus Rhythm, Sinus Tachycardia & Sinus Bradycardia

RateTachycardia >100 bpm; bradycardia <60 bpm; normal 60–100 bpm
RhythmRegular; P wave before every QRS, normal morphology
IdentifiersUpright P in II, inverted in aVR; PR 0.12–0.20s
High-yield stem clues: Any stem describing a regular rhythm with a normal-appearing P wave before every QRS just needs the rate to classify it.
Sinus tachycardia causes: sepsis, hypovolemia, PE, pain, fear, MI, hyperthyroidism, heart failure. Sinus bradycardia is normal in trained athletes and during sleep, but can reflect sick sinus syndrome, hypothyroidism, or drug effect (beta-blockers, digoxin).

Sinus Bradycardia (isolated example)

Rate<60 bpm (this example: ~43 bpm)
RhythmRegular sinus
IdentifiersNormal P-QRS-T morphology, just slow
Disorder of impulse formation from depression of sinus node activity.

Normal Sinus Arrhythmia

RhythmCyclic variation in rate with respiration (speeds up with inspiration, slows with expiration)
IdentifiersP-wave morphology and PR interval stay normal — only the R-R interval varies
High-yield stem clues: A "phasic" irregularity that tracks the respiratory cycle (not truly irregular like AFib) is normal, especially in younger patients.

Atrial Arrhythmias

Atrial Flutter

RateRegular, steady — classically ~150 bpm ventricular
RhythmNarrow QRS complexes, regular
Identifiers"Sawtooth" flutter-wave pattern in the baseline
High-yield stem clues: Sawtooth baseline in lead II or V1 at a suspiciously round ventricular rate (150, 100, 75) suggests flutter with fixed AV conduction (2:1, 3:1, 4:1 block).
Adenosine or calcium channel blockers slow AV conduction and can unmask the sawtooth pattern (adenosine has more diagnostic than therapeutic value here — it usually won't convert flutter).

Atrial Fibrillation

RateVariable
RhythmIrregularly irregular
IdentifiersNo organized/discrete P waves
High-yield stem clues: "Irregularly irregular" is the single word pairing that should trigger AFib before anything else.
Risk factors: hypertension, heart failure, diabetes, advanced age, hyperthyroidism, structural heart disease. Management centers on rate/rhythm control plus thromboembolic risk stratification (anticoagulation).

Multifocal Atrial Tachycardia (MAT)

Rate>100 bpm
RhythmChaotic/irregular — "wandering atrial pacemaker" at a faster rate
Identifiers≥3 distinct P-wave morphologies
High-yield stem clues: Elderly patient with COPD or CHF and an irregular tachycardia with multiple P-wave shapes = MAT; consider PE if MAT occurs outside a COPD/CHF context.
Rarely life-threatening. Consider theophylline toxicity as a cause. Treat with oxygen, magnesium, or verapamil; avoid beta-blockers if there's significant COPD.

Premature Atrial Contraction (PAC)

RhythmUnderlying sinus rhythm interrupted by an early beat
IdentifiersAn early P wave with a different morphology than the sinus P wave, sometimes not conducted ("PAC with block")
High-yield stem clues: An otherwise-normal rhythm with one oddly-shaped, early P wave here and there is a benign, extremely common PAC.

Junctional Rhythms

Premature Junctional Contraction (PJC)

IdentifiersAn early, narrow QRS with no preceding P wave (or an inverted/retrograde P wave)

Junctional Rhythm (escape)

Rate40–60 bpm intrinsic junctional escape rate
RhythmRegular, narrow QRS
IdentifiersP waves absent, or retrograde/inverted (axis reversed 180° from normal — inverted in II, upright in aVR)
High-yield stem clues: A slow, regular, narrow-complex rhythm with no visible P waves is junctional escape — the AV junction taking over when the sinus node fails or slows excessively.

Accelerated Junctional Rhythm

Rate60–100 bpm (faster than the intrinsic junctional escape rate, but not "tachycardia")
IdentifiersSame P-wave absence/retrograde pattern as junctional escape, just at a faster rate

SVT, AVNRT, AVRT & WPW

Supraventricular Tachycardia (SVT) — general

RateTypically 150–250 bpm
RhythmRegular, narrow QRS (absent aberrancy)
IdentifiersP waves often hidden in or immediately after the QRS

Paroxysmal SVT (PSVT)

RhythmAbrupt onset of a rapid regular narrow-complex rhythm, then abrupt return to sinus
IdentifiersSudden start/stop ("paroxysmal") is itself a diagnostic clue
High-yield stem clues: "Starts beating quickly, then stops and resumes normal sinus" is the classic PSVT description. Triggers: WPW, anemia, dehydration/fatigue, excess caffeine, pseudoephedrine, hyperthyroidism.

AVNRT (AV Nodal Reentrant Tachycardia)

Rate150–250 bpm
RhythmRegular, narrow QRS, driven by a reentrant circuit within the AV node
IdentifiersRetrograde P waves in II/III, or a pseudo-R′ in V1 (superimposed retrograde P); P waves often buried in the QRS
High-yield stem clues: Sudden-onset, sudden-offset palpitations with dizziness/SOB in an otherwise normal heart, sometimes triggered by alcohol, coffee, stimulants, or excitement — the most common SVT in adults (~50%).
Onset and termination are abrupt, usually initiated by a premature supraventricular beat.

AVRT (AV Reentrant Tachycardia)

MechanismAnatomical reentry using an accessory pathway (Bundle of Kent)
IdentifiersOrthodromic AVRT: anterograde via the AV node → regular narrow-complex rhythm (looks like AVNRT). Antidromic AVRT: anterograde via the accessory pathway → regular wide-complex rhythm that can mimic VT
RateRegular, typically rapid
High-yield stem clues: The most common SVT in children (~30%). Often triggered by a premature atrial or ventricular beat. On the surface EKG, orthodromic AVRT is difficult to distinguish from AVNRT — the diagnosis is usually made electrophysiologically.
Retrograde P waves with a long R-P interval support AVRT over AVNRT.

Wolf-Parkinson-White (WPW)

IdentifiersPR interval <120ms; delta wave (slurred, slow initial QRS upstroke); QRS >110ms; discordant ST-T changes; pseudoinfarction pattern in up to 70% of patients
Leads to Key In OnAny lead for the slurred delta-wave upstroke; inferior/anterior leads for pseudo-Q-waves
High-yield stem clues: A congenital accessory pathway (Bundle of Kent) that can degenerate into SVT, AFib, or AFlutter with aberrant/rapid conduction, and can mimic VT on the EKG.
AV-nodal blockers (adenosine, CCBs, beta-blockers, digoxin) are dangerous if WPW coexists with AFib/flutter — they favor 1:1 conduction down the accessory pathway and can precipitate VF. Definitive treatment is catheter ablation.

Ventricular Arrhythmias

Premature Ventricular Contraction (PVC)

IdentifiersWide, bizarre QRS (≥0.12s) not preceded by a premature P wave; usually followed by a compensatory pause
Isolated PVCs in a normal heart are common and rarely require treatment. A PVC during acute MI is more concerning — it can trigger VT/VF.

PVC Patterns — Bigeminy & Trigeminy

IdentifiersBigeminy = 1 sinus beat : 1 PVC, repeating. Trigeminy = 2 sinus beats : 1 PVC, repeating

Ventricular Tachycardia (VT)

Rate>120 bpm (usually 150–220)
Rhythm3 or more consecutive PVCs at a regular rate; wide QRS
IdentifiersAV dissociation, fusion beats, capture beats when present
High-yield stem clues: A regular, wide-complex tachycardia is VT until proven otherwise — don't assume it can't be VT just because the rate seems "too fast" or "too slow."
Classified as sustained (>30s or symptomatic) vs. nonsustained (<30s, asymptomatic), and monomorphic vs. polymorphic. Sustained VT or VT with hemodynamic instability is an emergency requiring immediate treatment.

VT with Fusion & Capture Beats

IdentifiersFusion beat (Dressler's beat): a sinus impulse conducts normally at the same time as the ventricular impulse, producing a hybrid QRS morphology. Capture beat: the sinus impulse fully captures the ventricles, producing a completely normal-looking QRS amid the VT
High-yield stem clues: Fusion and capture beats are strong confirmatory evidence of VT (rather than SVT with aberrancy) when present.

Nonsustained VT (NSVT)

IdentifiersA short run (≥3 beats) of VT lasting <30 seconds, self-terminating, asymptomatic

Polymorphic VT

RhythmUsually irregular; unstable, with continuously varying QRS morphology
Best treated with IV magnesium. A prolonged QT interval raises the risk of developing polymorphic VT — remove offending QT-prolonging drugs and correct K⁺/Ca²⁺ imbalances.

Torsades de Pointes

IdentifiersA distinctive polymorphic VT where the QRS complexes gradually change amplitude and appear to twist/spiral around the isoelectric baseline
High-yield stem clues: Torsades is associated with a prolonged QT (congenital or acquired) — always distinguish it from garden-variety VT because management differs.
First-line treatment is IV magnesium sulfate, given promptly, even before a confirmed low magnesium level.

Ventricular Fibrillation

IdentifiersFine or coarse zigzag pattern; no discernible P waves, QRS complexes, or T waves
High-yield stem clues: The most frequently encountered arrhythmia in adults with sudden death.
No organized perfusion, no pulse. Requires immediate CPR and defibrillation — there is no pharmacologic substitute.

Post-Defibrillation Rhythm / Accelerated Idioventricular Rhythm (AIVR)

Rate50–100 bpm
RhythmRegular, wide QRS (a ventricular escape focus that has accelerated enough to drive the heart)
High-yield stem clues: AIVR appearing after successful reperfusion (spontaneous or via PCI) of an occluded coronary artery is actually a reassuring "reperfusion arrhythmia," not a dangerous one.
Rarely sustained, does not progress to VFib, and rarely needs treatment. Below 50 bpm, drop the word "accelerated" and just call it idioventricular rhythm.

Bradycardia & AV Blocks

Bradyarrhythmias arise from either a disorder of impulse formation (depressed sinus node activity) or a disorder of conduction (a block somewhere in the system — sinus node, AV node/His bundle, or bundle branches).

1° AV Block

IdentifiersPR interval >0.2 sec; every P wave still conducts to a QRS (a "delay," not a true block)
High-yield stem clues: Common in normal hearts, especially during sleep; can also be an early sign of conduction-system disease, myocarditis, or drug toxicity (e.g., AV nodal blockers, Lyme disease).
Does not require treatment by itself (exception: Lyme carditis, which can progress rapidly).

Mobitz I / Wenckebach (2° AV Block)

IdentifiersProgressive PR lengthening with each beat until one P wave is not followed by a QRS (a "dropped beat"); R-R interval shortens as PR lengthens; usually a block within the AV node itself
High-yield stem clues: Usually transient and benign; rarely progresses to complete heart block and rarely needs a pacemaker unless the patient is symptomatic.

Mobitz II (2° AV Block)

IdentifiersConstant PR interval on conducted beats, then a sudden dropped QRS with no warning lengthening; often a block below the AV node in the His-Purkinje system; may have a bundle-branch-block QRS pattern
High-yield stem clues: "Dropped beat with unchanged PR intervals" = Mobitz II — the dangerous one.
More serious than Mobitz I: signifies underlying structural cardiac disease, can suddenly deteriorate into 3° heart block, and requires a pacemaker.

3° (Complete) AV Block

IdentifiersComplete AV dissociation — P waves and QRS complexes fire independently, each at their own intrinsic rate; ventricular rate slower than the atrial/sinus rate
High-yield stem clues: P waves marching straight through, completely unrelated to the QRS complexes, is the single clearest complete-heart-block clue.
Ventricles fall back on an escape rhythm (~30–45 bpm). Leading cause is degenerative conduction-system disease; can also complicate acute MI. Virtually always requires a pacemaker — a true medical emergency.

3° AV Block — Paced Afterward

IdentifiersRegular pacer spikes restoring AV synchrony after complete heart block is diagnosed

Bundle Branch Blocks

Bundle branch blocks are conduction abnormalities, not rhythm disturbances. QRS 0.09–0.11s = incomplete BBB; ≥0.12s = complete BBB.

RBBB vs. LBBB — Side-by-Side Comparison

IdentifiersCompares V1/V6 morphology for RBBB vs. LBBB against a normal QRS

Right Bundle Branch Block (RBBB)

IdentifiersQRS >0.12s; RSR′ ("rabbit ears") in V1–V2 with R′ larger than the initial R; wide reciprocal S waves in I, aVL, V5–V6; T wave opposite the terminal QRS deflection
Leads to Key In OnV1–V2 for rabbit ears; I, aVL, V5–V6 for reciprocal wide S waves
High-yield stem clues: "Rabbit ears" in V1 is the classic RBBB visual, though sometimes you'll only see a tall R wave rather than a true RSR′.

RBBB with Left Axis Deviation (Bifascicular Block)

IdentifiersRBBB pattern plus LAD from a concurrent left anterior fascicular block (LAFB): qR in I/aVL, rS in II/III/aVF
High-yield stem clues: RBBB + a fascicular block = bifascicular block; watch for progression toward complete heart block.

Left Bundle Branch Block (LBBB)

IdentifiersQRS >0.12s; broad/notched ("monophasic") R wave in I, aVL, V5–V6; deep QS or rS in V1; absent septal Q waves in lateral leads; left axis deviation may be present
Leads to Key In OnI, aVL, V5–V6 for the broad/notched R; V1 for the deep negative deflection
LBBB obscures standard ST-elevation criteria for MI — infarction generally cannot be reliably assessed in its presence.

LBBB Morphology Key (V1 vs. V6)

IdentifiersV1: rS or QS pattern. V6: broad, monophasic R wave

New LBBB with Tachycardia

IdentifiersLBBB morphology superimposed on a tachycardic rate
High-yield stem clues: New LBBB with chest pain/tachycardia should be treated as a possible MI equivalent, since standard ST criteria are unreliable.

Pacemakers

Indications: 3° AV block, sick sinus syndrome, AV/bundle-branch block complicating acute MI (often temporary), and recurrent VT (overdrive pacing).

Pacemaker Spike — General

IdentifiersA sharp vertical spike immediately preceding the paced chamber's depolarization

Atrial Pacing

IdentifiersA pacer spike followed by a P wave, then a normal PR interval and normal QRS (this example: atrial pacing with 1° AV block)

Ventricular Pacing

IdentifiersA pacer spike followed by a wide, bizarre QRS resembling a PVC — the lead sits in the RV, so the RV depolarizes first, generating an LBBB-like pattern

AV Sequential (Dual-Chamber) Pacing

IdentifiersTwo spikes per cycle — one preceding the P wave, one preceding the wide QRS

Ischemia & Infarction

STEMI — "Tombstone" ST Elevation

IdentifiersST segment bowed upward ("frown" shape), merging imperceptibly into the T wave — a monophasic current of injury
High-yield stem clues: ST elevation in ≥1mm in most leads (≥2mm in V2–V3 for men ≥40, ≥2.5mm for men <40, ≥1.5mm for women) across ≥2 contiguous leads meets STEMI criteria.
A true emergency — the myocardium is starved of blood supply and needs emergent revascularization.

STEMI Evolution Over Time

IdentifiersHyperacute T wave → J-point/ST elevation → more pronounced convex ST elevation → ST-T merge into one complex → ST returns to baseline with a new Q wave and loss of R-wave amplitude → T-wave inversion (or eventual normalization)

NSTEMI / Unstable Angina

IdentifiersST-segment depression and/or T-wave inversion — NOT elevation; new horizontal/downsloping ST depression ≥0.5mm in 2 contiguous leads, and/or T inversion >1mm with a prominent R wave or R/S ratio >1
High-yield stem clues: NSTEMI is actually more common than STEMI, involves less than full-thickness myocardium, and cannot be localized to one coronary territory the way a STEMI can.
Lower initial mortality than STEMI but higher risk of further infarction/mortality over time.

Atrial Enlargement & Ventricular Hypertrophy

Assess atrial enlargement in leads II (parallel to atrial current, most sensitive) and V1 (perpendicular, biphasic — separates right vs. left atrial components).

Right Atrial Enlargement (P Pulmonale)

IdentifiersIncreased amplitude of the early (right atrial) portion of the P wave; P-wave duration stays normal; P-wave axis may shift rightward toward/beyond +90°
Causes: tricuspid regurgitation, pulmonary valve stenosis, pulmonary hypertension, cor pulmonale (chronic lung disease) — pressure or volume overload of the right atrium.

Left Atrial Enlargement (P Mitrale)

IdentifiersTerminal (left atrial) portion of the P wave in V1 drops ≥1mm below baseline and is ≥0.04s (1 small box) wide
Causes: mitral stenosis, LV dysfunction, mitral regurgitation, VSD — pressure or volume overload of the left atrium.

Right Ventricular Hypertrophy (RVH)

IdentifiersRight axis deviation; narrow QRS; tall R in V1 (R>S, >7mm or R/S>1); dominant S in V6 (>7mm or R/S<1); RV strain (T inversion/ST depression in V1–V3, II, III, aVF); RBBB or incomplete RBBB may coexist
Most common causes: pulmonary disease and congenital heart disease.

Left Ventricular Hypertrophy (LVH)

IdentifiersSokolow-Lyon voltage criteria: S in V1 + R in V5 or V6 >35mm; supportive non-voltage criteria: increased R-wave peak time >50ms in V5/V6, ST depression + T inversion in left-sided leads (LV strain)
High-yield stem clues: Voltage criteria alone are not diagnostic — you need supportive non-voltage (strain) criteria to call true LVH rather than just "voltage criteria for LVH."
A major noninvasive marker of increased cardiovascular morbidity/mortality risk, including sudden cardiac death.

Metabolic & Other Manifestations

Hypothermia — Osborn (J) Wave

IdentifiersAn abrupt extra deflection right at the J point (Osborn/J wave); sinus bradycardia; prolonged PR, QRS, and QT
High-yield stem clues: ~80% of patients show an Osborn wave as core temperature approaches 86°F. Can be mistaken for acute coronary syndrome given the dramatic ST elevation.
Treatment: warm the patient. Watch for muscle-tremor (shivering) artifact, which can mimic AFib/flutter.

Hyperkalemia — Progressive Changes

IdentifiersMild-moderate: peaked/"tented" T waves. Moderate-severe: P-wave flattening, PR prolongation, QRS widening. Very severe: sine-wave pattern preceding asystole
High-yield stem clues: Recognize the progressive pattern, not just one isolated finding — hyperkalemia can evolve rapidly into lethal conduction and ventricular rhythms.

Hypokalemia

IdentifiersST-segment depression; flattened T wave with a prolonged-appearing QT; prominent U wave
The EKG can be a better marker of serious toxicity than the serum K⁺ level itself. Also potentiates digoxin toxicity.

Prolonged QT / Torsades Risk

IdentifiersQT interval prolonged relative to the heart rate (QTc); risk of degeneration into polymorphic VT/Torsades
High-yield stem clues: Long QT can be congenital (ion channel mutations) or acquired (drugs — antiarrhythmics like sotalol/amiodarone/dofetilide/procainamide/quinidine are the most common culprits; also psychotropics like haloperidol; electrolyte disturbances: hypoK, hypoMg, hypoCa).

Digitalis Effect

IdentifiersGradual, gently downsloping ("scooped") ST-segment depression merging almost imperceptibly from the preceding R wave, with flattened/inverted T waves
High-yield stem clues: The gradual scooped downslope helps distinguish the digitalis effect from the more symmetric ST depression of ischemia — though this can be harder to separate from LVH-with-strain, especially since digoxin is sometimes used in HFrEF patients who also have LVH.
The digitalis "effect" (a therapeutic-level EKG signature) is not the same as digitalis toxicity — toxicity requires correlating level, potassium, symptoms, and rhythm (can include junctional rhythms, PVCs, AV block).

Low QRS Voltage & Electrical Alternans

IdentifiersLow QRS voltage + tachycardia + electrical alternans (consecutive, normally-conducted QRS complexes alternating in height) — the classic triad of massive pericardial effusion
High-yield stem clues: Electrical alternans occurs because the heart swings back and forth within a large fluid-filled pericardial sac.
