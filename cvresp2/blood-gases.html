Ventilation, Diffusion & Oxygenation

1. Mechanisms of hypoxemia

Hypoxemia is a low level of oxygen in the blood. There are six mechanisms that can produce it:

MechanismA-a gradient
Ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) mismatchElevated
Right-to-left shuntingElevated
Decreased alveolar ventilation (hypoventilation)Normal
Decreased diffusionElevated
High altitude (low FiO2)Normal
Low mixed venous O2 (PvO2)Elevated
Clinical pearlOnly hypoventilation and high altitude give a normal A-a gradient — in both, oxygen never gets to a normal level in the alveolus to begin with. Every other mechanism widens the gradient because oxygen reaches the alveolus fine but fails to equilibrate into the capillary blood.

Check yourself

Exam-style question
A patient has hypoxemia with a normal A-a gradient. Which mechanism of hypoxemia is most consistent with this finding?
AVentilation/perfusion mismatch
BRight-to-left shunt
CDecreased alveolar ventilation (hypoventilation)
DDecreased diffusion capacity
ELow mixed venous PO2
Explanation: A normal A-a gradient in a hypoxemic patient means oxygen simply is not reaching the alveoli in adequate amounts — the alveoli-to-artery transfer step itself is intact. Only hypoventilation and high altitude (low FiO2) produce hypoxemia with a normal A-a gradient; every other mechanism (V/Q mismatch, shunt, diffusion defect, low PvO2) widens the gradient because oxygen reaches the alveoli fine but fails to equilibrate into arterial blood.
Exam-style question
Which of the following is one of the six recognized mechanisms of hypoxemia?
ALeft-to-right shunting
BIncreased diffusion capacity
CElevated mixed venous oxygen
DHyperventilation
ERight-to-left shunting
Explanation: The six mechanisms of hypoxemia are: ventilation/perfusion mismatch, right-to-left shunting, decreased alveolar ventilation, decreased diffusion, high altitude, and low mixed venous O2 (PvO2). A left-to-right shunt does not bypass oxygenated blood past the lungs and does not cause hypoxemia; increased diffusion and elevated PvO2 would raise, not lower, arterial oxygen.
Exam-style question
A patient living at 8,000 feet of altitude has hypoxemia. What is the expected A-a gradient, and why?
AElevated, because diffusion is impaired at altitude
BNormal, because the defect is a low inspired FiO2 rather than a problem getting oxygen from alveolus to capillary
CElevated, because of obligate V/Q mismatch at altitude
DNormal, because hemoglobin affinity for oxygen increases at altitude
EElevated, because minute ventilation falls at altitude
Explanation: High altitude causes hypoxemia purely because atmospheric (and therefore alveolar) oxygen tension is low — the alveolar-to-arterial oxygen transfer mechanism itself is completely normal, so the A-a gradient stays normal. This is the same reason hypoventilation gives a normal gradient: in both cases oxygen never reaches a normal alveolar level in the first place, rather than failing to cross into the blood once it gets there.
Exam-style question
In pneumonia, hypoxemia occurs primarily through which mechanism, and what happens to the A-a gradient?
ADecreased alveolar ventilation; A-a gradient is normal
BHigh-altitude physiology; A-a gradient is normal
CRight-to-left cardiac shunt; A-a gradient is normal
DDecreased diffusion/V-Q mismatch from alveolar filling; A-a gradient is elevated
ELow mixed venous oxygen; A-a gradient is normal
Explanation: Pneumonia fills alveoli with inflammatory exudate, impairing both ventilation to and diffusion across the affected alveoli (functional shunt/V-Q mismatch). Oxygen reaches some alveoli but fails to equilibrate normally into the pulmonary capillary blood, so the A-a gradient widens — exactly what was seen in Case #3, where the calculated gradient was over 500 mmHg.

3. The alveolar-arterial (A-a) gradient

The A-a gradient is the difference between the partial pressure of O2 in the alveoli (PAO2) and in the artery (PaO2). It assesses diffusion from the alveoli to the capillaries.

Clinical pearlBlood gases are used to assess three things at once: ventilation (PaCO2), diffusion (A-a gradient), and oxygen delivery (DO2). Keeping these three questions separate — "is the patient ventilating adequately?", "is oxygen crossing into the blood normally?", and "is enough oxygen actually reaching the tissues?" — is the backbone of every ABG interpretation in this guide.

Alveolar gas equation

TermValue / meaning
PAO2 = (Patm - Pwater) × FiO2 - PaCO2/RQThe alveolar gas equation
Pwater47 mmHg at 37°C (constant)
PatmDepends on altitude/temperature — 760 mmHg at sea level; ~676 mmHg at 3,100 ft (Billings/Rexburg); ~623 mmHg at 5,280 ft (Denver)
RQ (respiratory quotient)Ratio of CO2 produced to O2 consumed; 1.0 for carbohydrate metabolism, 0.7 for pure lipid metabolism; 0.8 used as an average
Worked example, sea level, room airPAO2 = (760 - 47) × 0.21 - 40/0.8 = 149.7 - 50 = 100 mmHg

Normal values & simplified bedside formula

QuantityValue
Normal A-a gradient (young adult)5-7 mmHg
Age-adjusted expected gradient(Age + 10) / 4
Practical upper limit of normal (any age)< 30 mmHg
Simplified bedside A-a gradient formulaA-a gradient = 7 × FiO2(%) - 1.25 × PaCO2 - PaO2
(comes from approximating (Patm - Pwater) ≈ 700 and 1/RQ = 1/0.8 = 1.25)

Interpreting a hypoxic patient's A-a gradient

FindingInterpretation
Hypoxic + normal A-a gradientOxygen is not getting to the alveoli (ventilation problem or low FiO2)
Hypoxic + high A-a gradientOxygen has reached the alveoli but isn't getting into the bloodstream (diffusion/V-Q/shunt problem)

Check yourself

Exam-style question
Using the alveolar gas equation, what is the calculated PAO2 for a patient breathing room air at sea level with a normal PaCO2 of 40 mmHg (assume respiratory quotient of 0.8)?
A40 mmHg
B100 mmHg
C70 mmHg
D150 mmHg
E300 mmHg
Explanation: PAO2 = (Patm - Pwater) x FiO2 - PaCO2/RQ = (760 - 47) x 0.21 - 40/0.8 = 149.7 - 50 = 100 mmHg. This is the classic "normal alveolar oxygen tension" value used as a reference point for calculating the A-a gradient at sea level.
Exam-style question
A 70-year-old patient's PaO2 is 82 mmHg on room air with a normal PaCO2. Using the age-based formula for expected A-a gradient [(age + 10) / 4], is this patient's oxygenation appropriate for age?
ANo, this represents severe hypoxemia for any age
BNo, the A-a gradient must always be under 10 mmHg regardless of age
CYes, but only because the patient is on supplemental oxygen
DYes — expected A-a gradient is about 20 mmHg for this age, and a PAO2 of ~100 minus a PaO2 of 82 gives a gradient of about 18 mmHg, which is appropriate
EThis cannot be determined without knowing the FiO2
Explanation: The age-adjusted expected A-a gradient is (age + 10) / 4 = (70 + 10) / 4 = 20 mmHg. A calculated gradient of about 18 mmHg (100 - 82) falls within that expected range, so this reflects normal age-related widening rather than a pathologic diffusion problem. A gradient under 30 mmHg is generally considered the outer limit of normal regardless of this age formula.
Exam-style question
Which simplified bedside formula approximates the A-a gradient without requiring the full alveolar gas equation?
AA-a gradient = 7 x FiO2 (in %) - 1.25 x PaCO2 - PaO2
BA-a gradient = PaO2 - PaCO2
CA-a gradient = FiO2 x PaCO2 - PaO2
DA-a gradient = (Patm - Pwater) - PaO2
EA-a gradient = 1.25 x FiO2 - 7 x PaCO2
Explanation: Because (Patm - Pwater) is approximately 700 at sea level and 1/RQ (using RQ = 0.8) is 1.25, the alveolar gas equation simplifies to PAO2 ~ 7 x FiO2(%) - 1.25 x PaCO2, and therefore A-a gradient ~ 7 x FiO2(%) - 1.25 x PaCO2 - PaO2. This is the shortcut used throughout the worked cases in this lecture.
Exam-style question
A hypoxic patient's calculated A-a gradient is elevated at 250 mmHg. What does this tell you about the site of the oxygenation problem?
AOxygen is not reaching the alveoli at all
BThe patient has a purely ventilatory problem with no gas-exchange abnormality
COxygen has reached the alveoli but is not getting into the bloodstream (a diffusion/V-Q/shunt problem)
DThe problem is at high altitude
EThe FiO2 must be miscalibrated
Explanation: An elevated A-a gradient in a hypoxic patient localizes the problem to the alveolar-capillary interface: oxygen reaches the alveoli (so the gradient calculation is even possible) but fails to equilibrate normally into arterial blood, implicating V/Q mismatch, shunt, or a diffusion abnormality — as opposed to a normal gradient, which would point to inadequate ventilation or low inspired oxygen.
Exam-style question
What is the normal A-a gradient at sea level in a young healthy adult, and what upper limit is generally used to call a gradient abnormal regardless of age?
A0-2 mmHg; abnormal above 10 mmHg
B20-30 mmHg; abnormal above 50 mmHg
C40 mmHg; abnormal above 60 mmHg
D100 mmHg; abnormal above 150 mmHg
E5-7 mmHg; abnormal above 30 mmHg
Explanation: The normal A-a gradient is 5-7 mmHg in a healthy young adult, rising gradually with age per the (age + 10)/4 formula. A gradient under 30 mmHg is generally considered the practical upper limit of normal across ages.

4. Oxygen delivery to tissues

Oxygen transport to tissues depends on (1) the amount of O2 transported to the tissues, and (2) how much O2 is taken up and utilized by the mitochondria/cells.

FormulaMeaning
DO2 = Cardiac output × (1.34 × Hgb × SaO2)Delivery of oxygen (DO2) — hemoglobin concentration, oxygen saturation, and cardiac output are all of key importance
Clinical pearlA normal PaO2 and SaO2 do not guarantee adequate tissue oxygen delivery — a patient in cardiogenic shock (low cardiac output) or with severe anemia (low hemoglobin) can be well-oxygenated on paper yet critically under-delivering oxygen to their tissues.

Check yourself

Exam-style question
Which formula correctly describes oxygen delivery (DO2) to the tissues?
ADO2 = Respiratory rate x Tidal volume
BDO2 = Cardiac output x (1.34 x Hemoglobin x SaO2)
CDO2 = PAO2 - PaO2
DDO2 = FiO2 x PaCO2
EDO2 = Hemoglobin / SaO2
Explanation: Oxygen delivery is DO2 = Cardiac output x (1.34 x Hemoglobin x SaO2), reflecting the three key determinants of tissue oxygen delivery: how much blood is pumped (cardiac output), how much oxygen each gram of hemoglobin carries (the 1.34 mL O2/g constant), and how saturated that hemoglobin is (SaO2).
Exam-style question
A patient's oxygen delivery to tissues is inadequate despite a normal PaO2 and SaO2. Which additional variable, if abnormal, could explain this?
AFiO2
BA-a gradient
CRespiratory quotient
DCardiac output or hemoglobin concentration
EAtmospheric pressure
Explanation: DO2 = Cardiac output x (1.34 x Hgb x SaO2). Even with normal oxygen saturation and PaO2, a low cardiac output (e.g., cardiogenic shock) or low hemoglobin (anemia) will reduce the total amount of oxygen actually delivered to the tissues, since delivery depends on all three variables together, not oxygen saturation alone.

Acid-Base Disorders

5. Acid-base classification & terminology

Term pairDistinction
Acidemia vs. acidosisAcidemia = decreased blood pH (normal 7.36-7.44); acidosis = the clinical process that decreases blood pH
Alkalemia vs. alkalosisAlkalemia = increased blood pH; alkalosis = the clinical process that increases blood pH
Hypercapnia vs. hypocapniaRefers to increased or decreased PaCO2 in the blood
VentilationThe process by which inhaled air (O2) reaches the alveoli and exhaled air (CO2) leaves the lungs
Minute ventilationRate air reaches the alveoli (L/min) = Respiratory rate × Tidal volume
Hyperventilation vs. hypoventilationIncreased or decreased minute ventilation, respectively — hyperventilation → hypocapnia; hypoventilation → hypercapnia

The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation

pH = 6.1 + log ( HCO3- / (0.03 × PaCO2) )

DisorderPattern
Respiratory acidosisHypoventilation → hypercapnia (↑PaCO2 in the denominator) → ↓pH
Respiratory alkalosisHyperventilation → hypocapnia (↓PaCO2) → ↑pH
Metabolic acidosisOver-production/accumulation of acid, loss of base, or under-excretion of acid → ↓HCO3- (numerator) → ↓pH; HCO3- and pH move in the same direction
Metabolic alkalosisLoss of acid or under-excretion of base → ↑HCO3- → ↑pH; HCO3- and pH move in the same direction

Check yourself

Exam-style question
What is the distinction between "acidemia" and "acidosis"?
AAcidemia is a decreased blood pH; acidosis is the clinical process in the body that decreases blood pH
BThey are interchangeable terms for the same thing
CAcidemia refers to increased PCO2; acidosis refers to decreased bicarbonate
DAcidemia is a compensatory response; acidosis is the primary disturbance
EAcidemia only occurs in respiratory disorders; acidosis only occurs in metabolic disorders
Explanation: Acidemia describes the resulting blood pH itself (below the normal 7.36-7.44 range), while acidosis describes the underlying clinical process producing that change. A patient can have an active acidotic process without frank acidemia if a compensatory alkalotic process brings the pH back toward normal (a mixed disorder).
Exam-style question
Using the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation [pH = 6.1 + log(HCO3-/(0.03 x PaCO2))], which pattern is characteristic of primary respiratory acidosis?
AHyperventilation, hypocapnia, and increased pH
BIncreased HCO3- with a proportional increase in pH and no change in PaCO2
CHypoventilation, hypercapnia, and decreased pH
DDecreased HCO3- with a proportional decrease in pH and no change in PaCO2
ENormal PaCO2 with decreased pH
Explanation: Respiratory acidosis arises from hypoventilation, which raises PaCO2 (hypercapnia). Because PaCO2 sits in the denominator of the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation, a rising PaCO2 drives the pH down — producing the classic hypoventilation -> hypercapnia -> acidosis chain.
Exam-style question
What is minute ventilation, and how is it calculated?
AThe volume of one breath, calculated as tidal volume alone
BThe rate of CO2 production, calculated as respiratory quotient x cardiac output
CThe alveolar oxygen tension, calculated from the alveolar gas equation
DThe rate of oxygen delivery, calculated as cardiac output x hemoglobin
EThe rate at which air reaches the alveoli, calculated as respiratory rate x tidal volume
Explanation: Minute ventilation is measured in liters per minute and equals respiratory rate (breaths/min) multiplied by tidal volume (volume of air per breath). Hyperventilation and hypoventilation refer to increased or decreased minute ventilation, respectively — not simply to a fast or slow respiratory rate alone.
Exam-style question
In primary metabolic acidosis, which of the following describes the underlying process(es) that can produce it?
AIncreased minute ventilation only
BOver-production or accumulation of acid, loss of base (HCO3-), or under-excretion of acid, resulting in decreased HCO3- and decreased pH
CLoss of acid or under-excretion of base, resulting in increased HCO3- and increased pH
DDecreased PaCO2 with no change in HCO3-
EIncreased PaCO2 with a compensatory decrease in HCO3-
Explanation: Metabolic acidosis results from an over-production or accumulation of acid, a loss of base (bicarbonate), or under-excretion of acid — all of which lower HCO3- and, in turn, lower pH. (The mirror-image process — loss of acid or under-excretion of base, raising both HCO3- and pH — describes metabolic alkalosis instead.)

Coma and CNS-depressant drugs act by blunting central respiratory drive rather than by mechanically limiting tidal volume.

7. Compensation in acid-base disorders — the Boston method

Three tiers of compensation, from fastest to slowest:

MechanismTimeframe
Buffering (bicarbonate, carbonic acid, proteins, phosphates)Minutes
Respiratory compensation (for metabolic disorders)Hours
Metabolic (renal) compensation (for respiratory disorders)2-3 days

Boston method formulas

DisorderRuleExpected HCO3- / PaCO2
Acute respiratory acidosisHCO3- rises 1 mmol/L per 10 mmHg rise in PaCO2Expected HCO3- = 24 + ((PaCO2-40)/10)
Chronic respiratory acidosisHCO3- rises 4 mmol/L per 10 mmHg rise in PaCO2Expected HCO3- = 24 + (4 × (PaCO2-40)/10)
Acute respiratory alkalosisHCO3- falls 2 mmol/L per 10 mmHg fall in PaCO2Expected HCO3- = 24 - (2 × (40-PaCO2)/10)
Chronic respiratory alkalosisHCO3- falls 5 mmol/L per 10 mmHg fall in PaCO2Expected HCO3- = 24 - (5 × (40-PaCO2)/10)
Metabolic acidosis (Winter's formula)Complete respiratory compensationPaCO2 = 1.5 × HCO3- + 8
Metabolic alkalosis ("0.7 + 20" rule)Complete respiratory compensationPaCO2 = 0.7 × HCO3- + 20
Clinical pearlRemember: the body never over-compensates. If the measured value is more compensated than the formula predicts, suspect a second, independent acid-base process rather than "extra-good" compensation.

Check yourself

Exam-style question
A patient has a chronically elevated PaCO2 of 70 mmHg from long-standing COPD. Using the Boston method for chronic respiratory acidosis (HCO3- rises 4 mmol/L for every 10 mmHg rise in PaCO2 above 40), what is the expected compensatory HCO3-?
A24 mmol/L (no compensation expected)
B36 mmol/L
C28 mmol/L
D42 mmol/L
E48 mmol/L
Explanation: Expected HCO3- = 24 + (4 x (PaCO2 - 40)/10) = 24 + (4 x 30/10) = 24 + 12 = 36 mmol/L. This reflects the slower, more complete renal compensation for a PaCO2 that has been elevated long enough (days) for the kidneys to retain additional bicarbonate.
Exam-style question
A patient with metabolic acidosis has a measured HCO3- of 12 mmol/L. Using Winter's formula, what PaCO2 would represent complete respiratory compensation?
APaCO2 = 12 mmHg
BPaCO2 = 20 mmHg
CPaCO2 = 32 mmHg
DPaCO2 = 26 mmHg
EPaCO2 = 40 mmHg
Explanation: Winter's formula: expected PaCO2 = (1.5 x HCO3-) + 8 = (1.5 x 12) + 8 = 18 + 8 = 26 mmHg. If the measured PaCO2 differs substantially from this predicted value, a second, independent respiratory process (acidosis or alkalosis) must also be present.
Exam-style question
In metabolic alkalosis, the "0.7 + 20" rule predicts the expected compensatory PaCO2. For a patient with an HCO3- of 34 mmol/L, what PaCO2 would represent appropriate respiratory compensation?
APaCO2 = 44.8 mmHg
BPaCO2 = 34 mmHg
CPaCO2 = 40 mmHg
DPaCO2 = 54 mmHg
EPaCO2 = 60 mmHg
Explanation: Expected PaCO2 = (0.7 x HCO3-) + 20 = (0.7 x 34) + 20 = 23.8 + 20 = 44.8 mmHg. Since the lungs cannot hypoventilate indefinitely (hypoxia eventually limits further CO2 retention), the body never fully normalizes pH through respiratory compensation for a metabolic alkalosis — it approaches but doesn't reach normal.
Exam-style question
Which general timeframes are correct for the three levels of acid-base compensation, from fastest to slowest?
ARenal compensation (seconds) -> buffering (minutes) -> respiratory compensation (days)
BRespiratory compensation (seconds) -> renal compensation (minutes) -> buffering (hours)
CBuffering (minutes) -> respiratory compensation (hours) -> renal/metabolic compensation (2-3 days)
DRenal compensation (minutes) -> buffering (hours) -> respiratory compensation (days)
EAll three occur simultaneously within seconds
Explanation: Chemical buffering (bicarbonate, proteins, phosphates) acts within minutes; respiratory compensation for metabolic disorders occurs over hours as ventilation adjusts; and renal (metabolic) compensation for respiratory disorders is the slowest, taking 2-3 days as the kidneys adjust bicarbonate handling.

8. Interpreting the ABG — clinical stepwise approach

The ABG is used predominantly for assessing the etiology of dyspnea/hypoxia, assessing severity of pulmonary disease, diagnosing respiratory failure, managing patients on ventilatory support, and diagnosing acid-base disorders.

Basic rules

  • Determine the predominant process by assessing blood pH
  • Differentiate between primary and secondary (compensatory) acid-base disturbances
  • The body never over-compensates
  • Know the main normal numbers: pH 7.40, PaCO2 40 mmHg, HCO3- 24 meq/L

Six-step approach

StepAction
1Look at pH: acidemia (<7.4) vs. alkalemia (>7.4)
2Look at HCO3- and PaCO2 to determine whether the primary process is metabolic or respiratory
3Determine if the process is uncompensated (acute), compensated (chronic), or partially compensated
4Calculate the anion gap to classify the metabolic acidosis, or to detect a mixed disorder; if a gap exists, calculate the delta ratio
5If a metabolic disorder is present, check PaCO2 for an additional respiratory process
6Refer back to the clinical picture — do the calculations make clinical sense?

Base excess

Base excess is an indicator of the metabolic component of an acid-base disorder — the amount of acid required to normalize pH at a PaCO2 of 40 mmHg. It is high in metabolic alkalosis and low/negative in metabolic acidosis.

Anion gap and delta ratio

CalculationFormula
Anion gapNa+ - Cl- - HCO3- (normal ≈ 12)
Delta ratio(measured AG - 12) / (24 - measured HCO3-)
Delta ratioInterpretation
< 1Mixed high anion gap + normal anion gap acidosis coexist
1 - 2Pure/uncomplicated high anion gap metabolic acidosis
> 2High anion gap acidosis + a concurrent metabolic alkalosis (bicarbonate barely shifted from normal despite the anion gap change)

Check yourself

Exam-style question
What does a delta ratio between 1 and 2 suggest when working up a high anion gap metabolic acidosis?
AA mixed high and normal anion gap acidosis coexist
BA concurrent metabolic alkalosis is also present
CThe anion gap calculation is invalid
DA concurrent respiratory alkalosis is present
EA pure high anion gap metabolic acidosis, without an additional metabolic process
Explanation: Delta ratio = (measured anion gap - 12) / (24 - measured bicarbonate). A ratio between 1 and 2 indicates that the fall in bicarbonate matches the rise in anion gap reasonably well — consistent with a straightforward, uncomplicated high anion gap metabolic acidosis. A ratio under 1 suggests a mixed high/normal anion gap acidosis, while a ratio over 2 suggests a concurrent metabolic alkalosis (bicarbonate has fallen less than expected).
Exam-style question
What does base excess measure, and what does a low (or negative) base excess indicate?
AThe amount of CO2 retained in the blood; low values indicate respiratory alkalosis
BThe amount of acid required to normalize pH at a PaCO2 of 40 mmHg; a low or negative value indicates metabolic acidosis
CThe difference between measured and calculated sodium; low values indicate hyponatremia
DThe oxygen saturation deficit; low values indicate hypoxia
EThe anion gap; low values indicate a normal anion gap acidosis
Explanation: Base excess is an indicator of the metabolic component of an acid-base disorder — specifically, the amount of acid that would need to be added to normalize the pH at a PaCO2 of 40 mmHg. It is high in metabolic alkalosis and low or negative in metabolic acidosis.

Case 1 — sudden dyspnea after hip replacement

Vignette74-year-old female develops SOB 4 hours after hip replacement surgery. PMH: MI 7 years ago, hyperlipidemia, hypertension. VS: T 100°F, BP 130/75, HR 110, pulse ox 75%. PE: alert but sedated/confused; tachycardic, regular, no murmurs; decreased breath sounds; diminished bowel sounds; mild lower extremity edema.

Differential (common problems after hip replacement): pulmonary embolus, aspiration pneumonia, CHF (fluid overload), over-sedation from pain medications.

ABG/ChemValue (normal range)
pH7.48 (7.35-7.45)
PaO295 (70-100)
O2 Sat94% (90-100)
PaCO232 (35-45)
HCO3-22 (22-26)
FiO260% (initially missing from the report — a crucial omission, since the A-a gradient cannot be calculated without it, and pulse oximetry and ABG saturation values can diverge without it for context)
Na / Cl138 / 106

Step 1 — Ventilation

PaCO2 32 → hyperventilation. pH is up → acute hyperventilation. This rules out over-sedation from pain medications (which would cause hypoventilation, not hyperventilation).

Step 2 — Diffusion

A-a gradient = 7 × 60% - 1.25 × 32 - 95 ≈ 285 mmHg (normal is up to 30) → a significant problem with diffusion. Pulmonary embolus, aspiration pneumonia, and CHF can all elevate the A-a gradient (over-sedation was already excluded).

A normal CXR rules out pneumonia and CHF, leaving pulmonary embolism as the most likely diagnosis — pursue CT angiogram.

Step 3 — Acid-base

pH up → alkalosis. HCO3- moves opposite to pH → respiratory alkalosis, confirmed by the low PaCO2. Compensation: acute respiratory alkalosis drops HCO3- by 2 for every 10 drop in PaCO2 (chronic: 5 per 10); here HCO3- is down 2 and PaCO2 down 8 — closer to acute. Anion gap = 138 - 106 - 22 = 10 (normal) → no metabolic acidosis, no delta ratio needed.

Conclusion: acute respiratory alkalosis from hypoxia-driven hyperventilation, due to pulmonary embolism → hypoxic respiratory failure. Treatment: anticoagulation and supplemental oxygen.

Case 4 — oxygen-dependent COPD, now intubated

Vignette65-year-old male with a history of oxygen-dependent COPD presents with sudden increase in shortness of breath and chest pain. In the ED, O2 sat is 72% on 100% FiO2; he is lethargic with SBP 85 mmHg. He is intubated on arrival.
ABG/ChemValue (normal range)
pH7.55 (7.35-7.45)
PaCO246 (35-45)
HCO3-36 (22-26)
PaO2160 (70-100)
O2 Sat100% on 100% FiO2 with ventilator
Na / Cl143 / 101

Step 1 — Ventilation

PaCO2 mildly high → hypoventilation, but pH is high — the opposite direction expected for acute hypoventilation. This mismatch signals that the hypoventilation is probably chronic.

Step 2 — Diffusion

A-a gradient = 7 × 100% - 1.25 × 46 - 160 = 700 - 57.5 - 160 = 482.5 mmHg → significant diffusion problem → combined hypoxic and hypercapnic respiratory failure.

Step 3 — Acid-base (first pass)

pH up → alkalosis; HCO3- moves in the same direction as pH → appears metabolic; pH up and uncompensated. Anion gap = 6 (no gap, no delta ratio needed). First-pass conclusion: uncompensated metabolic alkalosis.

Clinical pearlThis first-pass read doesn't fit the clinical picture: the patient presented with a respiratory problem in a patient with known oxygen-dependent COPD, yet the read calls it metabolic — and remember, the body never over-compensates, so an "uncompensated" disorder in someone who has had chronic lung disease for years should raise suspicion.

Reconsidering the clinical context

His known oxygen-dependent COPD means his baseline state is chronic hypoventilation with chronic respiratory acidosis and a secondary (compensatory) metabolic alkalosis — at baseline his PaCO2 and HCO3- are both markedly elevated (e.g., PaCO2 ~60, HCO3- ~40) with a near-normal pH. Now that he is intubated and ventilated better than his own usual breathing, his PaCO2 has acutely fallen relative to that chronically elevated baseline — producing an acute respiratory alkalosis on top of his chronic respiratory acidosis.

Corrected interpretation

1. pH up → alkalosis. 2. HCO3- moves opposite to the acute change in pH once baseline is considered → respiratory. 3. pH up → uncompensated (again, the body cannot over-compensate — the ventilator abruptly changed his PaCO2 faster than his kidneys can re-equilibrate). 4-6. Anion gap remains 6 (no gap); no further metabolic steps needed.

Conclusion: uncompensated respiratory alkalosis, superimposed on a background of chronic respiratory acidosis — not a primary metabolic alkalosis at all.
