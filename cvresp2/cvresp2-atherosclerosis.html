1. Definition & Overview

  • Atherosclerosis = plaque buildup (fatty deposits) in arteries — a form of arteriosclerosis ("hardening of the arteries").
  • Deposits are composed of cholesterol, fatty substances, cellular waste products, calcium, and fibrin/clotting materials.
  • As plaque builds up, vessel walls thicken, narrowing the arterial channel and reducing blood flow → ↓ oxygen and nutrient delivery to tissues.
  • May partially or totally block large or medium arteries anywhere in the body — heart, brain, pelvis, legs, arms, or kidneys.
Big-picture theme: The same underlying disease process (endothelial injury → lipid deposition → inflammation → plaque → thrombosis) produces different clinical syndromes purely based on which arterial bed is affected — stroke/TIA (carotid/cerebral), ACS/angina (coronary), aneurysm/dissection (aorta), mesenteric ischemia (SMA/IMA/celiac), renovascular hypertension (renal), and claudication/limb ischemia (peripheral).

Practice Questions

What best describes the composition of atherosclerotic plaque?

Atherosclerotic plaque is a mixture of cholesterol, fatty substances, cellular waste products, calcium, and fibrin/clotting materials — not a single substance.

Atherosclerosis is best described as a subtype of which broader process?

Atherosclerosis is a specific form of arteriosclerosis (a general term meaning hardening of the arteries), characterized by lipid-laden plaque formation.

2. Risk Factors & Inflammatory Mechanisms

Traditional Risk Factors

  • Smoking, dyslipidemia, hypertension, glucose intolerance/dysglycemia.

Emerging Risk Factors

  • Visceral/ectopic adipose tissue, periodontitis, local inflammatory conditions (e.g., rheumatologic disease), infection, and the body's microbiome.

Mechanism

  • Risk factors elicit overexpression of proinflammatory cytokines (IL-1, TNF, IL-18, CD40L, MCP-1).
  • This triggers a "messenger" cytokine, IL-6, which stimulates the liver to produce acute phase reactants: fibrinogen (precursor of clots) and PAI-1 (plasminogen activator inhibitor-1, blocks endogenous fibrinolysis) — both promote thrombus accumulation.
  • CRP (often measured as high-sensitivity CRP) and serum amyloid A (SAA) are acute phase reactants that serve as clinical biomarkers of inflammation.

Protective Lifestyle Factors

  • Physical activity, diet rich in fruits/vegetables/nuts, oily fish (omega-3 fatty acids), moderate alcohol intake.

Practice Questions

Which two acute phase reactants directly promote thrombus accumulation when overproduced?

Fibrinogen (the clot precursor) and PAI-1 (which blocks fibrinolysis) are the acute phase reactants that directly favor thrombus accumulation, while CRP and SAA are biomarkers of the inflammatory response rather than direct clot promoters.

Which cytokine acts as the key "messenger" linking peripheral inflammation to the hepatic acute phase response in atherosclerosis?

IL-6 is the "messenger" cytokine that travels to the liver and stimulates production of acute phase reactants like fibrinogen, PAI-1, CRP, and SAA.

3. Plaque Pathophysiology — Erosion vs. Rupture & Determinants of Thrombosis

Thrombosis Due to Erosion ("White" Thrombus)

  • Fibrous cap is thick and intact.
  • Collagen trigger; platelet-rich "white" thrombus.
  • Smooth muscle cells prominent; often sessile, nonocclusive; usually less remodeled outward; neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) involved.
  • More frequently associated with non-STEMI.

Thrombosis Due to Rupture ("Red" Thrombus)

  • Thin fibrous cap with a fissure.
  • Tissue factor trigger (released by plaque macrophages); fibrin-rich "red" thrombus with a lipid core.
  • Macrophages prominent; often occlusive; usually expansively remodeled.
  • More frequently causes STEMI.

Determinants of Thrombosis

  • Solid-state (plaque) determinants: tissue factor from macrophages, endothelial cell balance of pro-thrombotic vs. fibrinolytic properties (thrombomodulin, t-PA, u-PA, PAI-1).
  • Fluid-phase (blood) determinants: circulating fibrinogen, PAI-1, and circulating tissue factor microparticles — high levels favor thrombus accumulation.
Pearl: Because atherosclerosis affects the entire arterial tree, but risk factors like hypertension, smoking, and hyperlipidemia are systemic, plaque tends to develop preferentially at sites of disturbed flow or interrupted laminar shear stress — classically at branch points and bifurcations.

Practice Questions

A patient presents with a STEMI. Which plaque disruption mechanism is more likely responsible?

Rupture of a thin fibrous cap exposes tissue factor from macrophages, triggering a fibrin-rich "red" occlusive thrombus — the mechanism more frequently associated with STEMI. Erosion (intact cap, platelet-rich "white" thrombus) is more associated with non-STEMI.

Why does atherosclerotic plaque preferentially develop at arterial branch points despite risk factors being systemic?

Even though risk factors act systemically, regions with disturbed flow or loss of normal laminar shear stress — classically branch points — are selectively susceptible to atheroma formation.

4. Definitions — Embolus vs. Thrombosis

TermDefinition
EmbolusBlood clot, air bubble, fat, or atherosclerotic debris that breaks away and is carried distally to lodge in a blood vessel. Most arterial emboli come from the heart — atrial fibrillation is the most common cause. Other causes: cardiac mural thrombus, diseased valves, thoracic aortic debris/thrombus.
ThrombosisBlood clot forming in a blood vessel. In atherosclerotic disease, the thrombus may form at the site of a critical stenosis.

Practice Questions

What is the most common cause of arterial emboli originating from the heart?

Atrial fibrillation, with loss of coordinated atrial contraction and stasis (especially in the left atrial appendage), is the most common cardiac source of arterial emboli.

How does a thrombosis differ conceptually from an embolus?

Thrombosis is clot formation in situ (often at a critical stenosis), whereas an embolus is material — clot, air, fat, or atherosclerotic debris — that breaks free and travels distally to lodge in another vessel.

5. Cerebrovascular Disease

Ischemic vs. Embolic

IschemicEmbolic
MechanismThrombotic, embolic, or hypoperfusion-relatedCardiac source (A-fib, valve replacement, recent MI with mural thrombus)
Risk factorsHypertension, diabetes, coronary atherosclerotic diseaseSame cardiac risk factors as above
Clinical clueTransient neurologic deficits in the same vascular distribution suggest underlying vascular disease (thrombotic stroke)Transient deficits involving different vascular distributions suggest embolism

Anatomy

  • Carotid arteries supply ~80% of cerebral blood flow; vertebral arteries (from the subclavian arteries) supply the remaining ~20%, joining to form the basilar artery and Circle of Willis.

Pathophysiology & Classification of Stenosis

  • Plaques typically extend into the bifurcation; shear forces make plaques unstable, throwing atherosclerotic emboli.
  • Mild stenosis: <50% luminal narrowing. Moderate: 50–69%. Severe: 70–99%.

Presentation

  • Asymptomatic carotid bruit — heard on auscultation, no neurologic symptoms.
  • TIA: sudden focal neurologic deficit resolving completely within 24 hours; typically contralateral sensory/motor deficits or ipsilateral amaurosis fugax.
  • Stroke: acute neurologic deficit lasting >24 hours.

Diagnosis

  • Duplex ultrasonography — combines ultrasound + Doppler; used for screening/follow-up.
  • MRA — noninvasive; useful when iodinated contrast is contraindicated (AKI risk).
  • CTA — 3D images, comparable to conventional angiography, but requires iodinated contrast.

Treatment

  • Medical management for all: antithrombotic therapy (aspirin), statins for lipid control, BP/diabetes management, smoking cessation, increased physical activity. "Time is brain."
  • Mild stenosis (<50%): medical management alone — no benefit from revascularization.
  • Moderate-severe stenosis (≥50%): revascularize in addition to medical management — carotid endarterectomy or carotid stenting.

Practice Questions

A patient has recurrent transient neurologic deficits that always affect the same vascular distribution. What does this pattern suggest?

Deficits recurring in the same vascular territory point toward a fixed, underlying stenotic lesion (thrombotic mechanism), whereas deficits in varying distributions suggest embolism from a proximal source like the heart.

A patient has an asymptomatic carotid stenosis of 40% found incidentally. What is the most appropriate management?

Mild stenosis (<50%) does not benefit from revascularization procedures; medical management with antithrombotic therapy, statins, and risk factor control is appropriate. Revascularization is reserved for moderate-severe (≥50%) stenosis.

Which imaging modality is preferred for carotid disease evaluation when iodinated contrast is contraindicated due to renal impairment?

MRA is noninvasive and avoids iodinated contrast, making it preferable in patients at risk for contrast-induced acute kidney injury, whereas CTA requires iodinated contrast.

6. Coronary Atherosclerosis

Vascular Endothelium — Normal Functions

  • Local control of vascular tone, maintenance of an antithrombotic surface, control of inflammatory cell adhesion.

Risk Factors

  • High LDL, cigarette smoking, hypertension, diabetes mellitus — all damage endothelial function, leading to inappropriate vasoconstriction, luminal thrombus formation, and abnormal monocyte-platelet interactions.

Distribution

  • Develops at irregular rates across coronary segments; predilection for sites of increased turbulence, such as branch points.

Hemodynamic Significance of Stenosis

Degree of StenosisClinical Significance
~50% diameter reductionLimits the ability to increase flow to meet increased myocardial demand → exertional angina
~80% diameter reductionBlood flow reduced even at rest; further minor decreases can significantly reduce flow → rest/minimal-exertion angina, signaling an impending event

Acute Coronary Syndrome Mechanism

  • Plaque rupture/erosion exposes contents to blood → platelet activation/aggregation + coagulation cascade activation (fibrin deposition) → thrombus traps RBCs → reduces coronary flow → myocardial ischemia/infarction.

Practice Questions

A patient develops angina with minimal exertion, suggesting an impending cardiac event. What degree of coronary stenosis is most consistent with this presentation?

Around 80% diameter reduction, resting blood flow is already reduced, so further minor decreases can critically reduce flow — producing angina at rest or with minimal exertion, a warning sign of an impending event.

Which of the following is NOT a normal function of the vascular endothelium relevant to atherosclerosis prevention?

A healthy endothelium normally limits (not promotes) inflammatory cell and platelet adhesion. Endothelial damage from risk factors like smoking and hypertension leads to abnormal monocyte-platelet interactions instead.

7. Thoracic Aortic Aneurysms & Dissection

Risk Factors

  • Smoking (strongly associated with formation, expansion, and rupture), hypertension, hypercholesterolemia.
  • Aneurysm at other sites (AAA, cerebral aneurysm), connective tissue disease (e.g., Marfan syndrome), family history of aortic dissection, aortic valve disease.

Symptoms

  • Often asymptomatic, discovered incidentally.
  • Compression/distortion of adjacent structures: heart failure (aortic regurgitation from sinus dilatation), MI/ischemia (coronary compression), dysphagia (esophageal compression), hoarseness (left recurrent laryngeal/vagus nerve compression), hemidiaphragmatic paralysis (phrenic nerve), wheezing/cough/hemoptysis/dyspnea (tracheobronchial compression), SVC syndrome/thromboembolism (central vein compression), acute neurologic complaints (arch branch vessel compression).
  • Thromboembolism (e.g., stroke as first presentation).
  • Dissection: back, chest, arm, or abdominal pain that may migrate; can cause acute MI or CVA depending on location.
  • Rupture: back/chest pain with hypotension or syncope.

Classification of Aortic Dissection

SystemTypeDescription
StanfordType AInvolves the ascending aorta (may progress to arch/thoracoabdominal)

DeBakeyType IAscending aorta, arch, and descending aorta (may progress to abdominal aorta)

Management

  • Cardiovascular risk reduction: smoking cessation, BP control, lipid lowering.
  • Avoid fluoroquinolones — linked to increased risk of aortic dissection and aneurysm rupture.
  • Aneurysm surveillance (20% of TAA patients also have an AAA).
  • Surgical repair (open or endovascular) when indicated.

Practice Questions

A dissection originates in the ascending aorta and extends through the arch into the descending aorta. How is this classified in both the Stanford and DeBakey systems?

Involvement of the ascending aorta makes this a Stanford Type A; since it originates in the ascending aorta and extends through the arch into the descending aorta, it is DeBakey Type I.

Why should fluoroquinolone antibiotics generally be avoided in a patient with a known thoracic aortic aneurysm?

Fluoroquinolones have been associated with an increased risk of aortic dissection and aneurysm rupture and should generally be avoided in patients with known aortic aneurysm.

A patient with a thoracic aortic aneurysm develops hoarseness. What is the most likely mechanism?

Hoarseness in a patient with TAA classically results from compression of the left recurrent laryngeal nerve or left vagus nerve as the aneurysm expands.

8. Abdominal Aortic Aneurysms

Overview

  • Most commonly in the infrarenal aorta, may involve iliac vessels; a true aneurysm (degeneration/dilation of the full aortic wall).
  • Aortic diameter >3 cm = aneurysm; repair typically considered at ≥5.5 cm (deciding point for surgical intervention).
  • Usually due to atherosclerotic disease; also associated with age, male sex, smoking (major risk factor for accelerated growth/rupture), family history, hypertension, diabetes, COPD.
  • Rupture is the most feared complication — mortality ~75–80%.

Presentation

  • Asymptomatic (most common) — found on exam, screening ultrasound, or incidental imaging. Palpation accuracy ranges from 29% (small aneurysms) to 75% (>5 cm).
  • Symptomatic: back/flank/abdominal pain ± tender pulsatile mass; may be hemodynamically stable or unstable. Classic triad (abdominal pain + pulsatile mass + hypotension) seen in only ~1/3 of ruptures.
  • May present with lower limb ischemia from embolization/thrombus.
  • Ecchymosis signs of rupture: flank (Grey-Turner sign), periumbilical (Cullen's sign), proximal thigh (Fox's sign), scrotum (Bryant's sign).

Diagnosis

  • Ultrasound preferred for screening/asymptomatic patients and follow-up (sensitivity/specificity ~95/100%).
  • Hemodynamically unstable + known aneurysm → emergent surgery (bedside US >90% sensitive).
  • Hemodynamically stable symptomatic patients → CT scan (CTA best for anatomic detail).

Treatment

  • Goal: prevent rupture. Risk of rupture: <5 cm = 5–10%; >5 cm = 30–40%. 5.5 cm is the deciding point for surgical intervention.
  • Medical optimization: lipid lowering, smoking cessation, BP/diabetes control; surveillance with serial ultrasound (e.g., every 6 months).
  • Open repair: graft sewn in place — permanently obliterates the aneurysm, but risk of cardiac complications, emboli, and AKI (given the infrarenal location).
  • Endovascular repair (EVAR): percutaneous graft placement — less invasive, decreased operative time/blood loss; risks include endoleak (can cause continued expansion/rupture), graft migration, infection, distal thrombosis.

Practice Questions

A patient with a ruptured AAA has ecchymosis around the umbilicus. What is this sign called?

Periumbilical ecchymosis is Cullen's sign. Flank ecchymosis is Grey-Turner sign, proximal thigh is Fox's sign, and scrotal ecchymosis is Bryant's sign — all associated with retroperitoneal hemorrhage from a ruptured AAA.

At what aortic diameter is elective surgical repair of an AAA generally considered?

While an aortic diameter >3 cm defines an aneurysm, 5.5 cm is the threshold at which the risk of rupture (30–40% for aneurysms >5cm) is felt to outweigh the risks of surgical repair.

A patient with a known AAA presents hemodynamically unstable with severe abdominal pain. What is the most appropriate immediate step?

A hemodynamically unstable patient with a known AAA diagnosis should go emergently to surgery; bedside ultrasound (>90% sensitive) can support the diagnosis without delaying definitive treatment.

9. Mesenteric Ischemia

Overview

  • Occlusion of mesenteric vessels or systemic hypoperfusion; most common cause is arterial embolism.
  • May be acute (embolism) or chronic (partial vascular obstruction); rare in younger patients; median age ~70; mortality 60–80%.
  • With chronic disease, ischemia may not occur unless 2 of the 3 main mesenteric arteries (celiac, SMA, IMA) are occluded, due to rich collateral flow (pancreaticoduodenal arteries, marginal artery of Drummond, hypogastric arteries). In acute disease (embolism), sudden occlusion of just one artery can cause ischemia in its distribution.

Chronic Mesenteric Ischemia

  • "Intestinal angina" — postprandial abdominal pain starting 15–30 min after eating, lasting up to 4 hours; "fear of eating" and weight loss.
  • Nonspecific symptoms: nausea, vomiting, early satiety, diarrhea/constipation.
  • History often includes other atherosclerotic disease (angina, prior TIA/CVA, claudication).
  • Exam: diffuse, mild abdominal tenderness without rebound; occasional abdominal bruit.
  • Diagnosis: CT angiography (preferred noninvasive initial test) + clinical suspicion.

Acute Mesenteric Ischemia

  • Classic finding: pain out of proportion to physical exam findings. May progress to peritonitis/shock.
  • Diagnosis: CT angiography; clinical suspicion is key.
  • Treatment: medical resuscitation while confirming diagnosis and preparing for surgery — control pain, fluid resuscitate (improve splanchnic flow), avoid vasoconstrictors (worsen ischemia), broad-spectrum antibiotics for peritonitis.
  • Surgery: open surgery with bowel resection if bowel is dead; endovascular treatment if viable — embolectomy for embolic disease, bypass graft for thrombotic disease (thrombotic disease may have an underlying severely stenotic vessel, so bypass rather than simple thrombectomy).

Practice Questions

Why does chronic mesenteric ischemia typically require occlusion of at least 2 of the 3 main mesenteric arteries before symptoms develop?

Collateral pathways between the celiac, SMA, and IMA (via the pancreaticoduodenal arteries, marginal artery of Drummond, and hypogastric arteries) allow the bowel to tolerate chronic occlusion of a single mesenteric artery without ischemia.

A patient with acute mesenteric ischemia has severe abdominal pain but a relatively benign abdominal exam. What is this classic finding called, and what should NOT be given during resuscitation?

Pain out of proportion to physical exam findings is the classic presentation of acute mesenteric ischemia. Vasoconstrictors should be avoided during resuscitation because they worsen splanchnic hypoperfusion and ischemia.

A patient with acute mesenteric ischemia due to embolic occlusion of the SMA undergoes surgery. Which procedure is most appropriate for embolic (as opposed to thrombotic) disease?

Embolectomy (removal of the embolus) is appropriate for embolic disease of the SMA. Thrombotic disease usually reflects an underlying severely stenotic vessel and is better treated with a bypass graft rather than simple thrombectomy.

10. Renal Artery Stenosis

  • Atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis is common — affects ~7% of patients >65 years old in sampled populations.
  • Prevalence increases with age and in patients with other vascular disease (CAD, aortic/lower extremity disease).
  • If untreated, progresses in nearly 50% of cases over 5 years, sometimes to total occlusion.
  • Treatment: blood pressure management and statin therapy to slow progression and improve outcomes.

Practice Questions

What is the mainstay of treatment for atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis to slow disease progression?

Blood pressure control and statin therapy are the mainstays of managing atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis, aimed at slowing progression and improving clinical outcomes rather than routine revascularization.

If left untreated, what proportion of atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis cases progress over a 5-year period?

Untreated atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis progresses in almost 50% of cases over 5 years, and can occasionally progress to total occlusion.

11. Peripheral Arterial Disease

Risk Factors

  • Smoking (relative risk ~2.72), hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, age.
  • Ethnicity: higher incidence in African American patients than non-Hispanic white patients.

Chronic Signs & Symptoms

  • Leg pain when walking (claudication); muscle pain/cramping in calves or arms with exertion, relieved by rest; leg numbness/weakness; absent or weak pulses.
  • Skin changes: shiny skin, color changes, slow-growing toenails, non-healing sores, hair loss; erectile dysfunction.

Acute Limb Ischemia — The 6 P's

SignMechanism
PainSudden, severe
PallorPale appearance
ParesthesiasIschemic nerves
ParalysisIschemic nerves/muscles
PulselessNo pulse
PoikilothermiaCold extremity

Evaluation

  • Ankle-Brachial Index (ABI) — first-line test; compares ankle vs. brachial systolic pressure.
    • Healthy: ABI ≥1.00
    • ABI <0.90: may indicate PAD
    • ABI <0.40: severe PAD
  • Labs (cholesterol, triglycerides, glucose/A1c), 6-minute walking test, Doppler ultrasound, CTA, MRA, angiography.

Treatment of Limb Ischemia

  • Restore blood flow and prevent recurrent embolism/thrombosis: heparin (prevents clot extension/reocclusion); direct thrombin inhibitors if heparin-induced thrombocytopenia with thrombosis is a concern.
  • Early surgical/vascular consultation; options include catheter-directed thrombolysis, percutaneous mechanical thromboembolectomy, percutaneous transluminal angioplasty/stenting, or open surgery (bypass grafting).
  • Long-term: smoking cessation, structured exercise, antiplatelet agents, and cilostazol (PDE inhibitor — antiplatelet, vasodilator, inhibits vascular smooth muscle proliferation; contraindicated in heart failure).
Prognostic pearl: In patients with non-limb-threatening ischemia, the 5-year risk of amputation/tissue loss is <5%, but the 5-year risk of death (usually from MI) is ~30% — PAD is as much a marker of systemic atherosclerotic burden as it is a limb problem.

Practice Questions

A patient's ABI is measured at 0.35. What does this indicate?

An ABI below 0.40 indicates severe PAD. An ABI ≥1.00 is healthy, and <0.90 suggests PAD in general, but this level is specifically in the severe range.

Which of the following is NOT one of the classic "6 P's" of acute limb ischemia?

The 6 P's of acute limb ischemia are pain, pallor, paresthesias, paralysis, pulselessness, and poikilothermia. Polyuria is unrelated.

A patient with intermittent claudication asks about their long-term prognosis. What is the most accurate statement?

In patients with non-limb-threatening ischemia, the 5-year risk of amputation or tissue loss is low (<5%), but the 5-year risk of death — usually from myocardial infarction — is substantial (~30%), reflecting PAD as a marker of systemic atherosclerosis.

Overall framework: One disease (endothelial injury → lipid/inflammatory plaque → erosion or rupture → thrombosis or embolism), many faces. Systemic risk factors (smoking, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia) drive plaque formation preferentially at branch points/turbulent flow throughout the arterial tree, and management — risk factor modification, antiplatelet/statin therapy, and targeted revascularization when critical thresholds are crossed — follows the same basic logic regardless of the vascular bed involved.
