00 · Overview & Objectives

Framing the Lecture

Three pathologies, same skeleton: pathophysiology → presentation → treatment

Treatment is kept light in this lecture — pharmacology gets its own dedicated lecture — but boards love testing side effects and indications of the drugs mentioned here.

Big framing pearlSjögren's and RA are mediated by inflammation (autoimmune). Osteoarthritis is wear-and-tear — non-inflammatory joint destruction. This single distinction resolves most "which arthritis is this" board questions.
Practice Question
Which of the three arthritides discussed is fundamentally driven by mechanical wear rather than immune-mediated inflammation?

01 · Sjögren's Syndrome — Epidemiology & Sicca Symptoms

Epidemiology

Middle-aged women, insidious course, frequently co-occurs with other rheumatologic disease
  • Female:male ≈ 9:1 — think women far more than men
  • Onset typically age 40–60; rare in pediatrics (only ~1.3% of cases diagnosed under age 18)
  • Affects ~1% of the general population — probably an underestimate, since the disease is insidious and underdiagnosed
  • Very commonly co-presents with other rheumatologic disease (RA, scleroderma, etc.) — always dual-test the antibody panels together in practice

Symptom Onset Pattern

Symptoms are slow, progressive, insidious — not an acute "started 6 weeks ago" story. Patients accumulate dry eyes/dry mouth over years before presenting.

Core Sicca Symptoms

  • Dry eyes (gritty sensation) and dry mouth (cottonmouth), ongoing throughout the day, unrelieved by compensation
  • Patients self-treat early on: artificial tears, sucking on lemon drops/hard candies to stimulate salivary flow
  • As disease advances, compensation fails → patients present saying "artificial tears aren't cutting it anymore"
  • Parotitis (swollen, inflamed parotid gland) can occur, but is a later finding, not typical initial presentation
  • Other exocrine mucosal surfaces can dry out too: skin, bronchi, vagina, armpits
DefinitionSicca syndrome = dry eyes + dry mouth alone, without other systemic involvement — this is what most Sjögren's patients actually present with.

02 · Sjögren's Syndrome — Pathophysiology

What Is Sjögren's?

An autoimmune disorder of the exocrine glands

Exocrine glands secrete substances out of the body (tears, saliva, mucus, sweat), as opposed to endocrine glands, which secrete hormones internally. The pancreas is technically exocrine too (secretes into the GI lumen) — pancreatic insufficiency from Sjögren's is controversial/not well established in the literature.

Mechanism — Step by Step

  1. Lymphocytic infiltration of exocrine glands — starts with helper T cells invading the gland and misrecognizing self-tissue as antigen
  2. T cells recruit B cells → autoantibody production + IL-2 release
  3. Glandular cells express MHC as antigen-presenting cells → T cells, B cells, and antibodies attack the MHC molecule as if foreign
  4. This triggers cell apoptosis, destroying exocrine glandular cells → replaced by fibrous scar tissue
  5. Metalloproteases are secreted, gumming up the extracellular matrix and further impairing remaining healthy cells
  6. Disease is T-cell dominant early, shifts to B-cell dominant as it progresses
  7. Anti-acetylcholine receptor antibodies form, blocking vagal nerve stimulation of mucin-secreting cells — so remaining glandular cells are also functionally inhibited, not just destroyed

Histology

CD3 (T cell) and CD20 (B cell) staining show dense lymphocytic infiltration surrounding salivary gland lumens. B-cell infiltration increases as disease progresses.

Genetics & Trigger Theory

  • 87% of patients are HLA-DR52 positive — genetic susceptibility alone is not sufficient (epigenetics)
  • Leading theory: a viral trigger — the immune response mounted against a virus cross-reacts with self-antigens (molecular mimicry), especially in genetically susceptible individuals
Timeline pearlEarly Sjögren's = T-cell mediated. Late/progressive Sjögren's = B-cell dominant. This shift underlies why later-stage disease responds to B-cell-targeted therapy (rituximab).

03 · Sjögren's Syndrome — Diagnosis & Testing

Diagnostic Criteria

4 of 6, and one of the 4 must be biopsy or antibody positivity
  1. Dry eyes
  2. Dry mouth
  3. Swollen salivary glands
  4. Positive Schirmer test
  5. Abnormal oral/sialography findings
  6. Positive biopsy or antibodies (anti-Ro/SSA, anti-La/SSB)
MemorizeAnti-Ro (SSA) and anti-La (SSB) antibodies — think of them like Chapman's points: on boards, they'll give you a positive antibody and expect you to know the associated disease instantly.

Specific Tests

TestWhat It Shows
Salivary gland biopsyT-cell/B-cell lymphocytic infiltration
Schirmer testTissue paper hung on lower eyelid, patient closes eye for 5 min; normal = tears diffuse ≥10mm down the paper
Lissamine green (or fluorescein) stainingStains areas of corneal/scleral breakdown from chronic dryness
SialogramRadiology test with contrast dye to visualize salivary gland secretion; abnormal = poor dye flow

04 · Sjögren's Syndrome — Ocular Complications, Glands Affected, Primary vs. Secondary

Why Dry Eyes Progress to Real Damage

  1. Glandular destruction → less tear volume, but the same amount of salt still gets secreted → tears become hyperosmolar
  2. Hyperosmolarity triggers an inflammatory cascade and immune activation in the conjunctiva
  3. This breaks down normal ocular surface architecture and normal flora
  4. Result: risk of permanent vision loss and selection for multidrug-resistant pathogens

Glands That Can Be Affected (Beyond Eyes/Mouth)

  • Vaginal dryness (women) and urethral/prostatic dryness (men)
  • GI tract glands (controversial) and bronchopulmonary glands

Primary vs. Secondary Sjögren's

TypeDefinition
PrimaryOccurs by itself
SecondaryOccurs alongside another connective tissue disease — classically RA, SLE, scleroderma. More common than primary; usually the other autoimmune disease is diagnosed first.

Reasons Sjögren's Is Underdiagnosed

  • Head and neck irradiation
  • Hepatitis C infection
  • HIV/AIDS
  • Sarcoidosis, graft-versus-host disease, IgG4-related disease
  • Anticholinergic medications — need to stop the drug and reassess symptoms before diagnosing Sjögren's

06 · Sjögren's Syndrome — Systemic Manifestations

Mucous Membrane / GI

  • Upper respiratory tract dryness → recurrent infection, hoarseness
  • Vaginal/urethral/prostate dryness; anorectal dryness → chronic itching, anal fissures
  • Dysphagia from esophageal dryness — patients need water to help swallow food
  • Controversial: acute/chronic pancreatitis, delayed gastric emptying

Renal

  • Interstitial nephritis and renal tubular acidosis — up to 10% of patients
  • Glomerulonephritis from immune complex deposition
Two Types of RTA
TypeLocationFindings
Distal RTADistal tubuleLow K⁺, high urine pH, low serum bicarb → acidosis
Fanconi syndrome (proximal RTA)Proximal tubuleAmino acids + bicarb wasted in urine, low serum bicarb → acidosis

Can also develop nephrogenic diabetes insipidus. Increased risk of renal calculi and interstitial cystitis.

Neurologic

  • Peripheral neuropathy (immune complex deposition — also causes purpura)
  • Myelitis, cognitive dysfunction, cranial neuropathies, neuromyelitis optica

Pulmonary

  • Cough, bronchiolitis, obstructive pattern on spirometry
  • Interstitial lung disease → restrictive pattern on spirometry
  • Anti-SSA positivity → increased risk of ILD, independent of Sjögren's diagnosis itself

Hepatic

Cardiac

  • Pericarditis, pulmonary hypertension
  • Autonomic dysfunction (M3 muscarinic receptor antibody) → orthostatic hypotension

Skin

  • Purpura — cutaneous vasculitis
  • Raynaud's phenomenon
  • Hair drying/loss → alopecia in severe cases
  • Skin dryness, pruritus

Other

  • Fatigue
  • Arthritis in 42% — non-erosive, mild

07 · Sjögren's Syndrome — Treatment & Complications

Treating Sicca Symptoms

TargetTreatment
GeneralStay well hydrated
EyesArtificial tears; topical cyclosporine; punctal plugs
MouthArtificial saliva; sugar-free lozenges; pilocarpine and cevimeline
Arthritis (non-erosive)NSAIDs as-needed; topical NSAIDs; DMARDs (hydroxychloroquine, methotrexate); low-dose corticosteroids for acute flares
FatigueExercise is the only effective treatment
Do notDo NOT use TNF-alpha inhibitors to treat sicca symptoms — those target systemic/organ-involvement manifestations, not sicca syndrome itself.

Escalation to Biologics

  • If arthritis fails methotrexate/hydroxychloroquine → rituximab
  • Rituximab also used for: severe parotid gland swelling, immune complex-mediated disease, pulmonary disease, severe peripheral neuropathy

Major Complications

ComplicationDetail
Lymphoma4.3% of patients — most commonly MALT lymphoma
Parotid infections / tumorsChronic inflammation → increased risk of tumor formation
Antiphospholipid antibody syndromeCauses first-trimester miscarriages; neonatal lupus with congenital heart block

Prognosis

  • Most patients don't progress much beyond sicca syndrome
  • Overall mortality similar to general population; lymphomas tend to be "friendlier" cancers
  • Poor prognostic indicators: complement issues, cryoglobulins, severe immune cell dysfunction

08 · Rheumatoid Arthritis — Overview, Epidemiology, Hand Findings

Overview

RA carries far more morbidity than Sjögren's and is much more heavily tested on boards

Expect picture questions on joint deformities and X-rays.

Classic Hand Deformities

  • Swan neck deformity of the DIPs
  • Ulnar deviation — fingers deviate toward the ulnar side while the wrist often stays straight
  • X-ray: classic finding is joint erosion

Definition

Symmetric inflammatory polyarthritis, typically involving MCPs, PIPs, and wrist joints.

Key Distinguishing Feature: Morning Stiffness
RAOA
Duration>60 minutes<60 minutes, improves over the day

Epidemiology

  • Affects ~1% of population
  • Female:male ≈ 2–3:1
  • Men tend to have more severe disease when affected
  • Peak incidence: age 50–60

Distinguishing RA From "Just Joint Pain"

  • Abrupt onset (weeks) of joint stiffness/pain/swelling lasting >6 weeks
  • Symmetric joint swelling with true joint effusion
  • Morning stiffness >1 hour, improves with use
  • <4 weeks should raise suspicion for a viral cause (e.g., parvovirus B19)

09 · Rheumatoid Arthritis — Diagnostic Criteria & Pathogenesis

ACR/EULAR Diagnostic Criteria (2010, Still Used)

  • Joint involvement (more points for small joints than large joints)
  • Serology (anti-RF or anti-CCP)
  • Symptom duration
  • Elevated acute phase reactants (ESR/CRP)
Not perfectThe criteria underdiagnose early disease — patients with few involved joints and low-positive serologies may not meet the point threshold, but should still be followed closely for RA development.

Pathogenesis — Step by Step

  1. External trigger (viral infection theory) → increased inflammation → autoimmune reaction
  2. → Synovial hypertrophy in genetically susceptible individuals (HLA-DRB1)
  3. Antibodies: rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP
  4. Risk factors: smoking is the biggest one; occupational exposures; gingivitis (via Porphyromonas gingivalis toxin)

Emerging Marker: Anti-CarP Antibody

Can become positive in the pre-symptomatic stage and predicts more severe disease.

Joint Destruction Mechanism

  1. Synovial cell hyperplasia + endothelial cell activation
  2. Immune cell infiltration → tissue/cell death, inflammation, joint effusions
  3. Cytokine cascade alters osteoclast/osteoblast signaling → erosive joint disease
  4. Synovium proliferates and destroys cartilage, bone, tendons, ligaments, blood vessels

Broader Differential for "Hot Swollen Painful Joint"

OA, polyarticular gout, psoriatic arthritis, other connective tissue diseases, neoplastic syndromes, and immune checkpoint inhibitor side effects.

10 · Rheumatoid Arthritis — Physical Exam

Physical Exam

  • Multiple swollen, symmetric joints
  • Joints often not very tender to direct palpation of the effusion, but ARE tender with joint articulation
  • Synovitis: palpable soft, spongy tissue along the joint space
Board buzzwords"Synovitis of the joints" or "tissue hypertrophy along the joint space with a soft, spongy feel" both point toward RA.

11 · Rheumatoid Arthritis — Skin Findings

Rheumatoid Nodules

  • Associated with rheumatoid factor and immune complex deposition
  • No bone inside — soft tissue/fibrous tissue
  • More likely with anti-CCP positivity and associated vasculitis
  • Classically on hands, but can occur in the lungs

Pyoderma Gangrenosum

  • Frequently misdiagnosed as arterial/venous stasis ulcers
  • Starts as a small pustule, progresses to an ulcerative lesion
  • Not infectious, not truly "gangrenous"
  • Associated with: RA, Crohn's, ulcerative colitis, IgA-mediated multiple myeloma
  • Treatment: combination prednisone + cyclosporine beats either alone (only 40–50% resolution)

Vasculitis-Related Skin Findings

Livedo reticularisLacy pattern; pathologic/permanent pattern suggests immune complex vasculitis

14 · Rheumatoid Arthritis — Ocular Involvement & Felty Syndrome

Ocular

  • RA can cause sicca-like dry eyes/dry mouth independent of a co-diagnosis of Sjögren's, usually milder
  • Scleral malacia — degenerative thinning of the sclera, very specific to RA
  • Refer to ophthalmology for scleral irritation/redness

Felty Syndrome

Classic triad: RA + neutropenia + splenomegaly. Mnemonic: SANTA — splenomegaly, anemia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, arthritis.

Clinical pearlIf an RA patient's screening labs show neutropenia + thrombocytopenia, examine for splenomegaly.

15 · Rheumatoid Arthritis — Serology & Imaging

Rheumatoid Factor (RF)

  • Not specific to RA — also seen in Sjögren's, SBE, leprosy, ILD, primary biliary cirrhosis
  • Specificity ~90% for any rheumatologic condition

Anti-CCP

  • Specificity ~99% for RA
  • Present in 60–70% of RA patients
  • Positivity predicts increased risk of major joint damage
  • Can be positive well before clinical RA develops

17 · Osteoarthritis — Overview & Primary vs. Secondary

Definition

Joint space destruction disease — loss of cartilage, bony overgrowth, resulting pain.

Epidemiology

  • Most common type of joint disease — over 30 million patients in the US
  • Leading cause of chronic disability in the US
  • Effusions are non-inflammatory and less common than in autoimmune arthritis
Primary vs. Secondary

SecondaryDue to an underlying disorder that damages the joint

18 · Osteoarthritis — Pathophysiology & Staging

Overall Mechanism

Chondrocytes don't function properly → cartilage thins → subchondral bone thickening/sclerosis → bone remodeling with osteophyte formation → reactive, mild synovitis.

Stages
StageChanges
Stage 1Early synovium/cartilage changes; usually asymptomatic
Stage 2Imperfect proteoglycan repair, cartilage swelling, mild synovial hypertrophy
Stage 3Proteoglycans lost — cartilage softens and breaks down (clefts on histology); joint space narrows, osteophytes calcify

Resulting Findings

  • Osteoarthritic nodules — contain bone
  • Subchondral cysts — chronic impaction allows synovial fluid into bone

Patellar Osteoarthritis

  • Usually history of patellar trauma
  • Diagnostic maneuver: hand over patella, extend the leg — crepitus + pain = essentially diagnostic

Hand Osteoarthritis

  • Heberden's nodes (DIP) and Bouchard's nodes (PIP) — more common in postmenopausal women
  • Also common at the first CMC joint

Erosive Osteoarthritis

  • Subset of hand OA, up to 5–15% of patients
  • More acute onset pain and increased inflammation than typical OA
  • X-ray shows true erosions (classic "sawtooth" pattern)
  • Diagnosis: negative antibodies (RF, CCP), normal ESR/CRP, OA in ≥2 joints with at least one being a DIP

20 · Osteoarthritis — Spine Involvement & DISH

Spondylosis

Age-related spine degeneration — usually asymptomatic.

Spondylolisthesis

Vertebral body slippage — most commonly at L4-L5.

Spinal Stenosis

  • Common, especially in elderly women
  • Central canal narrowing (more severe) vs. facet joint narrowing
  • Neurogenic claudication: shooting leg pain relieved by flexing forward
  • Treatment: epidural steroid injections or neurosurgery

DISH (Diffuse Idiopathic Skeletal Hyperostosis / Forestier Disease)

  • Osteophytes across ≥4 contiguous vertebrae, ossification of the anterior longitudinal ligament
  • Joint and disc space preserved
  • No systemic inflammation — normal CRP, no antibodies
  • Classic imaging: "flowing candle wax" pattern
  • Male > female predominance
  • Often asymptomatic; can develop dysphagia
  • Associations: type 2 diabetes, hyperinsulinemia, hyperuricemia
DISH vs. ankylosing spondylitisDISH osteophytes do NOT bridge (paper-slide test positive). AS syndesmophytes bridge and fuse.

Topical Capsaicin — Mechanism Deep Dive

Binds TRPV1 receptor, keeps it open continuously → constant nerve depolarization → nerve becomes numb to that pain pathway. Must be used continuously.

Big takeawayThink about what you might be hurting your patient with — opiate dependence, NSAID GI bleed — this is why topical therapies and acetaminophen are preferred first-line.

Sjögren's Syndrome

CategoryHighest-yield buzzword / distinguishing feature
Epidemiology9:1 female, age 40–60, ~1% of population (likely underdiagnosed); frequently secondary to RA/SLE/scleroderma
PresentationSicca syndrome (dry eyes + dry mouth) — insidious, years-long course; parotitis is a later finding
AntibodiesAnti-Ro (SSA) and anti-La (SSB) — memorize cold; 87% HLA-DR52 positive
Diagnosis4 of 6 criteria, must include biopsy or antibody positivity; Schirmer test, lissamine green, sialogram
Feared complicationsMALT lymphoma (4.3%); antiphospholipid syndrome → neonatal lupus with congenital heart block
TreatmentHydration, artificial tears/saliva, pilocarpine/cevimeline; never TNF-alpha inhibitors for sicca alone; rituximab for refractory/severe disease

Rheumatoid Arthritis

CategoryHighest-yield buzzword / distinguishing feature
Epidemiology2–3:1 female, peak age 50–60; men have more severe disease when affected
PresentationSymmetric inflammatory polyarthritis; morning stiffness >60 min improving with use; ulnar deviation, swan neck deformity
AntibodiesRF (~90% specific for "any rheum. disease," not RA-specific); anti-CCP (~99% specific for RA, predicts joint damage)
Systemic complicationsAtlantoaxial instability (C1-C2, contraindicates cervical HVLA); Caplan syndrome (RA + pneumoconiosis); Felty syndrome (RA + neutropenia + splenomegaly); scleral malacia; ILD
ImagingProgressive erosion — soft tissue swelling → cortical thinning → scalloped erosions + joint space narrowing
TreatmentNever NSAIDs alone — every patient needs a DMARD (methotrexate + folic acid); DAS28 to monitor response

Osteoarthritis

CategoryHighest-yield buzzword / distinguishing feature
EpidemiologyMost common joint disease, >30 million in the US, leading cause of chronic disability
Primary vs. secondaryPrimary = age-related wear (hands, knees, hips); secondary = anything that damages the joint (trauma, malalignment, crystal disease, endocrine/metabolic)
PresentationInsidious, morning stiffness <30 min improving quickly, crepitus, cold/damp sensitivity, non-inflammatory effusion if present
Special formsErosive OA (sawtooth erosions, negative serologies); DISH (flowing non-bridging osteophytes ≥4 levels, no inflammation, associated with T2DM); spinal stenosis (neurogenic claudication relieved by flexion)
Synovial fluidNon-inflammatory (low WBC) — contrast with inflammatory (<50–100K, neutrophilic) and septic (>80K, 80–90% PMNs)
TreatmentTopical NSAIDs first-line for isolated joints; acetaminophen mainstay; avoid opiates (dependence cycle); capsaicin via TRPV1 desensitization

The Classic Confusable Pairs — Quick Differentiation

Pairs Board Questions Love to Contrast
PairHow to tell them apart
Rheumatoid nodule vs. osteoarthritic noduleRheumatoid = soft/fibrous, no bone. OA (Heberden's/Bouchard's) = bony, osteophyte-based.
RF vs. anti-CCPRF = sensitive but nonspecific (~90% specific for "any rheum. disease"). Anti-CCP = 99% specific for RA, better predictor of joint damage.
Erosive OA vs. RAErosive OA: negative serologies, normal ESR/CRP, DIP-predominant sawtooth erosions. RA: positive serologies, elevated markers, symmetric small-joint polyarthritis with true effusion.
DISH vs. ankylosing spondylitisDISH osteophytes don't bridge (paper-slide test positive), no inflammation, disc space preserved. AS syndesmophytes bridge/fuse, elevated inflammatory markers, HLA-B27 association.
Primary biliary cholangitis vs. autoimmune hepatitisPBC = cholestatic (↑ bilirubin), anti-mitochondrial antibody. AIH = hepatocellular (↑AST/ALT), anti-smooth muscle antibody/ANA.
Sjögren's sicca arthritis vs. RASjögren's arthritis (in 42% of patients) is mild and non-erosive; RA is erosive, symmetric, and the dominant joint pathology rather than a secondary feature.

Lecturer's Own Wrap-Up

  • Sjögren's is likely underdiagnosed — most patients only have sicca syndrome. Know anti-Ro/anti-La cold. Remember it can progress to severe systemic disease and increases lymphoma risk.
  • RA = classic inflammatory polyarthritis. Know RF vs. anti-CCP well. The single biggest clinical priority is an accurate, timely diagnosis so patients start a DMARD. Every RA patient needs a DMARD like methotrexate, and every methotrexate patient needs folic acid.
  • Osteoarthritis = primary vs. secondary framework; it's a progressive joint degeneration disease and, because of its sheer prevalence, treatment safety is paramount.
Overarching test-taking frameworkWhen you see a joint complaint, first sort inflammatory vs. non-inflammatory using onset speed, morning stiffness duration, symmetry, and effusion character — then use serology/antibodies to pin down the specific diagnosis.
